# 2. Don't die slowly

This chapter takes only two kinds of chronic risk: those that matter a lot for all-cause mortality, and those with solid evidence. Entries run from highest to lowest value for money. A few are labeled "randomized trial", meaning people were randomly split into two groups and compared - those results are the most trustworthy. Every other number comes from studies that only follow and record, without randomization. In those studies, risk ratios like HR and RR carry two impurities: the people differed to begin with, and some started doing the thing only after getting sick. So read those numbers as "direction and rough size", not as "a discount you collect by following along".

### 1. Quit smoking, the earlier the better
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=yes benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Free - it actually saves money. A pack a day runs about 20-30 yuan; quit, and that money stays with you. The hard part is enduring withdrawal, usually weeks to months.
- TL;DR: Smokers live more than ten years less than non-smokers on average. Quitting before 40 erases about 90% of the added death risk from continuing to smoke. The earlier you quit, the more you get back.
- Benefit: An American cohort study (record-only, no randomization) found that current smokers have a life expectancy more than 10 years shorter than never-smokers. Quitting before 40 eliminates about 90% of the added mortality risk from continued smoking. Quitting at 25-34 buys about 10 extra years of life, at 35-44 about 9 years, at 45-54 about 6 years (reported as years of life in the original). The Chinese counterpart study: in the 2010s, urban male smokers had 1.65 times the mortality risk of non-smokers (RR 1.65), rural males 1.22 times. After 10 full years of not smoking, the smoking-attributable share of risk nearly disappears.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Jha P et al. (2013). 21st-century hazards of smoking and benefits of cessation in the United States. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMsa1211128> ; Chen Z et al. (2015). Contrasting male and female trends in tobacco-attributed mortality in China: evidence from successive nationwide prospective cohort studies. Lancet. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(15)00340-2> ; Oberg M et al. (2011). Worldwide burden of disease from exposure to second-hand smoke: a retrospective analysis of data from 192 countries. Lancet. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(10)61388-8> (the two secondhand-smoke figures)
- Notes: Breathing other people's smoke kills too. In 2004 about 603 thousand people worldwide died from secondhand smoke - about 1% of all deaths that year. Even if you don't smoke, stay out of other people's smoke. Above all, keep it away from children. This entry only covers how much quitting buys you, not how to quit. The concrete methods are the next four entries: entry 3 (quit-smoking medication), entry 4 (set a quit date), entry 5 (go to a smoking-cessation clinic), entry 6 (e-cigarettes)

### 2. Don't smoke at home or in the car, and don't let guests smoke in your home either
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: No money, no time. The hard part is opening your mouth - telling family and guests not to smoke inside.
- TL;DR: About 600 thousand people a year worldwide die from breathing other people's smoke, nearly three in ten of them children. Long-term secondhand-smoke exposure raises the risk of hypertension, heart disease and stroke by roughly 30% each. Smoke breathed at home hurts more than smoke breathed elsewhere.
- Benefit: In 2004, 603,000 people died from secondhand smoke worldwide, about 1.0% of global deaths, 28% of them children. Pooling 57 studies: people exposed to secondhand smoke had about 1.28 times the odds of hypertension (odds ratio) compared with the unexposed. Heart disease 1.39 times, myocardial infarction 1.50, stroke 1.36. The risk from secondhand smoke at home is higher than from exposure outside the home
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Öberg M, Jaakkola MS, Woodward A, Peruga A, Prüss-Ustün A (2011). Worldwide burden of disease from exposure to second-hand smoke: a retrospective analysis of data from 192 countries. Lancet: "603,000 deaths were attributable to second-hand smoke in 2004, which was about 1·0% of worldwide mortality. 47% of deaths from second-hand smoke occurred in women, 28% in children, and 26% in men", "61% of DALYs were in children". <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(10)61388-8> ; (2026). The Associations Between Secondhand Smoke Exposure and Various Cardiovascular Diseases: A Meta-Analysis. Nicotine & Tobacco Research: 57 studies, "hypertension (OR: 1.28, 95% CI: 1.15 to 1.40), heart disease (OR: 1.39, 95% CI: 1.28 to 1.50), myocardial infarction (OR: 1.50, 95% CI: 1.17 to 1.84), stroke (OR: 1.36, 95% CI: 1.18 to 1.54)" "Home exposure has a higher risk of CVD than non-home exposure". <https://doi.org/10.1093/ntr/ntaf111>
- Notes: Start with your home. For one, you spend the most time there; for another, pooled studies show home exposure does more harm than exposure elsewhere. Of the health loss caused by secondhand smoke, 61% lands on children. For quitting yourself, see entry 1 of this chapter.

### 3. Don't quit on willpower alone - get the medication first: success rates more than double
<!-- tags: money=low time=low will=some benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Nicotine patches and gum are over-the-counter - any pharmacy counter has them. One course runs 8 to 12 weeks, a few hundred to over a thousand yuan. Varenicline and bupropion are prescription drugs, from a smoking-cessation clinic or a respiratory-medicine department. The cost roughly cancels out against the cigarettes you stop buying over the same period.
- TL;DR: White-knuckling it, most people fail. People taking varenicline quit successfully at more than twice the rate of those on placebo. Nicotine replacements like patches and gum beat no medication by about half. Patch plus gum together adds another 20-plus percent.
- Benefit: Pooling 41 randomized trials with 17,395 people, the varenicline group's quit success rate was 2.32 times the placebo group's (RR 2.32, 95% CI 2.15-2.51 - that's the plausible range; high-certainty evidence). Varenicline also beat bupropion (RR 1.36, about 36% higher) and a single nicotine replacement (RR 1.25, about 25% higher). Nicotine replacement versus no medication: 133 trials, 64,640 people, about 55% higher success (RR 1.55, 95% CI 1.49-1.61). A patch plus a fast-acting form (gum, lozenge) beats one form alone by about 27% (RR 1.27, 95% CI 1.17-1.37, 16 trials, 12,169 people, high-certainty evidence).
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Livingstone-Banks J, Fanshawe TR, Thomas KH, et al. (2023). Nicotine receptor partial agonists for smoking cessation. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 5, CD006103. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD006103.pub8> ; Hartmann-Boyce J, Chepkin SC, Ye W, Bullen C, Lancaster T (2018). Nicotine replacement therapy versus control for smoking cessation. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 5, CD000146. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD000146.pub5> ; Theodoulou A, Chepkin SC, Ye W, et al. (2023). Different doses, durations and modes of delivery of nicotine replacement therapy for smoking cessation. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 6, CD013308. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD013308.pub2> ; Shanghai Municipal Health Commission (2021). Choosing the right medication makes quitting easier: "The quit-smoking medications on the market fall into three main kinds: nicotine replacement therapy drugs, bupropion, and varenicline", "Nicotine replacement therapy drugs are over-the-counter (OTC) and can be bought at pharmacy counters; bupropion and varenicline are prescription drugs, obtained only with a doctor's prescription dispensed by a pharmacist after a visit to a hospital smoking-cessation clinic or respiratory-medicine department". <https://wsjkw.sh.gov.cn/jtyx/20211119/df50681e01ba49f896d54f771d8176ae.html>
- Notes: These three kinds are the only quit-smoking drugs sold in China, and only nicotine replacements are over-the-counter. Varenicline commonly causes nausea, vivid dreams and poor sleep; anyone with a psychiatric history should tell the doctor. A nicotine-replacement course usually runs 8 to 12 weeks - don't stop abruptly on your own; taper as the doctor says. Medication only gets you through the rough withdrawal weeks; it doesn't handle the "I want one" situations, so pair it with entry 4 of this chapter (set a quit date) and entry 5 (go to a smoking-cessation clinic).

### 4. Set a quit date and stop all at once that day - don't taper down first
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=yes benefit=mid lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Free. Just pick a day on the calendar and tell your family and coworkers ahead of time.
- TL;DR: Many people plan to smoke fewer each day and taper to zero. A 697-person randomized trial compared the two approaches head to head. By month six, 22% of those who quit all at once on a set day were still not smoking, versus 15.5% of the taperers. People who had preferred tapering still did better when assigned to quit abruptly.
- Benefit: 697 British smokers were randomly split into two groups. One stopped all at once on their quit date; the other cut their cigarette count by 75% over the two weeks before it. Both groups got behavioral support from nurses and used nicotine replacement before and after the quit date. At week 4, 49.0% of the abrupt group (95% CI 43.8-54.2, the plausible range) had not relapsed, versus 39.2% (34.0-44.4) of the taper group - relative risk 0.80 (0.66-0.93). By month 6, 22.0% (18.0-26.6) of the abrupt group versus 15.5% (12.0-19.7) of the taper group, relative risk 0.71 (0.46-0.91). People who had originally preferred tapering also succeeded less at week 4 than those who preferred quitting abruptly (38.3% versus 52.2%).
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Lindson-Hawley N, Banting M, West R, Michie S, Shinkins B, Aveyard P (2016). Gradual Versus Abrupt Smoking Cessation: A Randomized, Controlled Noninferiority Trial. Annals of Internal Medicine, 164(9), 585-592. <https://doi.org/10.7326/M14-2805> ; Theodoulou A, Chepkin SC, Ye W, et al. (2023). Different doses, durations and modes of delivery of nicotine replacement therapy for smoking cessation. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 6, CD013308 (the part on starting nicotine replacement early). <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD013308.pub2>
- Notes: Disputed. Cochrane once had a review dedicated to comparing the two approaches, concluding they were about equal (RR 0.94, 95% CI 0.79-1.13, 10 trials, 3,760 people), but that review was withdrawn in 2019 and is no longer updated. So the best current evidence is the randomized trial above, which favors quitting abruptly. Also, in that trial both groups started nicotine replacement before the quit date - a practice with its own evidence: starting early beats starting on the quit day itself by about 25% (RR 1.25, 95% CI 1.08-1.44, 9 trials, 4,395 people, moderate-certainty evidence). So the sensible sequence is: pick a day, start the patch two weeks ahead, stop all at once on the day.

### 5. Go to a smoking-cessation clinic, or call 12320 to ask what quit services your area has
<!-- tags: money=low time=mid will=some benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: A registration fee of a few yuan to a few dozen yuan per visit. Behavioral support usually means 4 to 8 sessions of half an hour to an hour each, totaling under 5 hours in most cases. Calling the hotline is free.
- TL;DR: Medication and "someone checking in on you regularly" are two separate things - together they work best. People on medication with dedicated follow-up quit at nearly twice the rate of those who only hear one brief piece of advice. Phone calls count too: a quitline that calls you back proactively raises success another 20-30%.
- Benefit: Pooling 52 trials with 19,488 people, the medication-plus-behavioral-support group quit at 1.83 times the rate of usual care or brief advice (RR 1.83, 95% CI 1.68-1.98 - the plausible range; high-quality evidence). In the 43 trials that recruited in medical institutions it was 1.97 times (1.79-2.18). Telephone counseling on its own: among people who had called a quitline themselves, those receiving multiple proactive callback sessions did about 38% better than those who only got materials or a single call (RR 1.38, 95% CI 1.19-1.61, 14 trials, 32,484 people); among people who hadn't called a quitline and were offered telephone counseling by the researchers, about 25% better (RR 1.25, 95% CI 1.15-1.35, 65 trials, 41,233 people). Both are moderate-certainty evidence.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Stead LF, Koilpillai P, Fanshawe TR, Lancaster T (2016). Combined pharmacotherapy and behavioural interventions for smoking cessation. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 3, CD008286. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD008286.pub3> ; Matkin W, Ordóñez-Mena JM, Hartmann-Boyce J (2019). Telephone counselling for smoking cessation. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 5, CD002850. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD002850.pub4> ; Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention (2021). The "China Quit Platform" WeChat mini-program officially launches: "Under the guidance of the Healthy China Action tobacco-control working group, the Chinese CDC and the WHO China office jointly produced 'China Quit Platform', an authoritative WeChat mini-program library of quit-smoking resources. Its content mainly includes quitlines, detailed smoking-cessation clinic listings, and online quit services"; the same site's footer lists "Health consultation hotline: 12320". <https://www.chinacdc.cn/jkyj/yckz/gzdt/202203/t20220310_296389.html>
- Notes: How to find one in China: search WeChat for the "China Quit Platform" mini-program, which lists quitlines and cessation clinics around the country; or call 12320 to ask whether your area has a cessation clinic. Most of the trials above were run abroad, and the intensity of clinic services varies a lot by region - treat the numbers as directional only. For the medication to pair with this, see entry 3 of this chapter (quit-smoking drugs).

### 6. Consider e-cigarettes only if you can't quit any other way - non-smokers should never touch them
<!-- tags: money=low time=low will=some benefit=mid lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Device plus pods run tens to hundreds of yuan a month. In China only tobacco-flavored ones may be sold, and only in shops holding a tobacco monopoly retail license.
- TL;DR: Switching cigarettes for nicotine e-cigarettes does beat nicotine patches for quitting - there's a high-quality randomized trial on that. But they aren't harmless: long-term safety data doesn't exist yet. For someone who doesn't smoke, they bring zero benefit.
- Benefit: A continuously updated review covered 80 randomized trials with 29,861 people. Nicotine e-cigarettes versus nicotine replacement therapy: quit success about 61% higher (RR 1.61, 95% CI 1.23-2.12 - the plausible range; high-certainty evidence, 11 trials, 4,114 people). In absolute terms, roughly 4 more quitters per 100 people (1 to 7 more). The rates of serious adverse events were about the same in both groups (risk difference 0.01, 95% CI -0.01 to 0.02, moderate-certainty evidence). Versus behavioral support alone or no support: about 75% higher (RR 1.75, 95% CI 1.39-2.20, low-certainty evidence).
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Lindson N, Livingstone-Banks J, Butler AR, et al. (2026). Electronic cigarettes for smoking cessation. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 8, CD010216. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD010216.pub11> ; State Tobacco Monopoly Administration (2022). Administrative Measures for E-Cigarettes (STMA Announcement No. 1 of 2022, effective May 1, 2022), Article 18: engaging in e-cigarette retail requires applying to the tobacco monopoly authority for a tobacco monopoly retail license. Article 22: "The sale of e-cigarette products to minors is prohibited." Article 23, paragraph 2: "No individual, legal person or other organization may sell e-cigarette products, aerosol substances or e-cigarette nicotine through information networks other than the e-cigarette transaction management platform specified in these Measures." Article 26: "The sale of flavored e-cigarettes other than tobacco flavor, and of e-cigarettes to which users can add their own aerosol substances, is prohibited.". <http://www.gov.cn/gongbao/content/2022/content_5697988.htm>
- Notes: Disputed. The World Health Organization does not recommend e-cigarettes as a quit method, citing insufficient long-term safety data and the risk of initiating young people into nicotine. The UK, by contrast, has folded them into its quit services. China hasn't banned e-cigarettes outright but regulates them strictly - the rules are in the Administrative Measures for E-Cigarettes. Only tobacco-flavored products may be sold; fruit flavors and refillable devices are banned. They may be sold only in physical shops holding a tobacco monopoly retail license. Selling them on any website, online store or social app outside the national e-cigarette transaction platform is illegal. Selling to minors is banned. So the fruit-flavored pods still circulating come through illegal channels, outside regulation - that's how pods laced with synthetic cannabinoids, the so-called "high pods", get around; see entry 4 of chapter 22 (don't take pods others hand you). The reviews above tested only regulated nicotine products; mystery e-liquids aren't covered. In sequence, try entry 3 of this chapter (quit-smoking medication) first - the evidence there is harder and it's cheaper.

### 7. No sugary drinks - and switching to sugar-free doesn't settle it either
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: No money, no time. Switching to water or unsweetened tea actually saves money. The hard part is breaking the sweet-drink habit, sugar-free versions included.
- TL;DR: People who drink more than two sweet drinks a day have about 20% higher same-period mortality than people who barely drink them. One to two a day is still over 10% higher. Switching to sugar-free shows no benefit either: people drinking two or more diet drinks a day come out about 25% higher.
- Benefit: Two large American cohort studies (record-only, no randomization), about 118 thousand people and 36 thousand deaths. People drinking 2+ servings a day had about 21% higher mortality than those drinking less than 1 a month (HR 1.21). One to two servings a day: about 14% higher (HR 1.14). The European EPIC cohort study (record-only, no randomization) followed 450 thousand people in 10 countries. People drinking 2+ glasses a day versus under 1 a month: for sugar-sweetened soft drinks, about 8% higher (HR 1.08, 95% CI 1.01-1.16). For artificially sweetened drinks - the sugar-free versions - about 26% higher (HR 1.26, 1.16-1.35). All soft drinks combined: about 17% higher (HR 1.17, 1.11-1.22).
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Malik VS et al. (2019). Long-Term Consumption of Sugar-Sweetened and Artificially Sweetened Beverages and Risk of Mortality in US Adults. Circulation. <https://doi.org/10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.118.037401> ; Mullee A, Romaguera D, Pearson-Stuttard J, et al. (2019). Association Between Soft Drink Consumption and Mortality in 10 European Countries. JAMA Internal Medicine, 179(11), 1479-1490. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2019.2478>
- Notes: The dispute sits on the sugar-free side. The association for diet drinks comes out stronger than for sugared ones, which is very likely reverse causation: people already overweight or diabetic are the ones who switch to sugar-free. So don't conclude that sweeteners are more toxic. But it does at least show that "just switch to sugar-free and you're fine" has no evidence behind it. Switching to plain water or unsweetened tea is the safest move. Also, these studies only followed and recorded, with no randomization - heavy sweet-drink consumers tend to have worse lifestyles overall, so the numbers may run high.

### 8. Don't chew betel nut
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: No money, no time - and you save the betel-nut money. The hard part is kicking the chewing habit.
- TL;DR: Betel-nut chewers have about 20% higher same-period mortality than non-chewers. The risk of diabetes and metabolic syndrome runs nearly 50% higher. Oral cancer and esophageal cancer risk come on top of that.
- Benefit: Pooling 17 Asian studies with 388 thousand people. Chewers' mortality risk was about 21% higher than non-chewers' (relative risk 1.21, P=0.02). The mortality figure is computed over 179.6 thousand of them. Diabetes risk about 47% higher (1.47). Metabolic syndrome risk about 51% higher (1.51). Betel nut itself is also a known risk factor for oral and esophageal cancer
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Yamada T, Hara K, Kadowaki T (2013). Chewing betel quid and the risk of metabolic disease, cardiovascular disease, and all-cause mortality: a meta-analysis. PLoS One, 8(8), e70679. <https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0070679>
- Notes: The longer you've chewed and the more you chew daily, the higher the oral cancer risk. After quitting, the risk declines over time. Betel quid containing tobacco is more dangerous

### 9. Switch your household table salt to low-sodium salt (potassium salt)
<!-- tags: money=low time=low will=no benefit=mid lens=mortality -->
- Cost: A few yuan more per bag than regular salt. Just swap it in at your next purchase - no extra time. The taste barely changes.
- TL;DR: In a 20-thousand-person randomized trial, people whose household salt was swapped for low-sodium salt had about 12% lower five-year mortality and about 14% fewer strokes. This comes from a randomized two-group comparison, so it's more trustworthy than record-only numbers.
- Benefit: A randomized trial run in rural China with 20,995 people - all stroke survivors or hypertension patients over 60 - followed for 4.74 years. Result: the low-sodium-salt group versus the regular-salt group had about 12% lower mortality (RR 0.88). Stroke about 14% lower (RR 0.86). Major cardiovascular events about 13% lower (RR 0.87). No statistically detectable difference in hyperkalemia (excess blood potassium) events between the two groups.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Neal B et al. (2021). Effect of Salt Substitution on Cardiovascular Events and Death. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2105675> ; O'Donnell M et al. (2014). Urinary sodium and potassium excretion, mortality, and cardiovascular events. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1311889> (PURE, the disputing side)
- Notes: Disputed. The PURE study, which only followed and recorded without randomization, reported that people excreting under 3 g of sodium a day had about 27% higher risk of death plus cardiovascular events, and those excreting over 7 g about 15% higher - too little and too much are both bad, the middle is best. But in the rural-China randomized trial this entry cites, low-sodium salt only replaces part of the sodium and never pushes intake that low. Also note the trial subjects were high-risk older people. Healthy young people gain much less from switching. People with impaired kidney function, or those on potassium-sparing medication, should ask a doctor before switching.

### 10. Brush properly, clean between your teeth once a day, and get missing teeth replaced promptly
<!-- tags: money=low time=low will=some benefit=mid lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Floss or interdental brushes run a few dozen yuan a year; a cleaning at the dentist costs one to two hundred per visit. Two or three extra minutes a day. The hard part is making interdental cleaning a daily habit.
- TL;DR: People who floss or use interdental brushes daily have about 10% lower same-period mortality. Elderly people who have lost all their teeth have about 90% higher mortality. Fewer than 20 teeth left: about double.
- Benefit: A Japanese study of 9,676 people followed 6 years. Users of interdental cleaning tools had about 11% lower mortality risk (hazard ratio 0.89). Users of tongue-cleaning tools, about 23% lower (0.77). Separately, pooling multiple studies of community-dwelling older adults: people with no teeth at all had about 1.87 times the odds of death (odds ratio, 95% CI 1.35-2.59 - the plausible range). Fewer than 20 teeth: about 2.04 times (1.67-2.49)
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Wang K, Matsuyama Y, Kiuchi S, et al. (2026). Routine oral health practices and all-cause mortality. Journal of Dentistry. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdent.2026.106789> ; Ko MJ, Seo S, So JS, et al. (2026). Deteriorated oral health and function as risk factors for physical disability and mortality in community-dwelling older adults: a systematic review and meta-analysis. European Geriatric Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1007/s41999-025-01319-4>
- Notes: Disputed. These studies only follow and record. People with bad teeth tend to have worse overall health and finances to begin with. Whether the teeth drag down the body or the failing body drags down the teeth is unclear. But this habit costs next to nothing, and periodontitis plus missing teeth directly impair eating

### 11. Walk 7000-8000 steps a day
<!-- tags: money=0 time=high will=some benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Free. 60-90 minutes of walking a day, which can be folded into commuting and grocery runs. The hard part is doing it every day.
- TL;DR: People walking 5800 steps a day have about 40% lower same-period mortality than people walking only 3500. At 7800 steps, about 45% lower. More helps a bit further, then the benefit plateaus past a certain count. For people over 60, 6000-8000 steps is enough.
- Benefit: Pooling 15 cohort studies (record-only, no randomization) - 47,471 people, 3,013 deaths. Splitting people into four tiers by daily steps, the lowest tier's median was 3,553 steps/day. The other three tiers' medians were 5,801, 7,842 and 10,901 steps/day, and their mortality risks were about 40%, 45% and 53% lower than the lowest tier's (HR 0.60, 0.55, 0.47). For people over 60 the benefit flattens after 6000-8000 steps; for under-60s, after 8000-10,000. Another pooled analysis found the benefit kicks in from about 3,867 steps/day, with each additional 1,000 steps/day cutting mortality risk by about 15%.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Paluch AE et al. (2022). Daily steps and all-cause mortality: a meta-analysis of 15 international cohorts. Lancet Public Health. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-2667(21)00302-9> ; Banach M et al. (2023). The association between daily step count and all-cause and cardiovascular mortality: a meta-analysis. European Journal of Preventive Cardiology. <https://doi.org/10.1093/eurjpc/zwad229>
- Notes: Going from 4000 to 7000 steps is the best-value stretch - no need to chase ten thousand. Bear in mind these studies only follow and record: the lowest-step group includes people too sick to walk. They walk little because they're already declining, not the other way around, so the computed gap often runs high. But the more-you-walk-the-better trend is clear. This entry and entry 14 (150-300 minutes of moderate exercise a week) describe the same thing in different units - hitting either one counts.

### 12. If you have hypertension or high blood lipids, take your medication as prescribed - don't stop on your own
<!-- tags: money=low time=low will=some benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Centrally procured blood-pressure drugs and statins run a few yuan to a few dozen yuan a month. One dose a day, hardly any time. The hard part is keeping at it - not stopping because you feel fine.
- TL;DR: Every 10 mmHg of blood pressure reduction cuts mortality about 13% and stroke about 27%. Every 1-unit LDL drop from statins cuts mortality about 10%. People who take their medication on schedule have 30-45% lower mortality than those who keep missing doses.
- Benefit: Pooling multiple randomized trials: every 10 mmHg drop in systolic pressure (the higher of the two blood-pressure numbers) cuts mortality risk about 13% (RR 0.87). Major cardiovascular events about 20% lower (RR 0.80). Stroke about 27% lower (RR 0.73). Heart failure about 28% lower (RR 0.72). Statins lower low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL). Every 1.0 mmol/L LDL reduction cuts mortality risk about 10% (RR 0.90), and major vascular events about 22% (RR 0.78). Separately, pooling multiple record-only follow-up studies: people taking at least 80% of their prescribed doses had lower mortality than frequent dose-missers. For statins, about 45% lower (RR 0.55). For blood-pressure drugs, about 29% lower (RR 0.71).
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Ettehad D et al. (2016). Blood pressure lowering for prevention of cardiovascular disease and death: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Lancet. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(15)01225-8> ; Cholesterol Treatment Trialists' (CTT) Collaboration (2010). Efficacy and safety of more intensive lowering of LDL cholesterol: a meta-analysis of data from 170 000 participants in 26 randomised trials. Lancet. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(10)61350-5> ; Chowdhury R et al. (2013). Adherence to cardiovascular therapy: a meta-analysis of prevalence and clinical consequences. European Heart Journal. <https://doi.org/10.1093/eurheartj/eht295>
- Notes: This entry applies only to people a doctor has judged should be on medication - healthy people shouldn't take these. The "keeps taking it" figures come from record-only studies; people who stick with medication tend to be more disciplined overall, which inflates the apparent benefit. The two sets of randomized-trial numbers earlier are more reliable. This chapter has no separate entry on blood-sugar control: the evidence that pushing blood sugar lower improves all-cause mortality is not as consistent as it is for blood pressure and lipids.

### 13. Sleep about 7 hours a night, on a fixed schedule
<!-- tags: money=0 time=high will=some benefit=mid lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Free. You do have to set aside the time - for most people that means trading phone-scrolling time for sleep. The hard part is fixing your bedtime and wake time.
- TL;DR: People chronically sleeping under 7 hours have about 10% higher same-period mortality. The more irregular the schedule, the higher the risk. The most regular sleepers run 20% to nearly 50% lower than the most irregular. In other words, schedule regularity reveals mortality risk better than sleep duration does.
- Benefit: Pooling 16 follow-up studies (record-only, no randomization) - 1.38 million people, 113 thousand deaths. Short sleepers had about 12% higher mortality risk (RR 1.12). Long sleepers about 30% higher (RR 1.30). Another pooled analysis tiered by sleep duration found 7 hours the lowest-risk tier. Under 7 hours, each hour less meant about 6% higher risk (RR 1.06); over 7, each hour more about 13% higher (RR 1.13). UK Biobank has wrist-wearable records for about 61 thousand people (record-only, no randomization). Splitting people into five tiers by schedule regularity, the four more regular tiers had 20-48% lower mortality than the most irregular tier. Schedule regularity predicts mortality better than sleep duration. Another UK Biobank study measured the sleep timing of 51,562 people by wearable, during which 3,853 developed new cardiovascular disease. Social jetlag means the gap between your sleep midpoint on workdays and on weekends. People with social jetlag of 2+ hours had about 30% higher cardiovascular risk (HR 1.30, 95% CI 1.11-1.54). The association held even among people whose sleep duration was normal.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Cappuccio FP et al. (2010). Sleep duration and all-cause mortality: a systematic review and meta-analysis of prospective studies. Sleep. <https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/33.5.585> ; Yin J et al. (2017). Relationship of Sleep Duration With All-Cause Mortality and Cardiovascular Events: A Systematic Review and Dose-Response Meta-Analysis of Prospective Cohort Studies. JAHA. <https://doi.org/10.1161/JAHA.117.005947> ; Windred DP et al. (2024). Sleep regularity is a stronger predictor of mortality risk than sleep duration: A prospective cohort study. Sleep. <https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/zsad253> ; Kumar N, Krishnamurthy S (2026). Social jet lag is associated with incident cardiovascular disease independent of sleep duration and cardiac genetic risk. Journal of Internal Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1111/joim.70133>
- Notes: What to fix is sleeping too little and an erratic schedule - don't deliberately cut your sleep short. The higher risk among long sleepers is mostly reverse causation: depression, chronic illness and sleep apnea make people sleep more. Also, the schedule-regularity point currently rests on two follow-up studies, both record-only with no randomization - on its own that would be grade-B evidence. For how to recover after staying up, see entry 39 of this chapter (catch up on sleep the very next night).

### 14. Rack up 150-300 minutes of moderate exercise a week - brisk walking is enough
<!-- tags: money=0 time=high will=some benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Free. 20-45 minutes a day. The hard part is sticking with it long-term.
- TL;DR: People who brisk-walk 150-300 minutes a week have about 30% lower same-period mortality than non-exercisers. Even falling short of the target buys about 20%. At three to five times the recommended amount the benefit bottoms out at about 40% lower. Going beyond that doesn't lower it further, but does no harm either.
- Benefit: Pooling multiple follow-up studies (record-only, no randomization): people reaching 1-2 times the guideline minimum had about 31% lower mortality risk than non-exercisers (HR 0.69). That amount is 7.5-15 MET·h/week, roughly 150-300 minutes of brisk walking a week. People below the minimum still ran about 20% lower (HR 0.80). At 3-5 times the minimum, about 39% lower (HR 0.61) - the peak of the benefit. Beyond that, no further drop but no harm either (10x and above is HR 0.69). Another pooled study using accelerometer measurements: the quarter of people with the most moderate-to-vigorous activity had about 48% lower mortality risk than the quarter with the least (HR 0.52). An accelerometer is a body-worn instrument that measures activity directly.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Arem H et al. (2015). Leisure time physical activity and mortality: a detailed pooled analysis of the dose-response relationship. JAMA Internal Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2015.0533> ; Ekelund U et al. (2019). Dose-response associations between accelerometry measured physical activity and sedentary time and all cause mortality: systematic review and harmonised meta-analysis. BMJ. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.l4570>
- Notes: Pick either this entry or entry 11 (walk 7000-8000 steps a day). Bear in mind these studies only follow and record. The ones using devices to measure activity had short follow-up and older participants, so their numbers carry a "got sick first, then stopped moving" component. The true benefit isn't as big as "risk down to 0.52 times".

### 15. Play a racket sport three times a week, 45 minutes a session
<!-- tags: money=low time=mid will=some benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Court fees run a few dozen yuan a session. About 2 hours a week. The hard part is rounding up people and a court, and showing up weekly.
- TL;DR: Regular tennis, badminton and table-tennis players have about half the same-period mortality of non-players, and about 56% lower cardiovascular mortality. Swimmers run about 28% lower. Running and soccer showed no difference in this dataset.
- Benefit: A British follow-up study of 80.3 thousand people compared those who did a given sport with those who didn't. Racket-sport players had about 47% lower mortality risk (hazard ratio 0.53), with a plausible range of 95% CI 0.40-0.69. Cardiovascular mortality about 56% lower (0.44, 0.24-0.83). Racket sports means tennis, badminton and table tennis. Swimmers' two figures were 0.72 and 0.59. Aerobics: 0.73 and 0.64. Cycling: 0.85. Running and soccer showed no statistically detectable difference
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Oja P, Kelly P, Pedisic Z, et al. (2017). Associations of specific types of sports and exercise with all-cause and cardiovascular-disease mortality: a cohort study of 80 306 British adults. British Journal of Sports Medicine, 51(10), 812-817. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2016-096822>
- Notes: Disputed. These studies only follow and record. Racket players are healthier and more social to begin with. The fact that running shows no difference also suggests the effect may come from "what kind of person picks which sport". Don't take this entry to mean running is useless - this chapter has separate entries computed by total exercise volume

### 16. Accumulate 4-5 minutes a day of scattered vigorous activity - stairs, brisk walking to get somewhere
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Free, and no need to carve out workout time. The hard part is remembering to take the stairs and pick up the pace instead of taking it easy.
- TL;DR: Among people who never formally exercise, those getting three 1-2-minute bursts of vigorous activity a day have about 40% lower same-period mortality than those with none at all, and nearly 50% lower cardiovascular mortality. Vigorous activity means things like climbing stairs and hurrying on foot.
- Benefit: A UK Biobank study of 25.2 thousand people who don't formally exercise, followed on average 6.9 years, during which 852 died. People getting 3 daily bouts of 1-2 minutes of incidental vigorous activity had 38-40% lower all-cause and cancer mortality than people with none. Cardiovascular mortality 48-49% lower. People accumulating 4.4 minutes of such activity a day had 26-30% lower all-cause and cancer mortality, and 32-34% lower cardiovascular mortality
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Stamatakis E, Ahmadi MN, Gill JMR, et al. (2022). Association of wearable device-measured vigorous intermittent lifestyle physical activity with mortality. Nature Medicine, 28, 2521-2529. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-022-02100-x>
- Notes: Disputed. This study measured activity with wearables, which is more reliable than recall questionnaires. But it still only followed and recorded, with no randomization, and only for 6.9 years. The subjects were people who don't exercise at all - this entry doesn't apply to those already exercising regularly

### 17. Do 30-60 minutes of strength training a week
<!-- tags: money=0 time=mid will=some benefit=mid lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Bodyweight squats and push-ups are free. Train 1-2 times a week, 20-30 minutes a session. The hard part is getting it onto the weekly schedule.
- TL;DR: People doing 30-60 minutes of strength training a week have 10-20% lower same-period mortality than non-trainers. More training isn't better. Strength plus cardio together works best.
- Benefit: Pooling multiple follow-up studies (record-only, no randomization): strength trainers had 10-17% lower mortality risk than non-trainers. It's not the more the better - the biggest drop, about 10-20%, comes at around 30-60 minutes a week, and training more adds nothing. People doing both strength and cardio had lower mortality than people doing neither.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Momma H et al. (2022). Muscle-strengthening activities are associated with lower risk and mortality in major non-communicable diseases: a systematic review and meta-analysis of cohort studies. British Journal of Sports Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2021-105061>
- Notes: These studies only follow and record, and the exercise amounts are self-reported. The "too much training backfires" segment rests on weak evidence - no need to cap your training over it. Strength training also protects older people against falls and muscle loss - that's covered in chapter 1.

### 18. Don't sit for long stretches - get up and move every so often
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Free, and standing up a few times takes almost no time. The hard part is that you sink into the chair and lose track - set a reminder.
- TL;DR: The longest sitters have 2.6 times the same-period mortality of the shortest sitters. Long uninterrupted sitting bouts add a separate risk on top. But 60-75 minutes of moderate activity a day basically cancels out the extra risk from prolonged sitting.
- Benefit: An American follow-up study (record-only, no randomization) measured sitting with accelerometers. Splitting people into four groups by total daily sitting time, the most-sitting group had 2.63 times the mortality risk of the least-sitting (HR 2.63). Splitting again by longest uninterrupted sitting bout: the longest-bout group had 1.96 times the risk of the shortest-bout group (HR 1.96). In other words, total sitting time and bout length are each independently associated. Another pooled analysis combining studies of over a million people (record-only, no randomization): people sitting 8+ hours a day with almost no exercise had 1.59 times the mortality risk of the most active people sitting under 4 hours a day (HR 1.59). About 60-75 minutes of moderate activity a day can offset the added risk from prolonged sitting. Even the most active group sitting 8+ hours a day had an HR of only 1.04 - no statistically detectable difference.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Diaz KM et al. (2017). Patterns of Sedentary Behavior and Mortality in U.S. Middle-Aged and Older Adults: A National Cohort Study. Annals of Internal Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.7326/M17-0212> ; Ekelund U et al. (2016). Does physical activity attenuate, or even eliminate, the detrimental association of sitting time with mortality? A harmonised meta-analysis of data from more than 1 million men and women. Lancet. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(16)30370-1>
- Notes: The reliable conclusion is: how much you move matters more than how long you sit - with enough exercise, the sitting risk essentially disappears. Bear in mind these studies only follow and record, and the longest sitters include many people who were already ill - the "2.63 times risk" figure clearly contains a "got sick first, then sat all day" component. Also, in the same pooled analysis, watching TV 3+ hours a day carried risk at every activity level; being in the most active group only pushed that threshold out to 5 hours.

### 19. Eat less processed meat (ham, bacon, sausage, lunch meat)
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Free - it even saves money - and takes no time. The hard part is eating less of something you love.
- TL;DR: The heaviest consumers of ham, bacon and sausage have about 20% higher same-period mortality than the lightest. Each extra daily serving of processed meat adds about 20% too.
- Benefit: Pooling multiple follow-up studies (record-only, no randomization): the highest processed-meat group had about 23% higher mortality risk than the lowest (RR 1.23). All red meat combined, about 29% higher (RR 1.29). Unprocessed red meat, about 10% higher (RR 1.10), though that one showed no statistically detectable difference. Another pooled analysis computed per daily serving: each additional daily serving of processed meat, about 23% higher mortality risk (RR 1.23); of red meat, about 10% higher (RR 1.10).
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Larsson SC, Orsini N (2014). Red meat and processed meat consumption and all-cause mortality: a meta-analysis. American Journal of Epidemiology. <https://doi.org/10.1093/aje/kwt261> ; Schwingshackl L et al. (2017). Food groups and risk of all-cause mortality: a systematic review and meta-analysis of prospective studies. American Journal of Clinical Nutrition. <https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.117.153148> ; Johnston BC et al. (2019). Unprocessed Red Meat and Processed Meat Consumption: Dietary Guideline Recommendations From the NutriRECS Consortium. Annals of Internal Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.7326/M19-1621> (NutriRECS guideline, the disputing side)
- Notes: Disputed. The NutriRECS guideline applied the standard GRADE evidence-rating method and classed these studies as "low certainty", offering only a very weak recommendation: keep eating as you do now. The two sides disagree about how hard the evidence is, not about direction. No study says processed meat is beneficial. Unprocessed red meat has a smaller effect that isn't statistically detectable. What needs watching is processed meat.

### 20. Drink less alcohol, or none
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Free - it even saves money - and takes no time. The hard part is the moment of awkwardness turning down drinks in social settings.
- TL;DR: Past 100 g of pure alcohol a week, the more you drink the earlier you die. 100 g of pure alcohol is about 2.5 L of beer. At age 40, people drinking 100-200 g a week lose about half a year of life; 200-350 g loses one to two years; over 350 g loses four to five. For overall health, the best-value amount is none.
- Benefit: Pooling 83 prospective studies (record-only, no randomization) covering 600 thousand current drinkers. The low point of mortality risk sits at no more than 100 g of pure alcohol a week - roughly 2.5 L of 5% beer, or 300 mL of 40% baijiu. At age 40, drinking 100-200 g a week shortens life expectancy by about 6 months; 200-350 g by 1-2 years; over 350 g by 4-5 years. (The original reports all three in years of life.) The Global Burden of Disease study GBD 2016, combining all kinds of health loss, put the lowest-risk intake at 0 drinks a week. Another pooled analysis corrected for study-design biases: people drinking 1.3-24 g a day versus lifetime abstainers had about 7% lower mortality risk (RR 0.93), but with no statistically detectable difference. People drinking 45-64 g a day ran about 19% higher (RR 1.19); 65+ g a day, about 35% higher (RR 1.35).
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Wood AM et al. (2018). Risk thresholds for alcohol consumption: combined analysis of individual-participant data for 599 912 current drinkers in 83 prospective studies. Lancet. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(18)30134-X> ; GBD 2016 Alcohol Collaborators (2018). Alcohol use and burden for 195 countries and territories, 1990-2016: a systematic analysis for the Global Burden of Disease Study 2016. Lancet. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(18)31310-2> ; Zhao J et al. (2023). Association Between Daily Alcohol Intake and Risk of All-Cause Mortality: A Systematic Review and Meta-analyses. JAMA Network Open. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2023.6185> ; Di Castelnuovo A et al. (2006). Alcohol dosing and total mortality in men and women: an updated meta-analysis of 34 prospective studies. Archives of Internal Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1001/archinte.166.22.2437> (the disputing side)
- Notes: Disputed. The "a little is good for you" side rests on a pooled analysis of 34 prospective studies reporting that light drinkers had at most 17-18% lower total mortality, at up to 4 drinks a day for men and 2 for women - within those limits, heavier drinkers had lower mortality. The opposing side argues the "non-drinker" group is contaminated with people who quit because of illness and people who were frail to begin with; one pooled analysis stripped out exactly these confounders, after which the protective effect vanished. The Global Burden of Disease study likewise finds 0 safest. The prudent reading: light drinking doesn't clearly shorten life, heavy drinking definitely does. "Starting to drink for your health" has no basis. For how to cut back, see entry 22 of this chapter (count your drinks first, then talk to a doctor). Daily drinkers must not quit cold turkey on their own - see entry 21 of this chapter (don't white-knuckle a quit).

### 21. If you drink daily and get shaky and palpitating when you stop, don't quit cold turkey on your own
<!-- tags: money=low time=mid will=some benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: One appointment at a psychiatry or addiction-medicine department, a few to a few dozen yuan. Severe cases need a few days in hospital.
- TL;DR: A heavy daily drinker who stops abruptly can go into withdrawal. Mild cases shake, sweat, race and can't sleep; severe cases seize or develop delirium tremens, which is a fatal emergency. Norway followed over 30 thousand people: of those who had delirium tremens, 8% died each year thereafter. If you want to quit, go to a hospital first and state plainly how much you drink each day.
- Benefit: A 2026 clinical review states that long-term heavy drinkers develop withdrawal syndrome after abrupt cessation or sharp reduction, with signs including tremor, autonomic hyperactivity, anxiety, insomnia, perceptual disturbances, seizures and delirium tremens - in general medical practice it is "common and potentially life-threatening". Norway's national registry cohort covered 36,287 people diagnosed with alcohol dependence, alcohol withdrawal state or delirium tremens between 2009 and 2015: annual mortality was 8.0% in the delirium tremens group, 5.0% in the withdrawal-state group, and 3.6% in the dependence group. The delirium tremens group's standardized mortality ratio was 9.8 (95% CI 8.9-10.7, the plausible range) - meaning about 9.8 times the mortality risk of the same-age general population.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Caputo F, Lungaro L, Costanzini A, De Giorgio R, Addolorato G (2026). Alcohol withdrawal syndrome in hospitalized patients: a practical review. European Journal of Internal Medicine, 107103. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ejim.2026.107103> ; Bramness JG, Heiberg IH, Høye A, Rossow I (2023). Mortality and alcohol-related morbidity in patients with delirium tremens, alcohol withdrawal state or alcohol dependence in Norway: A register-based prospective cohort study. Addiction, 118(12), 2352-2359. <https://doi.org/10.1111/add.16297>
- Notes: How to judge for yourself: drinking almost daily; getting shaky, sweaty, palpitating and sleepless after a day without; needing a morning drink to feel right - any one of these means don't stop on your own. Hospitals routinely get you through those days with benzodiazepines while supplementing thiamine (vitamin B1). This is graded B because the Norwegian cohort describes "what happens to people after delirium tremens", not "what happens when you quit cold turkey on your own" - the step between them is an inference. This entry doesn't tell you to keep drinking; it tells you to quit a different way. For how much per week counts as a lot, see entry 20 of this chapter (drink less or none); for how to cut back, entry 22 (count your drinks first, then talk to a doctor).

### 22. To drink less, first count how much you drank this week, then spend a few minutes with a doctor
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some benefit=small lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Free. Counting drinks is one line in a notebook each day. Asking a doctor during a regular visit costs nothing extra.
- TL;DR: A doctor spending a few minutes reviewing your intake, spelling out the risks and helping you set a target leads to an average of 20 g less pure alcohol a week one year later - about half a liter of beer. Longer counseling adds nothing. For those who can't stop anymore, two drugs proven abroad work: roughly one more person in every 12 treated avoids sliding back into heavy drinking.
- Benefit: Pooling 34 randomized trials with 15,197 people, those receiving brief intervention drank 20 g less pure alcohol a week after one year than those getting minimal or no intervention (95% CI: 12 to 28 g less, the plausible range; moderate-quality evidence). Baseline intake in these studies averaged 244 g a week. Brief intervention means up to 5 sessions totaling under 60 minutes of advice or lifestyle counseling; longer counseling added no benefit. Medication: pooling 122 randomized trials with 22,803 people, acamprosate lets 1 extra person in every 12 treated (95% CI 8 to 26) avoid returning to any drinking; oral naltrexone at 50 mg a day lets 1 extra person in every 12 (8 to 26) avoid returning to heavy drinking.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Kaner EF, Beyer FR, Muirhead C, et al. (2018). Effectiveness of brief alcohol interventions in primary care populations. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 2, CD004148. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD004148.pub4> ; Jonas DE, Amick HR, Feltner C, et al. (2014). Pharmacotherapy for adults with alcohol use disorders in outpatient settings: a systematic review and meta-analysis. JAMA, 311(18), 1889-1900. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2014.3628>
- Notes: The benefit tier is "small" because both bodies of evidence measured how much people drank, not mortality - under this book's rules, surrogate endpoints alone rate a "small". Most medication trials were run abroad, on subjects who mostly met the diagnostic criteria for alcohol dependence, and all included psychosocial intervention - so the numbers are "extra benefit on top of that". Whether these two drugs are indicated and prescribable in China is for doctors and the drug labels to say - don't buy them online yourself. If withdrawal symptoms have already appeared, see entry 21 of this chapter (don't white-knuckle a quit).

### 23. Eat a small handful of nuts every day
<!-- tags: money=low time=low will=no benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: 28 g a day runs about two to three hundred yuan a year. Eat as you go - no time cost.
- TL;DR: Regular nut eaters have about 20% lower same-period mortality than non-eaters. Even once a week shows a difference, about 10%. A handful a day shows the clearest difference.
- Benefit: Two American follow-up studies, 119 thousand people, over 3 million person-years. Person-years means headcount times years followed. Versus non-nut-eaters: people eating nuts under once a week had about 7% lower mortality risk (hazard ratio 0.93, 95% CI 0.90-0.96, the plausible range). Once a week, about 11% lower (0.89, 0.86-0.93). 2-4 times a week, about 13% lower (0.87, 0.83-0.90). 5-6 times, about 15% lower (0.85, 0.79-0.91). 7+ times a week, about 20% lower (0.80, 0.73-0.86)
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Bao Y, Han J, Hu FB, et al. (2013). Association of nut consumption with total and cause-specific mortality. New England Journal of Medicine, 369(21), 2001-2011. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1307352>
- Notes: Buy plain, unsalted ones. Nuts are calorie-dense - don't treat them as an open-ended snack. Bear in mind these studies only followed groups of people; nut eaters have better lifestyles overall, so discount the numbers somewhat

### 24. Swap part of your red meat for fish and poultry
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some benefit=small lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Free, no time. This is swapping one meat for another, not buying extra. The hard part is bending your taste habits.
- TL;DR: Two extra servings a week of processed or red meat raises same-period mortality about 3%. Swap in the same number of servings of fish or poultry and the rise disappears. This entry's effect is on the small side for this chapter - a change you make in passing, not one to sweat over.
- Benefit: Pooling 6 American follow-up studies with nearly 30 thousand people. Two extra servings of processed meat a week: about 3% higher mortality risk (hazard ratio 1.03), plausible range 95% CI 1.02-1.05. Two extra servings of unprocessed red meat: also about 3% higher (1.03, 1.01-1.05). The same eating pattern with poultry instead: 0.99 (0.97-1.02); with fish: 0.99 (0.97-1.01). Neither showed a statistically detectable difference
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Zhong VW, Van Horn L, Greenland P, et al. (2020). Associations of Processed Meat, Unprocessed Red Meat, Poultry, or Fish Intake With Incident Cardiovascular Disease and All-Cause Mortality. JAMA Internal Medicine, 180(4), 503-512. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2019.6969>
- Notes: The difference from two servings a week is tiny - don't expect extra years from swapping meat. This entry's use is simpler: when you're spending the same money on meat anyway, which kind to pick

### 25. Swap part of your refined rice and white flour for whole grains
<!-- tags: money=low time=low will=some benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Brown rice, oats and whole-wheat flour cost a bit more than refined versions; no time cost. The hard part is adjusting to the taste.
- TL;DR: People eating an extra 90 g of whole grains a day have about 17% lower same-period mortality. 90 g is about three servings. The benefit keeps growing up to around 200 g a day.
- Benefit: Pooling multiple follow-up studies (record-only, no randomization): each extra 90 g of whole grains a day cut mortality risk about 17% (RR 0.83). 90 g is about 3 servings. The benefit continued out to 210-225 g a day. In another pooled analysis, each extra daily serving cut risk about 8% (RR 0.92).
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Aune D et al. (2016). Whole grain consumption and risk of cardiovascular disease, cancer, and all cause and cause specific mortality: systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis of prospective studies. BMJ. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.i2716> ; Schwingshackl L et al. (2017). Food groups and risk of all-cause mortality: a systematic review and meta-analysis of prospective studies. American Journal of Clinical Nutrition. <https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.117.153148>
- Notes: No need to go fully whole-grain - swapping half already puts you in the range where the benefit is clearest. ### 26. Drink tea three or more times a week
<!-- tags: money=low time=low will=no benefit=mid lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Tens to hundreds of yuan a year. A cup takes a few minutes to brew - hardly any time.
- TL;DR: Habitual tea drinkers have about 15% lower same-period mortality. Counting from age 50, they live 1.26 years longer on average and gain 1.41 years free of cardiovascular disease.
- Benefit: A Chinese study of 100.9 thousand people (China-PAR), half of them followed a full 7.3 years. Habitual tea drinkers had about 15% lower mortality risk (hazard ratio 0.85, 95% CI 0.79-0.90, the plausible range). Counting from age 50: habitual tea drinkers had 1.41 more years free of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease and 1.26 more years of life expectancy
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Wang X, Liu F, Li J, et al. (2020). Tea consumption and the risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease and all-cause mortality: The China-PAR project. European Journal of Preventive Cardiology, 27(18), 1956-1963. <https://doi.org/10.1177/2047487319894685>
- Notes: The dispute: these studies only follow and record, and among Chinese men tea drinkers smoke and drink at higher rates. The authors adjusted away part of that, but not cleanly. One more thing: don't drink it scalding - see this chapter's entry on hot-drink temperature

### 27. Drink three to four cups of coffee a day, no sugar, no milk foam
<!-- tags: money=low time=low will=no benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: A yuan or two a day if you brew it yourself. A few minutes a cup - hardly any time.
- TL;DR: People drinking three to four cups of coffee a day have about 17% lower same-period mortality than non-drinkers.
- Benefit: A review combined the results of 201 pooled analyses, whose underlying original studies were all record-only with no randomization. Result: people drinking 3-4 cups a day versus non-drinkers had 17% lower mortality risk (relative risk 0.83, 95% CI 0.79-0.88, the plausible range)
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Poole R, Kennedy OJ, Roderick P, et al. (2017). Coffee consumption and health: umbrella review of meta-analyses of multiple health outcomes. BMJ, 359, j5024. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.j5024>
- Notes: The dispute: the original authors wrote that the evidence comes almost entirely from follow-up records and that "robust randomized controlled trials are needed to understand whether the observed associations are causal" - meaning only randomizing people into two groups can settle causation, which record-watching can't. In practice, drink it black - sugar and milk foam cancel the benefit. Pregnant women and people with arrhythmia, anxiety or insomnia: this entry doesn't apply

### 28. Eat 5 servings (about 400 g) of fruit and vegetables a day
<!-- tags: money=low time=mid will=some benefit=mid lens=mortality -->
- Cost: A few to a dozen-plus yuan a day. Some daily washing and cutting time. The hard part is buying daily and hitting the amount daily.
- TL;DR: Each extra 200 g of fruit and vegetables a day cuts same-period mortality about 10%. People hitting 5 servings a day run about 13% lower than those eating only 2. More than that doesn't go lower.
- Benefit: Pooling multiple follow-up studies (record-only, no randomization): each extra 200 g of fruit and vegetables a day cut mortality risk about 10% (RR 0.90), a benefit extending out to 800 g a day. Two large American follow-up studies plus a pooled analysis of 26 studies: people eating 5 servings a day had about 13% lower mortality risk than those eating 2 (HR 0.87). The best mix was 2 fruit plus 3 vegetable servings - more didn't go lower.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Aune D et al. (2017). Fruit and vegetable intake and the risk of cardiovascular disease, total cancer and all-cause mortality: a systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis of prospective studies. International Journal of Epidemiology. <https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/dyw319> ; Wang DD et al. (2021). Fruit and Vegetable Intake and Mortality: Results From 2 Prospective Cohort Studies of US Men and Women and a Meta-Analysis of 26 Cohort Studies. Circulation. <https://doi.org/10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.120.048996>
- Notes: Five servings is enough - no need to chase ten. Both studies agree on that point. Bear in mind these studies only follow and record, with obvious confounding: people who eat more vegetables differ in income, education and exercise. So "about 10% lower" is the ceiling.

### 29. Eat less ultra-processed food (chips, instant noodles, pastries, fast food)
<!-- tags: money=0 time=high will=yes benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: No extra money - you're just buying differently. You do need to cook, or spend time choosing less-processed ingredients, which takes some time every day. The hard part is fighting the "convenient is fine" inertia long-term.
- TL;DR: The heaviest consumers of ultra-processed food have about 20% higher same-period mortality than the lightest, and 50% higher cardiovascular mortality. But this body of evidence is rated fairly uncertain - read the numbers with a discount.
- Benefit: A review combined multiple pooled analyses, whose underlying original studies were all record-only with no randomization. Result: heavy ultra-processed-food consumers versus light ones had about 21% higher mortality risk (RR 1.21), and about 50% higher cardiovascular death (RR 1.50). These two were graded "highly suggestive" and "convincing" respectively - but under the standard GRADE evidence-rating method their certainty is low or very low.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Lane MM et al. (2024). Ultra-processed food exposure and adverse health outcomes: umbrella review of epidemiological meta-analyses. BMJ. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2023-077310>
- Notes: The dispute has three parts. One, the NOVA classification lumps foods with very different nutritional profiles into one category. Two, it overlaps heavily with sugary drinks and processed meat (entries 7 and 19), so it's unclear which factor is doing the work. Three, under the standard GRADE method the rating is low anyway. So after doing those two entries, how much this one adds is uncertain. The opposing evidence is that same review's low rating itself; no original study has reached the opposite conclusion. So do entries 7 and 19 (no sugary drinks, less processed meat) first, then look at this one.

### 30. Don't cook or heat with coal or firewood - switch to electricity or gas
<!-- tags: money=high time=low will=no benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: A rural household replacing its stove plus fuel runs several hundred to over a thousand yuan a year. It's a one-time change with no ongoing time cost. City homes are mostly on electricity or gas already.
- TL;DR: People cooking with coal or firewood have about 10% higher same-period mortality; heating with them, about 14% higher. People who already switched to electricity or gas have 13-33% lower mortality than those still burning coal and wood.
- Benefit: A Chinese chronic-disease prospective study (record-only, no randomization) of 271 thousand adults without cardiovascular disease. People cooking with solid fuels like coal and firewood had about 11% higher mortality risk than those using electricity or gas (HR 1.11). Heating with solid fuels, about 14% higher (HR 1.14). Some people had already switched from solid to clean fuels: versus those still burning solid fuels, their cooking-related risk was about 13% lower (HR 0.87) and heating-related risk about 33% lower (HR 0.67). Outdoor PM2.5 is a separate account. Pooling 104 follow-up studies: every 10 µg/m³ higher long-term exposure raised natural-cause mortality risk about 8% (RR 1.08). Natural causes means death from illness, not accident.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Yu K et al. (2018). Association of Solid Fuel Use With Risk of Cardiovascular and All-Cause Mortality in Rural China. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2018.2151> ; Chen J, Hoek G (2020). Long-term exposure to PM and all-cause and cause-specific mortality: A systematic review and meta-analysis. Environment International. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.envint.2020.105974>
- Notes: These studies only follow and record. Households that switch fuels also tend to be better off - the "risk down to 0.67 times" figure carries household-wealth effects. As for outdoor PM2.5, what an individual can do is limited: move, wear a mask, run a purifier. Air purifiers have no mortality-endpoint studies, so this chapter gives no number for them.

### 31. Let hot things sit before drinking - no scalding tea, soup or coffee
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Free - just wait two or three extra minutes each time. The hard part is resisting when you're eager.
- TL;DR: Drinking very hot tea multiplies esophageal cancer odds 8-fold versus warm tea. Even just "hot" doubles them. Drinking within two minutes of pouring means 5 times the risk of waiting four or more.
- Benefit: In a high-esophageal-cancer region of northern Iran, 300 patients were compared with 571 cancer-free people. All comparisons below are against warm-tea drinkers. "Hot" tea drinkers had about 2.07 times the odds of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (odds ratio), plausible range 95% CI 1.28-3.35. "Very hot": about 8.16 times (3.93-16.9). Drinking within 2 minutes of pouring: about 5.41 times those waiting 4+ minutes (2.63-11.1)
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Islami F, Pourshams A, Nasrollahzadeh D, et al. (2009). Tea drinking habits and oesophageal cancer in a high risk area in northern Iran: population based case-control study. BMJ, 338, b929. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.b929> ; Loomis D, Guyton KZ, Grosse Y, et al. (2016). Carcinogenicity of drinking coffee, mate, and very hot beverages. Lancet Oncology, 17(7), 877-878. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S1470-2045(16)30239-X>
- Notes: The International Agency for Research on Cancer classifies beverages above 65°C as Group 2A - probably carcinogenic. The same assessment found coffee itself not carcinogenic. China's Chaoshan region and the Taihang mountain belt have high esophageal cancer rates, linked to the habit of drinking things scalding
### 32. Get some daylight when you're out during the day - don't go all day without light
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no benefit=mid lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Free. Ten-odd minutes a day; the commute and lunch break are enough.
- TL;DR: Women who deliberately avoid the sun have life expectancies 0.6-2.1 years shorter than the most-exposed. The original also reports that non-smokers who avoid the sun live about as long as the smokers in the highest-exposure group.
- Benefit: A Swedish study of 29.5 thousand women followed 20 years. Versus the highest-exposure group, deliberate sun avoiders lost 0.6 to 2.1 years of life expectancy. The authors wrote that "nonsmokers who avoided sun exposure had a life expectancy similar to smokers in the highest sun exposure group"
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Lindqvist PG, Epstein E, Nielsen K, et al. (2016). Avoidance of sun exposure as a risk factor for major causes of death: a competing risk analysis of the Melanoma in Southern Sweden cohort. Journal of Internal Medicine, 280(4), 375-387. <https://doi.org/10.1111/joim.12496>
- Notes: The dispute: Sweden is high-latitude with little sun, so the conclusion doesn't transfer directly to China. Also, sun avoiders may be frail and homebound to begin with. Separately, don't get sunburned - shield yourself in harsh midday light, because sun exposure raises skin cancer risk

### 33. Keep your BMI in 20-25; if overweight, lose it
<!-- tags: money=0 time=high will=yes benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Free. Diet plus exercise takes time every day. This is the most willpower-demanding entry in the chapter: long-term control of eating and moving, and maintaining the loss is harder than losing.
- TL;DR: Mortality is lowest in the BMI 20-25 range. BMI 27.5-30 runs about 20% higher, 30-35 about 45% higher, 35-40 nearly double. East Asians are more sensitive to overweight - every 5 BMI points higher means about 40% higher risk.
- Benefit: Pooling individual-level data from 239 prospective studies (record-only, no randomization). The analysis kept only three kinds of people: never-smokers, those without chronic disease at entry, and those who survived the first 5 years after entry. Result: mortality risk bottoms out at BMI 20-25. BMI 25-27.5 runs about 7% higher (HR 1.07). 27.5-30, about 20% higher (HR 1.20). 30-35, about 45% higher (HR 1.45). 35-40, about 94% higher (HR 1.94). 40-60, 2.76 times (HR 2.76). For people above BMI 25, each 5 kg/m² higher meant about 39% higher risk in East Asian populations (HR 1.39).
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Global BMI Mortality Collaboration (2016). Body-mass index and all-cause mortality: individual-participant-data meta-analysis of 239 prospective studies in four continents. Lancet. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(16)30175-1> ; Flegal KM et al. (2013). Association of all-cause mortality with overweight and obesity using standard body mass index categories: a systematic review and meta-analysis. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2012.113905> (the disputing side)
- Notes: Disputed. One side holds that "a bit overweight means living longer": their pooled analysis found overweight people had about 6% lower mortality risk, and mildly obese people about 5% lower - slightly heavy, slightly lower. The disagreement is mainly over whether to strip out three kinds of data: smokers, people already ill, and the first few years after study entry - the reason for stripping is that seriously ill people lose weight first. The 239-study analysis stripped all three, and the risk from overweight reappeared. One more caveat: no randomized trial proves that losing weight itself lowers all-cause mortality. The benefit above comes from comparing people of different weights side by side - it can't be read directly as "a discount you collect by losing the weight". People trying to lose weight don't need to fuss over meal timing: neither breakfast nor 16:8 intermittent fasting brings extra benefit - see entry 26 of chapter 6 (don't count on breakfast or 16:8 to control your weight)

### 34. Eat chili peppers four or more times a week
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Free, no time.
- TL;DR: People eating chili peppers 4+ times a week have about 23% lower same-period mortality than rare eaters, and about a third lower cardiovascular mortality.
- Benefit: An Italian study of 22.8 thousand people, half followed a full 8.2 years, with 1,236 deaths. People eating chili 4+ times a week had about 23% lower mortality risk than rare eaters (hazard ratio 0.77), plausible range 95% CI 0.66-0.90. Cardiovascular death about 34% lower (0.66, 0.50-0.86)
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Bonaccio M, Di Castelnuovo A, Costanzo S, et al. (2019). Chili Pepper Consumption and Mortality in Italian Adults. Journal of the American College of Cardiology, 74(25), 3139-3149. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2019.09.068>
- Notes: The dispute: this was record-keeping done against a Mediterranean-diet background, so chili eaters may simply eat closer to the local traditional diet overall. Also, people with reflux, hemorrhoids or irritable bowel get worse symptoms from chili - no need to force it

### 35. Drink one or two servings of milk or yogurt a day
<!-- tags: money=low time=low will=no benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: A few yuan a day, no time.
- TL;DR: People having two or more servings of milk or yogurt a day have about 17% lower same-period mortality than non-drinkers.
- Benefit: The PURE follow-up study covered 136 thousand people in 21 countries, followed on average 9.1 years, with 6,796 deaths. People eating 2+ servings of dairy a day had about 17% lower mortality risk than those eating none (hazard ratio 0.83), plausible range 95% CI 0.72-0.96, trend P=0.0052
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Dehghan M, Mente A, Rangarajan S, et al. (2018). Association of dairy intake with cardiovascular disease and mortality in 21 countries from five continents (PURE): a prospective cohort study. Lancet, 392(10161), 2288-2297. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(18)31812-9>
- Notes: The dispute: PURE includes many low- and middle-income countries, where drinking milk daily itself signals a comfortable household - that confounding is hard to strip out. Also, the lactose-intolerant should switch to yogurt or low-lactose milk rather than forcing down regular milk

### 36. No need to give up eggs - just don't eat three or four a day
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no benefit=small lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Free, no time.
- TL;DR: Each extra half egg a day raises same-period mortality about 7%. Swapping that half for egg whites, fish, poultry or nuts lowers every category of mortality risk instead. No need to quit eggs - just don't eat three or four daily.
- Benefit: An American follow-up study of 521 thousand people with 129 thousand deaths. Each extra half whole egg a day raised mortality risk about 7% (hazard ratio 1.07, 95% CI 1.06-1.08, the plausible range). Replacing that half egg with an equivalent amount of egg whites, poultry, fish, dairy or nuts and legumes lowered mortality risk overall - and cardiovascular, cancer and respiratory mortality specifically
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Zhuang P, Wu F, Mao L, et al. (2021). Egg and cholesterol consumption and mortality from cardiovascular and different causes in the United States: A population-based cohort study. PLoS Medicine, 18(2), e1003508. <https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1003508>
- Notes: Heavily disputed: other long-term studies following large groups, and pooled analyses of multiple studies, found no link between eggs and mortality. US dietary guidelines have dropped the daily cholesterol cap. This entry objects only to three or four eggs a day - one a day is nothing to fret over

### 37. If you can, take baths - don't only shower
<!-- tags: money=low time=mid will=no benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: A bit more water and electricity. Half an hour each time.
- TL;DR: Near-daily bathers have about 28% lower cardiovascular-event risk than people bathing under twice a week, and about 46% lower brain hemorrhage.
- Benefit: A Japanese study of 30 thousand people followed 19 years, with 2,097 cardiovascular events. Near-daily bathers had about 28% lower cardiovascular-event risk than those bathing no more than twice a week (hazard ratio 0.72, 95% CI 0.62-0.84, the plausible range). Coronary heart disease about 35% lower (0.65, 0.45-0.94). Stroke about 26% lower (0.74, 0.62-0.87). Brain hemorrhage about 46% lower (0.54, 0.40-0.73)
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Ukai T, Iso H, Yamagishi K, et al. (2020). Habitual tub bathing and risks of incident coronary heart disease and stroke. Heart, 106(10), 732-737. <https://doi.org/10.1136/heartjnl-2019-315752>
- Notes: The dispute: this result comes from a Japanese population with a bathtub culture - most Chinese households have no way to soak daily. Also, keep the water from getting too hot and don't soak too long. For the elderly and people with cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease, overly hot water and long soaks are dangerous instead - Japan sees large numbers of elderly people dying suddenly in bathtubs each year

### 38. Keep naps under half an hour, never past an hour - if you need one or two hours to function, get the cause checked
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no benefit=mid lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Free. Set an alarm before you lie down - almost no willpower needed.
- TL;DR: People napping under an hour show no rise in mortality or chronic-disease risk, and somewhat sharper minds. Past an hour, coronary disease risk runs about 30% higher, diabetes and obesity about 20% higher. So set an alarm and wake yourself at half an hour. And if you can't get through a day without an hour or two of napping, that's a signal to get your body checked.
- Benefit: An umbrella review - that is, a review of existing reviews - combined 16 meta-analyses covering 244 health outcomes. Its conclusion: people napping under 60 minutes showed no rise in all-cause mortality or chronic-disease risk, and the clearest cognitive improvement (SMD 0.69, 95% CI 0.37-1.00, the plausible range). Short 20-30-minute naps improved athletic performance most (SMD 0.99, 0.67-1.31). People napping over 60 minutes had about 30% higher coronary disease risk and about 20% higher diabetes and obesity risk. Another study put wrist wearables on 1,338 people over 56 to measure naps objectively, instead of relying on questionnaires. Followed up to 19 years, 926 died. Each extra hour of napping raised mortality risk about 13% (HR 1.13, 95% CI 1.04-1.23). Each extra nap per day, about 7% higher (HR 1.07, 1.02-1.13). Morning nappers ran about 30% higher than early-afternoon nappers (HR 1.30, 1.03-1.64).
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Du P, Li J, Hua Z, et al. (2026). Multiple Health Outcomes of Daytime Napping: A Comprehensive Umbrella Review. Public Health Reviews. <https://doi.org/10.3389/phrs.2026.1609013> ; Gao C, Cai R, Zheng X, et al. (2026). Objectively Measured Daytime Napping Patterns and All-Cause Mortality in Older Adults. JAMA Network Open. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.7938> ; Dashti HS et al. (2021). Genetic determinants of daytime napping and effects on cardiometabolic health. Nature Communications. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-020-20585-3>
- Notes: Disputed. Both studies above are record-only with no randomization. Long nappers are more likely to have sleep apnea, anemia, hypothyroidism, depression or other chronic illness to begin with - so how much of "long nappers die more" is caused by the naps themselves is unclear. Gene-based causal inference (Mendelian randomization, 453 thousand plus 541 thousand people) supports only one thing: more frequent napping goes with slightly higher blood pressure and waist circumference. It found no causal effect of napping on coronary disease or diabetes. So don't treat "nap less" as a way to cut mortality. The "get the cause checked" half is the author's advice, grade C: if daytime sleepiness forces long naps, first check your nighttime sleep - snoring and breathing pauses in sleep warrant a sleep-apnea workup - then blood counts and thyroid. For how a short nap refreshes, see entry 11 of chapter 3 (nap 10 minutes when afternoon drowsiness hits). For nighttime sleep duration, see entry 13 of this chapter (7 hours a night).

### 39. After a late night, catch up on sleep the very next night - don't save it for the weekend
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no benefit=mid lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Free. You want that extra hour or two anyway.
- TL;DR: Over 80 thousand people wore wrist devices logging daily sleep. People who lost sleep and didn't recover it the next night had about 15% higher same-period mortality risk than regular sleepers. Severe deprivation without recovery ran about 42% higher. The two groups that did recover showed no detectable risk increase. So after a late night, get to bed early the next evening.
- Benefit: UK Biobank, 85,618 people, average age 61.8, wrist-worn devices recording day-by-day sleep, median follow-up 8 years. The study computed each person's own sleep need, then marked "sleep-restricted nights" as those 2.5+ hours under it. The first night after a restricted stretch counted as a "recovery night" - sleeping more than one's need that night counted as recovered. People were sorted into five patterns on that basis. Versus regular sleepers, restricted-without-recovery carried about 15% higher all-cause mortality risk (HR 1.15, 95% CI 1.01-1.31, the plausible range). Cumulative restriction over 3.5 hours without recovery ran about 42% higher (HR 1.42, 1.24-1.63). The two groups that recovered showed no significant association, with restrict-then-recover at HR 1.12 (0.98-1.28). Among already-short sleepers, restriction without recovery ran about 19% higher (1.19, 1.01-1.40), severe restriction without recovery about 38% higher (1.38, 1.17-1.63). The result replicated in a separate batch of 4,586 people from an American health survey.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Li X, Zhang M, Li Z, et al. (2026). Acute sleep rebound following sleep restriction is associated with reduced mortality risk. Nature Communications. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-72461-1>
- Notes: This is a record-only study with no randomization. People who fail to recover may be busier and sicker to begin with - that layer can't be stripped cleanly. "No detectable risk increase in the two recovered groups" means no difference was found, not that recovery makes it harmless - their point estimates still sit above 1. So this entry says pay back a late night quickly, not that late nights are free. Keep two things separate. The recovery in this study means sleeping a bit more on the first night after losing sleep - not banking it for the weekend. Making "weekday deprivation, weekend catch-up" your weekly rhythm is a different thing called social jetlag, itself linked to cardiovascular disease - see entry 13 of this chapter (about 7 hours a night, fixed schedule). The fixed wake time on weekends in entry 2 of chapter 3 doesn't conflict with this entry: this entry tells you to go to bed earlier that night, not to sleep till noon the next day.

### 40. The longer you work night shifts, the higher your cardiovascular risk - transfer early if you can
<!-- tags: money=high time=mid will=yes benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: A transfer or job change may mean less pay, and the night-shift allowance goes too. Job hunting takes time, and deciding is hard. If you factor this account in while choosing a job, the cost is zero.
- TL;DR: Night-shift workers have about 13% more cardiovascular disease and about 27% more cardiovascular death than non-shift workers. And it grows with tenure: every additional 5 years of night work adds about 7% more cardiovascular disease. So this bill is counted in years - transfer early if you can. As for night shifts causing cancer, the evidence is far softer than the folklore suggests.
- Benefit: Pooling 23 follow-up studies (record-only, no randomization). Night-shift workers had about 13% higher cardiovascular-event risk (RR 1.13, 95% CI 1.10-1.16, the plausible range). Cardiovascular death about 27% higher (RR 1.27, 1.18-1.36). By years worked: each additional 5 years of night shifts raised cardiovascular disease incidence about 7% more (RR 1.07, 1.04-1.09) and cardiovascular death about 5% more (RR 1.05, 1.03-1.06). By disease: coronary heart disease incidence about 22% higher (1.22, 1.16-1.28), coronary death about 22% higher (1.22, 1.10-1.36), ischemic heart disease death about 39% higher (1.39, 1.06-1.84), stroke death about 49% higher (1.49, 1.04-2.12). Stroke incidence was not significant (1.06, 0.95-1.18).
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Xi J, Ma W, Tao Y, et al. (2025). Association between night shift work and cardiovascular disease: a systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis. Frontiers in Public Health. <https://doi.org/10.3389/fpubh.2025.1668848> ; Esposito G, Bravi F, Santucci C, et al. (2025). Night shift work and breast cancer risk in healthcare workers: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Occupational Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1093/occmed/kqaf040> ; Shen QM, Li ZY, Tan YT, et al. (2026). Night shift work and risk of total and site-specific cancer: results from a prospective cohort study among Chinese men. Scandinavian Journal of Work, Environment & Health. <https://doi.org/10.5271/sjweh.4290> ; Czeisler CA, Johnson MP, Duffy JF, et al. (1990). Exposure to bright light and darkness to treat physiologic maladaptation to night work. New England Journal of Medicine, 322(18), 1253-1259. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM199005033221801>
- Notes: These are all record-only studies with no randomization. Night-shift workers differ in smoking, weight and income to begin with, and the adjustment isn't clean. On night shifts causing cancer, the evidence is far softer than folklore. Pooling 12 studies with 12,132 breast-cancer cases, ever working nights raised risk only about 5%, with the plausible range crossing 1 (RR 1.05, 0.96-1.14). The 20-plus-years tier ran about 25% higher (1.25, 1.01-1.55), but that turned non-significant after the authors' influence analysis, and correcting for possible publication bias brought it near 1 - in their words, the association is "far from established". Another study following Chinese men 16.1 years with 8,202 cancers found no association between night shifts and total or most common cancers; only pancreatic cancer ran about 59% higher in the 11-to-20-years tier (HR 1.59, 1.09-2.31). So this entry is priced on the cardiovascular account, not cancer. It doesn't argue everyone should leave night work - it just lays out the bill in years for you to tally yourself. If you're already on nights, first fix what's fixable: quit smoking (entry 1 of this chapter), blood pressure and lipids (entry 12), and how to recover sleep after nights (entry 39). Two countermeasures are also worth trying: confining eating to daytime, and moving your body clock with bright light plus strict daytime darkness. The latter is the only method proven to actually move the clock - under ordinary indoor lighting, six straight night shifts left the clock unmoved; switching to 7000-12000 lux of bright light plus near-total daytime darkness flipped it entirely in four days. **Bright enough light at night, strict blackout for daytime sleep, sunglasses blocking morning light on the way home - all three are required**; skip the last and the whole effort is wasted. Both countermeasures' endpoints reach only metabolic markers and sleep - no trial has tested whether doing them lowers cardiovascular risk. Go back to a daytime life on rest days and the clock gets knocked back again. Doses, raw numbers, the remaining sources, and how the body tells time are all in [docs/body-clock-and-night-shifts.md](../docs/body-clock-and-night-shifts.md).
