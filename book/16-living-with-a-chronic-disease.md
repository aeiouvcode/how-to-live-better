# 16. Living with a chronic disease

Lens: all-cause mortality and money. A chronic-disease diagnosis isn't the end. How the following decades get managed is the real account.

### 1. Take the medication the full prescribed course - don't stop because you feel fine
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=yes benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Free - it actually saves the later cost of treating complications. Daily pills take minutes. The hard part is taking them without missing a day.
- TL;DR: People who take their medication as prescribed are about half as likely to die in the same period as sporadic takers. Discount that number: even people who faithfully took a placebo died less, so part of the benefit comes from "people who can stick to medication mind their health more anyway". But the harm of stopping is certain - blood-pressure, anticoagulant and anti-rejection drugs above all.
- Benefit: Pooling 21 studies and 46,847 people (meta-analysis - many studies computed together): versus sporadic takers, adherent patients were about 44% less likely to die in the same period. The raw figure is odds ratio 0.56, 95% CI 0.50-0.63 - the range is the number's credible span. Looking only at treatments with proven efficacy: about 45% less (0.55, 0.49-0.62)
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Simpson SH, Eurich DT, Majumdar SR, et al. (2006). A meta-analysis of the association between adherence to drug therapy and mortality. BMJ, 333(7557), 15. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.38875.675486.55>
- Notes: Disputed. This is the number in this book most in need of discounting. In the same pooled analysis, even faithful placebo takers died less (0.56, 0.43-0.74) - a large share of the benefit comes from "people who can stick to medication mind their health more anyway", which the authors call the healthy-adherer effect. But the harm of stopping is definite - above all for blood-pressure, anticoagulant and anti-rejection drugs.

### 2. Get the outpatient chronic/special-disease recognition first, then the cross-region filing - hypertension, diabetes, chemo/radiotherapy, dialysis and anti-rejection can then settle directly away from home
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no benefit=big lens=money -->
- Cost: Free. Do the cross-region care filing once in the medical-insurance app before traveling - done on the phone.
- TL;DR: Hypertension, diabetes, outpatient chemo/radiotherapy for malignant tumors, uremia dialysis, and post-transplant anti-rejection: these five can be swiped directly at outpatient visits in other provinces - no paying up front and claiming reimbursement back home. But you need the chronic/special-disease recognition in your insured place first, then the cross-region filing. Both done, or neither works.
- Benefit: The National Healthcare Security Administration's document says this step is achieved, verbatim: "basically achieved county-level availability of cross-province direct settlement for five outpatient chronic/special diseases - hypertension, diabetes, outpatient chemo/radiotherapy for malignant tumors, uremia dialysis, and post-transplant anti-rejection treatment". "County-level availability" means doable at county level. The document adds 5 more diseases on top of those 5
- Evidence: A
- Sources: NHSA General Office and Ministry of Finance General Office (2024). Notice on steadily expanding the disease scope for cross-province direct settlement of outpatient chronic/special diseases (Yi Bao Ban Fa [2024] No. 19). <https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/202409/content_6974467.htm>
- Notes: The precondition is doing both: the chronic/special-disease recognition in the insured place, then the cross-region filing. How far the 5 new diseases have rolled out differs by place - follow your local medical-insurance department's publications.

### 3. Do rechecks at the intervals the doctor gives, and record every result in the same notebook
<!-- tags: money=low time=low will=some benefit=mid lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Tens to hundreds of yuan per check. A hospital trip plus waiting for results takes some time. The hard part: going every interval, easy to keep postponing.
- TL;DR: A chronic disease is read by change over time, not one reading. Record date, values, current medication and dose in the same notebook. Switching hospitals, bring it out - no redoing the checks, no re-spending the money.
- Benefit: What a chronic disease needs is not one reading but how the readings move over time. Recording in one place has a second benefit: switching hospitals without repeated checks or repeated spending
- Evidence: C
- Sources: Author's experience; no direct literature
- Notes: Record at least four things each time: date, values, current medication, dose. Photographing lab sheets on the phone works too. The key is one place - not sheets scattered everywhere.

### 4. Don't stop proper treatment to try folk remedies or supplements
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=yes benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Free, and it saves supplement money. No time. The hard part is holding out when people keep urging you to try.
- TL;DR: The harm of stopping is certain; the benefit of folk remedies and supplements is uncertain. That's trading a certain loss for an uncertain gain. Wanting to try a supplement: tell your treating doctor first. Some supplements change blood levels of anticoagulants and anti-rejection drugs.
- Benefit: The harm of stopping is definite, the benefit of folk remedies uncertain. Chapter 6 of this book lists a batch of common ineffective categories
- Evidence: C
- Sources: Author's experience; no direct literature. Related evidence: chapter 6
- Notes: Wanting to try a supplement: tell the treating doctor first. Some interact with prescription drugs - for instance, changing blood levels of anticoagulants and anti-rejection drugs.

### 5. With a stable chronic disease, a community clinic can prescribe up to 12 weeks of medication at once
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no benefit=mid lens=time -->
- Cost: Free. Ask at the community health-service center whether long-term prescriptions are available - one sentence.
- TL;DR: With a stable condition and unchanged medication, a community health-service center can prescribe up to 12 weeks at once - saving the half day of monthly trips to queue at the big hospital. In the same insured area, primary hospitals' reimbursement rate usually runs about ten points above the next level up.
- Benefit: No monthly big-hospital trips to queue for refills. In the same insured area (also called the pooling region), primary hospitals' inpatient reimbursement rate in principle runs about 10 percentage points above the next level up
- Evidence: A
- Sources: State Council General Office (2026). Measures for accelerating the tiered diagnosis-and-treatment system. <https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/202604/content_7065031.htm>: "for eligible chronic-disease patients, primary medical institutions may write long-term prescriptions of up to 12 weeks per visit"; same document, "in principle, inpatient reimbursement rates within a pooling region step down by about 10 percentage points per level", and it requires higher-level hospitals to run hypertension, diabetes, COPD and other common chronic-disease clinics at the primary level
- Notes: "Eligible" is the doctor's call - generally people with a definite diagnosis and a stable regimen. The medication is the same medication; what's saved is the monthly half-day trip and registration fee. If the primary site lacks your drug, ask about shortage registration and delivery.

### 6. Before signing up with a family doctor at the community clinic, ask which parts go through insurance and which are self-paid
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no benefit=mid lens=money -->
- Cost: Free. One extra question before signing - a minute.
- TL;DR: The signed basic service package goes through insurance by rule. A personalized service package is paid entirely out of pocket. When someone sells you an "upgrade package", first ask whether it's the basic or the personalized package, whether it's on file, and how much per year.
- Benefit: The basic service package is paid by insurance per the rules. A personalized package is entirely out of pocket, and such packages must be filed with the county health department
- Evidence: A
- Sources: State Council General Office (2026). Measures for accelerating the tiered diagnosis-and-treatment system. <https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/202604/content_7065031.htm>: "link primary outpatient payment with contracted-service policy: basic service packages are included in medical-insurance payment as prescribed; personalized packages are filed by the contracted primary medical institution with the county health department through procedure, with fees paid by the individual"
- Notes: The value of signing is someone following your condition long-term - not how many items are bundled. When someone sells an "upgrade package", ask three things first: basic or personalized, filed or not, how much per year.

### 7. Diagnosed with diabetes: get a fundus exam once, then at the doctor's intervals; check the feet once a year
<!-- tags: money=low time=low will=some benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: A dilated fundus exam runs tens to hundreds of yuan. The foot check usually comes with the outpatient visit at no extra cost. The hard part is remembering to go when neither eyes nor feet feel wrong.
- TL;DR: Diabetes damaging the fundus and the feet gives almost no sensation before it goes too far - before sight is lost or ulcers form. Caught early, both are treatable. Type 2: a dilated fundus exam at diagnosis; type 1: starting 5 years after onset. After one or two clear exams with blood sugar at target, it can relax to every year or two. Feet: once a year.
- Benefit: Diabetes damaging the fundus is retinopathy; damaging the feet is diabetic foot. Both give almost no sensation before trouble. Caught early, both are treatable. Waiting until sight goes or the foot ulcerates is too late
- Evidence: A
- Sources: American Diabetes Association (2026). Standards of Care in Diabetes 2026, chapter 12, retinopathy, neuropathy and foot care. Diabetes Care: "People with type 2 diabetes should have an initial dilated and comprehensive eye examination by an ophthalmologist or optometrist at the time of the diabetes diagnosis."; "Adults with type 1 diabetes should have an initial dilated and comprehensive eye examination by an ophthalmologist or optometrist 5 years after the onset of diabetes."; "If there is no evidence of retinopathy from one or more annual eye exams and glycemic indicators are within the goal range, then screening every 1-2 years may be considered."; "Perform a comprehensive foot evaluation at least annually to identify risk factors for ulcers and amputations." <https://doi.org/10.2337/dc26-S012>
- Notes: Type 2 gets checked at diagnosis; type 1 only 5 years after onset - because type 2 has usually run silently high for years already. After one or two clear exams with blood sugar at target, it can relax to every 1-2 years. People whose feet have gone numb: look at your soles yourself daily - a mirror works.

### 8. After a kidney stone: drink up to 2.5-3 liters of water a day, and keep salt under 6 grams
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Free. Make drinking throughout the day a habit. High-sweat days need more still - the hard part is remembering daily.
- TL;DR: Of people who've had one stone, about a quarter grow another within five years. 199 people fresh from a first calcium stone were split into two groups; one did just one thing - drink enough water. Five-year recurrence: 12% in that group, 27% in the do-nothing group. The UK guideline's amounts: 2.5 to 3 liters of water a day, salt under 6 grams.
- Benefit: A 5-year randomized controlled trial. 199 first-time calcium-stone patients with no identifiable cause (idiopathic calcium stones) were randomized: one group drank at least 2 liters of water daily, the other got no intervention. Five-year recurrence: 12/99 (12.1%) in the water group versus 27/100 (27.0%) in the control, P=0.008 - the smaller P, the less likely the difference is chance. Mean time to recurrence: 38.7±13.2 versus 25.1±16.4 months, P=0.016. Baseline 24-hour urine volume was markedly lower in stone patients than in stone-free people: men 1,057±238 mL versus 1,401±562 (P<0.0001); women 990±230 versus 1,239±440 (P<0.001). NICE guideline NG118 recommendation 1.8.1: adults drink 2.5-3 liters daily, children and teens 1-2 liters by age; add fresh lemon juice to the water, skip carbonated drinks; adults keep salt under 6 grams a day
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Borghi L, Meschi T, Amato F, Briganti A, Novarini A, Giannini A (1996). Urinary volume, water and recurrences in idiopathic calcium nephrolithiasis: a 5-year randomized prospective study. The Journal of Urology, 155(3), 839-843. <https://doi.org/10.1016/s0022-5347(01)66321-3> ; National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (2019). Renal and ureteric stones: assessment and management. NICE guideline NG118, recommendation 1.8.1. <https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng118/chapter/Recommendations>
- Notes: Recurrence down about 55% at zero cost - by this book's ordering rule, the best-value entry in this chapter. What to watch is 24-hour urine volume - target 2 to 2.5 liters and up, not how much went in. Heavy sweaters and people working in heat must drink more. The trial covered first-time idiopathic calcium-stone patients. Stones come in more than one composition - uric-acid, infection and cystine stones each have their own countermeasures. Recurrent formers deserve a stone-composition analysis and 24-hour urine workup before deciding on medication. Don't cut out calcium to prevent stones - entry 21 of chapter 6. An attack with severe waist-abdominal colic means the hospital, not flushing it down with water. Painless blood in urine is a different workup - entry 27 of chapter 1.

### 9. Diagnosed with gout: take urate-lowering medication long-term, hold blood uric acid under 360 µmol/L and keep it there
<!-- tags: money=low time=low will=yes benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Allopurinol runs a few to tens of yuan a month. Early on, blood uric acid is checked every few weeks to tune the dose; once stable, every few months. For the first 3 to 6 months, an anti-attack preventive goes alongside. The hard part is keeping it up when nothing hurts.
- TL;DR: The right way to fight gout is long-term urate-lowering medication holding uric acid under 360 µmol/L - not painkillers only when it flares. In a UK trial of 517 people, the dose-to-target group hit target in 95% at two years versus 30% under usual care. Hitting target doesn't mean stopping either. Diet alone can't reach the line - even quitting alcohol lowers it only 1.6 mg/dL.
- Benefit: A randomized controlled trial of 517 adults who'd had a flare in the past 12 months. One group got nurse-led care: the disease explained, then uric acid titrated to target. The other saw their GP as usual. At 2 years, blood uric acid under 360 µmol/L (6 mg/dL): 95% versus 30%. The likelihood of target was 3.18 times usual care (RR 3.18, 95% CI 2.42-4.18 - the number's credible range), P<0.0001. Second-year flare frequency, tophi and quality of life - all secondary measures - also beat usual care. Each quality-adjusted life year gained cost £5,066. The same trial ran a follow-up: 438 surveyed, 82% returned. Both groups self-reported the past year's flares: the target group's median was 0 (the middle half fell in 0-0), usual care 1 (0-3), P<0.001 - that parenthesized span is the interquartile range. The share still on urate-lowering medication was 1.19 times higher in the target group (adjusted RR 1.19, 1.09-1.30). The American College of Rheumatology's 2020 gout guideline strongly recommends starting urate-lowering therapy in three kinds of patients: those with subcutaneous tophi; those with imaging showing gout-eroded bone damage; and those flaring ≥2 times a year. The method: monitor blood uric acid continuously, titrate the dose to target - goal under 6 mg/dL. First choice is allopurinol, including for stage-3+ chronic kidney disease, starting at ≤100 mg/day. At initiation, add anti-inflammatory prophylaxis for at least 3 to 6 months. The same guideline cites a case series: patients well controlled long-term who stopped, with uric acid still under 7 mg/dL after stopping - only 13% (27/211) stayed flare-free over 5 years of tracking. How much diet can do, in numbers: limiting or quitting alcohol leaves blood uric acid 1.6 mg/dL lower than not limiting. One serving of beer raises it 0.16 mg/dL. Healthy, Mediterranean and DASH diets do less still
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Doherty M, Jenkins W, Richardson H, et al. (2018). Efficacy and cost-effectiveness of nurse-led care involving education and engagement of patients and a treat-to-target urate-lowering strategy versus usual care for gout: a randomised controlled trial. Lancet, 392(10156), 1403-1412. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(18)32158-5> ; Abhishek A, Jenkins W, La-Crette J, Fernandes G, Doherty M (2020). Nurse-led care is preferred over GP-led care of gout and improves gout outcomes: results of Nottingham Gout Treatment Trial follow-up study. Rheumatology, 59(3), 575-579. <https://doi.org/10.1093/rheumatology/kez333> ; FitzGerald JD, Dalbeth N, Mikuls T, et al. (2020). 2020 American College of Rheumatology Guideline for the Management of Gout. Arthritis Care & Research, 72(6), 744-760. <https://doi.org/10.1002/acr.24180>
- Notes: **Han Chinese deserve an HLA-B*5801 test before allopurinol**. Carriers run 7.4% in Han, Korean and Thai populations, versus 0.7% in whites and Hispanics. Asians and Blacks on allopurinol face 3 times the whites' risk of allopurinol hypersensitivity syndrome - severe cases slough skin over large areas and can kill. The ACR 2020 guideline says these two groups may reasonably be tested before use. **Starting urate-lowering actually triggers flares more easily at first** - which is why the guideline requires an anti-inflammatory preventive alongside for the first 3 to 6 months. "The urate drug made it hurt more, so I stopped" is this thing's most common failure mode. What to do during a flare is adding anti-inflammatories per the doctor - not stopping the urate drug. Targets are written differently across sources: under 6 mg/dL is about 360 µmol/L; Chinese lab sheets mostly use µmol/L. High uric acid found on a physical with never a flare is another matter - entry 19 of chapter 6. Sugary drinks and alcohol: entries 7 and 20 of chapter 2 (no sugary drinks; less or no alcohol).
