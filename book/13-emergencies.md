# 13. Emergencies: what to do first

This chapter answers one thing only: when something happens, what to do first, what next, and what not to do. The first-aid and wilderness entries count specific causes of death and survival rates. The threat and scam entries count money and personal freedom. The two kinds of numbers are not interconverted. Ordering: "how common this is x how many lives right versus wrong moves apart", high to low. These entries are first for you, your spouse and your immediate family: 79.2% of cardiac arrests happen at home, so the person you'd most likely save is family. Friends and colleagues come next. For strangers there is still some benefit, but that tier is lowest and carries the side of being extorted or dragged into a case - so these entries put the self-protective moves and the liability exemptions up front.

This chapter holds only actions done within minutes. Recognizing a scam, or being blackmailed over compromising material, is handled over days - not here. Anti-fraud hard rules and the seven high-incidence scams: entry 3 of chapter 8 (anti-fraud hard rules). Being extorted over intimate photos or recorded nude chats: entry 32 of chapter 8 (being blackmailed over leverage). Money already transferred: call 110 or 96110 at once to stop payment - entry 2 of chapter 8 (stopping payment).

### 1. Someone collapsed and not breathing: press hard on the chest at once; have a bystander call 120 and find an AED
<!-- tags: money=0 time=mid will=no benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Free. A Red Cross or hospital CPR course runs half a day to a day - one course lasts years. Untrained works too: interlock both hands at the midpoint of the line between the nipples, press hard about 2 times a second, don't stop.
- TL;DR: The person you'd most likely press on is family: 79.2% of cardiac arrests happen at home. With bystander compressions, the share leaving hospital alive rises from 3.9% to 16.1%. No response to shouting, no breathing - press. The spot is the midpoint between the nipples; both hands stacked, press hard, 2 times a second, nonstop. Have a second person call 120 and fetch the AED; your hands don't stop.
- Benefit: 79 studies and 142,740 people pooled (meta-analysis): of people whose hearts stop outside hospital (out-of-hospital cardiac arrest), only 7.6% leave hospital alive in total. With bystander CPR, the share leaving alive rises from 3.9% to 16.1%. Someone nearby witnessed the collapse in 53%; only 32% actually got bystander compressions. China's BASIC-OHCA registry recorded 38,227 EMS-attended cases; 30,282 (79.2%) arrested at home. Among them, the bystander-compression rate was 20.3%, and 1.2% left hospital alive. The order: press first, then have a second person call and fetch the AED. Once the machine is on, follow its voice prompts
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Sasson C, et al. (2010). Predictors of survival from out-of-hospital cardiac arrest: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Circulation: Cardiovascular Quality and Outcomes. <https://doi.org/10.1161/CIRCOUTCOMES.109.889576> ; Zheng J, et al. (2023). Incidence, process of care, and outcomes of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest in China: a prospective study of the BASIC-OHCA registry. The Lancet Public Health. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-2667(23)00173-1> ; National People's Congress (2020). Civil Code (Article 184). <https://www.spp.gov.cn/spp/fl/202006/t20200602_463888.shtml>
- Notes: Don't pinch the philtrum first, don't look for family first, don't film first. And don't hold back force for fear of breaking ribs. Civil Code Article 184: voluntarily giving emergency rescue and injuring the person rescued carries no civil liability.

### 2. An elderly person fallen, or someone on the ground: squat, call to them, dial 120 - don't rush to lift them; with strangers, walking away is legal too, and if you stop, don't move them by hand
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Free. Squat down, ask a question, make a call - minutes. The hard part is resisting the reach to help them up. With strangers there's one more account: calling 120 leaves your number - you may be called back or questioned once. If the family really fixes on you, a lawsuit is months at minimum, and even winning means paying your own lawyer.
- TL;DR: This entry's main line is your own elders and familiar people. Check breathing first; none, compress the chest per entry 1 (press hard on the chest). Breathing present, still don't pick them up: they may have fallen because of a stroke or faint first, and forcing them up can worsen it. Squat, call them, ask whether they remember how they fell. Lower-back pain or a suspected fracture - never move them; call 120 on the spot.
- Benefit: The health ministry's Technical Guide for Fall Intervention in the Elderly writes two sentences. One: "falls are the leading cause of injury death among Chinese over 65". The other: "on finding an elderly person fallen, do not rush to lift them; handle by situation". Unconscious: call emergency at once. Vomiting: turn the head to one side and clear the mouth and nose. Breathing and heartbeat stopped: start chest compressions at once. "If movement is needed, keep it steady and as flat as possible". Conscious: first ask whether they remember the fall - "if they cannot recall, it may be syncope or a cerebrovascular accident". Severe headache, or a crooked mouth, slurred speech, weak limbs: "immediately lifting the elderly person may worsen cerebral hemorrhage or ischemia and aggravate the condition". Signs of fracture, or lower-back pain with incontinence: "without relevant expertise, do not move them casually". All of these mean calling emergency at once. An elder wanting to stand on their own may be helped up slowly - "rest sitting or lying, observe, and leave only after confirming all is well". On "fear of being extorted", the legal side reads: Article 90 of the SPC's interpretation on applying the Civil Procedure Law has two sentences. First: "a party shall provide evidence to prove the facts on which its claim rests". Second: "where a party fails to provide evidence, or the evidence cannot prove its factual claim, before judgment, the party bearing the burden of proof bears the unfavorable consequences". Civil Code Article 1198, verbatim: "operators and managers of business and public premises such as hotels, malls, banks, stations, airports, stadiums and entertainment venues, and organizers of mass activities, who fail their safety-assurance duty and thereby harm others, bear tort liability". Civil Code Article 184: "one who voluntarily performs emergency rescue and harms the person rescued bears no civil liability". The SPC published a typical case: a pharmacy operator gave CPR to a person in cardiac arrest and broke their ribs; the rescued person spent 18 days in hospital and claimed 9,000+ yuan; the court held the rescuer bears no civil liability
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Ministry of Health (2011). Technical guide for fall intervention in the elderly ("handling after a fall"). Full PDF via Guangdong CDC <http://cdcp.gd.gov.cn/gcdcdfiles/editor/uploadfile/20111027094832117.pdf>; the on-site-handling section in full via Guinan County government <https://www.guinan.gov.cn/lnb/zc1__zwgk/zc/aztfl/qt/content_27609778> ; National People's Congress (2020). Civil Code (Articles 183, 184). <https://www.spp.gov.cn/spp/fl/202006/t20200602_463888.shtml> ; Supreme People's Court (2023). Typical people's-court cases practicing core socialist values through fairness and efficiency (case 13, Qi v. Sun health-right dispute, Kangping County People's Court, Liaoning). <https://www.court.gov.cn/zixun/xiangqing/408162.html> ; Supreme People's Court (2022, second amendment, effective April 10, 2022). Interpretation on applying the Civil Procedure Law (Article 90). <https://www.court.gov.cn/zixun/xiangqing/353651.html> ; Civil Code Article 1198, same SPP-reposted full text as above ; NPC Standing Committee (2023 amendment, effective January 1, 2024). Civil Procedure Law (Articles 152, 164). Full text via Shanghai Development and Reform Commission <https://fgw.sh.gov.cn/ys-syjf-zc-2.4.1-h5/20240408/073febdb2b04464390546232d4089f51.html> ; State Council (2006). Measures for paying litigation fees (State Council Order No. 481, Articles 6, 29). <https://www.gov.cn/ziliao/flfg/2006-12/29/content_483682.htm> ; NPC Standing Committee (2025). Public Security Administration Punishments Law (2025 revision, effective January 1, 2026, Articles 26(1), 50(5)). <https://www.spp.gov.cn/spp/fl/202506/t20250627_699863.shtml>
- Notes: B because the guide says only how to handle it, with no "how many fewer die if followed" numbers. Article 184 exempts damage caused in the course of rescue, given voluntariness and urgency - it doesn't cover "stuffing the person into a private car to the hospital on your own call"; waiting for 120 is steadier. After dialing 120, don't hang up - do as the dispatcher says. This judgment is first for your own elders and familiar people. For a stranger on the road, walking away is legal and cheapest: the law doesn't obligate you to rescue. Stopping has an account paid layer by layer: time, leaving your number, the family seeking you out, being sued, it reaching your workplace, being filmed and posted online. Details: [docs/should-you-stop-for-a-stranger.md](../docs/should-you-stop-for-a-stranger.md). If you do decide to stop, the one move with no second answer is **don't move them by hand** - a medical reason, not legal: forcing them up can worsen cerebral hemorrhage or spinal injury. One exception: if you operate or manage this place, or organized the activity, walking away stops being free - Civil Code Article 1198's safety-assurance duty says you owe the people in your venue a share of safety responsibility. Fall prevention itself: entry 13 of chapter 1 (train balance and leg strength). Delayed symptoms after an elder bumps their head: entry 10 of this chapter. Money after being injured while rescuing: entry 39 of this chapter. Preserving evidence and reporting online mobbing: entry 16 of chapter 8.

### 3. Sudden crooked mouth, one arm weak, speech unclear - call 120 at once; don't wait, don't drive yourself
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Free. Three moves, learned at a glance: have them smile, raise both hands level, say one full sentence.
- TL;DR: Crooked mouth, one arm won't rise, speech unclear - call 120 at once. Clot-dissolving drugs within 3 hours of onset make recovery to basic self-care about 1.75 times as likely as without; at 3 to 4.5 hours only 1.26; past 4.5 hours the difference is invisible. Note the exact time symptoms began. No water or medicine, no driving yourself.
- Benefit: Individual data from 16 intravenous-thrombolysis randomized trials and 6,756 patients pooled (meta-analysis). Intravenous thrombolysis means drugs that reopen the blocked vessel. Alteplase within 3 hours of onset: odds ratio 1.75 for recovering to basic self-care - modified Rankin score 0-1 - 95% CI 1.35-2.27. That is, this outcome is about 1.75 times as likely as without thrombolysis. At 3 to 4.5 hours: 1.26 (1.05-1.51). Past 4.5 hours: 1.15 (0.95-1.40), statistically indistinguishable
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Emberson J, Lees KR, Lyden P, et al. (2014). Effect of treatment delay, age, and stroke severity on the effects of intravenous thrombolysis with alteplase for acute ischaemic stroke: a meta-analysis of individual patient data from randomised trials. Lancet, 384(9958), 1929-1935. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(14)60584-5>
- Notes: Thrombolysis means drugs reopening the blocked vessel - the earlier the more effective, and past 4.5 hours it's basically useless. Call 120 rather than drive because the ambulance goes straight to a hospital with a stroke center and notifies it to prepare. Note the exact onset time - the doctor needs it to decide on thrombolysis. No water or medicine. Don't "rest and see".

### 4. Sudden spinning dizziness with unsteady standing, double vision, one eye going black, or a finger that can't touch your own nose - treat it as stroke and call 120 too
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Free. Add two moves to the "face, arm, speech" three (entry 3: sudden crooked mouth, unclear speech): have them stand straight with eyes closed and watch for sway, and ask about double vision or a missing patch; when doubting yourself, touch your nose with a finger, then again with eyes closed.
- TL;DR: Of 736 strokes, 14.1% began with neither a crooked mouth nor a weak arm - looking only at "face, arm, speech" misses them. Counting "unsteady standing, double vision or a missing patch" drops the missed share to 4.4%. Sudden spinning that won't let you stand, double vision, a finger missing your own nose - call 120 the same, note the time the same.
- Benefit: The University of Kentucky stroke center in 2014 reviewed records of 736 acute ischemic strokes (strokes from blocked vessels). 14.1% had none of the three FAST symptoms at onset - face droop, arm weakness, speech trouble. Adding "unsteady walking, leg weakness, visual symptoms" to screening cut the missed share from 14.1% to 4.4%
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Aroor S, Singh R, Goldstein LB (2017). BE-FAST (Balance, Eyes, Face, Arm, Speech, Time): Reducing the Proportion of Strokes Missed Using the FAST Mnemonic. Stroke, 48(2), 479-481. <https://doi.org/10.1161/STROKEAHA.116.015169>
- Notes: Cerebellar and brainstem infarcts (posterior-circulation infarcts) - blocked vessels in that territory - often present without crooked mouth or weak arm: just spinning dizziness, veering when walking, double vision, choking on water or food - most easily misread as cervical spondylosis, low blood sugar or BPPV and delayed. A finger missing the nose and failing to stand with eyes closed point to cerebellar involvement. But no move can rule a stroke out: sudden onset that doesn't ease - call 120 and note the exact time. The study reviewed one hospital's records; the authors themselves wrote it needs further validation.

### 5. One eye suddenly goes black like a curtain dropping - even if it clears in minutes, go to emergency as a stroke case the same day
<!-- tags: money=0 time=mid will=no benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Free. The emergency evaluation and vessel checks take half a day to a day.
- TL;DR: One eye going black like a dropped curtain is a blocked vessel in the eye's fundus. The longer the blockage, the less retina can be saved. It is also a form of brain ischemia - the following days are the high-stroke window. Switching these patients to same-day evaluation and same-day treatment cut 90-day recurrent stroke from 10.3% to 2.1%. Go the same day to an emergency department with a stroke center - don't wait to book ophthalmology tomorrow.
- Benefit: The American Heart Association's 2021 scientific statement on central retinal artery occlusion (the fundus's main vessel blocked) writes: "acute central retinal artery occlusion is an emergency" - care systems should recognize and triage it as one; the longer the blood is cut, the less retina can be saved. The Oxford EXPRESS study looked at two kinds of patients: brain ischemia that resolved on its own (medically, transient ischemic attack) and minor stroke. After switching these patients to same-day evaluation and same-day treatment, recurrent stroke fell - the 90-day share from 10.3% (32/310) to 2.1% (6/281)
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Mac Grory B, Schrag M, Biousse V, et al. (2021). Management of Central Retinal Artery Occlusion: A Scientific Statement From the American Heart Association. Stroke, 52(6), e282-e294. <https://doi.org/10.1161/STR.0000000000000366> ; Rothwell PM, Giles MF, Chandratheva A, et al. (2007). Effect of urgent treatment of transient ischaemic attack and minor stroke on early recurrent stroke (EXPRESS study): a prospective population-based sequential comparison. Lancet, 370(9596), 1432-1442. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(07)61448-2>
- Notes: B because EXPRESS wasn't randomized - it compares the same population before and after the change in practice, and other conditions may also have shifted between the periods; that part can't be separated. A black-out for minutes that clears on its own is most easily written off as "tired" or "low blood sugar". It is a form of transient ischemic attack - the following days are the high-stroke window. Painless, non-red, non-tearing one-eye vision loss especially deserves attention. Don't wait to book ophthalmology tomorrow - go straight to an emergency department with a stroke center.

### 6. One eye swollen and painful, red, seeing rainbow rings around lights, with headache, nausea and vomiting - ophthalmic emergency the same day
<!-- tags: money=0 time=mid will=no benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Free. The ophthalmic emergency measures eye pressure and checks the anterior chamber - half a day.
- TL;DR: This is an acute glaucoma attack. Aqueous fluid can't drain and eye pressure spikes. The longer the delay, the more the optic nerve is crushed - and damage doesn't come back. During Wuhan's lockdown, patients waited 241 hours on average for treatment and 21.87% went blind; the same period a year later without lockdown, 121 hours average and 7.84% blind.
- Benefit: A Wuhan eye hospital compared acute primary angle-closure glaucoma patients across two periods: 54 patients and 64 eyes in the 76 lockdown days of 2020, 46 patients and 51 eyes in the same period of 2021, with similar demographics. Symptom onset to treatment: 241.84±211.95 hours in the lockdown group versus 121.53±96.12 in the control (P=0.001). Eye pressure at presentation: 52.63±12.45 mmHg versus 45.16±9.79 (P=0.001). Blindness: 21.87% versus 7.84%. Glaucomatous optic neuropathy found: 20/64 eyes (31.25%) versus 7/51 (13.73%) (P=0.03). A Korean hospital reviewed 50 eyes that had one attack and later had the lens removed: a year on, 25 eyes (50%) had varying visual-field defects - the longer from symptoms to pressure control, the more likely the defect (P=0.005). Another prospective study had 43 shallow-anterior-chamber people lie prone in a dark room for an hour: both eyes' pressure rose significantly (P<0.01) - the dominant eye by a median 3.60 mmHg, the other 2.70 (P<0.05)
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Zhou L, Wu S, Wang Y, Bao X, Peng T, Luo W, Ortega-Usobiaga J (2022). Clinical presentation of acute primary angle closure during the COVID-19 epidemic lockdown. Frontiers in Medicine, 9, 1078237. <https://doi.org/10.3389/fmed.2022.1078237> ; Sung MS, Kim HJ, Park SW (2023). Predictors of long-term visual field outcome after an episode of acute primary angle closure. Clinical & Experimental Ophthalmology, 51(4), 291-299. <https://doi.org/10.1111/ceo.14206> ; Wang J, Wang J, Ng TK, Huang C (2025). Asymmetric intraocular pressure changes in dominant and contralateral eyes: the dark room prone provocative test. Seminars in Ophthalmology, 40(4), 325-331. <https://doi.org/10.1080/08820538.2024.2443972>
- Notes: B for two reasons: the Wuhan groups were compared from old records, and lockdown brought other differences inseparable from "delay"; the Korean series was only 50 eyes, also retrospective. This entry does not say screens cause glaucoma. Pupils dilate in the dark, and long periods looking down or lying prone push the lens forward - together, they can trigger an attack in people whose anterior chamber is already shallow. The genuinely high-risk are people over 50, farsighted, with family history of angle-closure glaucoma. Young people gaming all night basically don't take this road. The most common misread during an attack is gastroenteritis, migraine or high blood pressure, because headache and vomiting outshine the eye symptoms. Painless non-red one-eye vision loss is a different thing - entry 5 (one eye suddenly going black). Whether blue-light glasses help: entry 16 of chapter 6 (blue-light glasses).

### 7. Pressing chest pain, tightness or squeezing lasting over 15 minutes without easing - call 120; don't tough it out, don't drive yourself
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Free.
- TL;DR: Pressing chest pain, tightness or squeezing over 15 minutes without easing - call 120. Those whose vessel is opened within 55 minutes of reaching hospital: 2.8% dead within 30 days. Over 55 minutes: 9.7%. Every stretch of delay kills another patch of heart muscle. Don't drive yourself - losing consciousness on the road is a second accident.
- Benefit: Israel ran a national acute-coronary-syndrome survey. The analysis covered ST-elevation myocardial infarction patients - one kind of heart attack - and measured the interval from hospital arrival to opening the blocked vessel with a balloon (balloon dilation). Interval ≤55 minutes: 30-day death 5/178 (2.8%). Interval >55 minutes: 15/155 (9.7%)
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Karkabi B, Meir G, Zafrir B, et al. (2021). Door-to-balloon time and mortality in patients with ST-elevation myocardial infarction. European Heart Journal - Quality of Care and Clinical Outcomes. <https://doi.org/10.1093/ehjqcco/qcaa037>
- Notes: That study compared delay after reaching hospital, not onset-to-hospital. But the logic is the same: every stretch of delay kills another patch of heart muscle. The pain can sit in the upper belly, back or jaw, or be numbness down the left arm. Diabetics and the elderly may feel no pain - just breathlessness. Don't drive yourself; losing consciousness on the road is a second accident.

### 8. Sudden tearing pain migrating from chest to back to lower back - call 120 and say clearly "the pain is moving"
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Free.
- TL;DR: Tearing pain moving from chest to back to waist - when calling 120, say directly "like tearing, the pain is moving down". Without surgery, on medication alone, this disease kills 58%. Its handling is the opposite of a heart attack: only when the doctor thinks of it do they order a contrast CT instead of giving you anticoagulation and clot-busters. A large blood-pressure difference between the two arms, or an unpalpable pulse on one side - say those too.
- Benefit: The International Registry of Acute Aortic Dissection (IRAD) collected 464 cases from 12 centers. Aortic dissection is the tearing chest-back pain disease. The most common first symptom patients report is sudden severe sharp pain. Overall in-hospital mortality was 27.4%. Type A dissection without surgery, on medication alone: 58% mortality
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Hagan PG, Nienaber CA, Isselbacher EM, et al. (2000). The International Registry of Acute Aortic Dissection (IRAD): new insights into an old disease. JAMA, 283(7), 897-903. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.283.7.897>
- Notes: Dissection and heart attack are handled in exactly opposite ways. A heart attack gets anticoagulation and thrombolysis - drugs keeping blood from clotting; those drugs on a dissection mean bleeding out the tear. So at hospital, volunteer "tearing pain, moving downward" so the doctor thinks of a contrast CT. A large pressure gap between arms or a missing pulse on one side are clues. Poorly controlled blood pressure, Marfan syndrome, and family history of aortic aneurysm or dissection raise the risk. Same rules: don't drive yourself, don't endure it until tomorrow.

### 9. A sudden "worst headache of my life" peaking within an hour - go to emergency at once for a head CT
<!-- tags: money=low time=mid will=no benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: The emergency visit itself costs little. A head CT runs a few hundred yuan.
- TL;DR: What matters isn't how bad it hurts but how fast it peaks. Of 2,131 people whose severe headache peaked within an hour, 6.2% had a burst vessel on the brain's surface. Over 40, neck pain or stiffness, someone saw you pass out, onset during activity, instant explosion, or limited neck flexion - any one means emergency for a head CT. The vast majority checked turn out fine; missing one rebleed is death.
- Benefit: Ten Canadian emergency departments ran a prospective cohort - standards set in advance, then tracked - of 2,131 "severe headaches peaking within an hour". 132 (6.2%) were subarachnoid hemorrhage - a burst vessel on the brain's surface. The Ottawa SAH rule has six items: age 40+, neck pain or stiffness, witnessed loss of consciousness, onset during activity, thunderclap onset, limited neck flexion on exam. Any one means workup. The rule caught every bleeder - sensitivity 100%, 95% CI 97.2%-100.0% - at the cost of ruling out only 15.3% of non-bleeders (specificity 15.3%)
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Perry JJ, Stiell IG, Sivilotti MLA, et al. (2013). Clinical decision rules to rule out subarachnoid hemorrhage for acute headache. JAMA, 310(12), 1248-1255. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2013.278018>
- Notes: The key isn't "how bad" but "how fast to peak". A headache exploding within seconds to a minute is the danger sign; a slowly building migraine isn't. Vomiting, stiff neck, light sensitivity, brief fainting make it more so. Specificity of 15.3% means most people checked under this rule turn out fine - but missing one rebleed is death, so it's worth checking. Don't press it down with a painkiller and go back to sleep.

### 10. An elder who bumped their head becoming unsteady, dull, sleepy or one-sidedly weak two to three weeks to months later - get a head CT
<!-- tags: money=low time=mid will=no benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: A head CT runs two to three hundred yuan.
- TL;DR: After an elder bumps their head, the blood usually accumulates only by the third week. The moment of impact can be completely uneventful - they may not even remember it. This disease recurs after surgery in up to 33% and kills up to 32%. Finding an elder suddenly confused, floating when walking, always sleepy, weak in one hand - first ask backward whether they fell or hit their head in these two or three months, then get the CT.
- Benefit: The Chinese Society of Neurosurgery and others issued the Chinese Expert Consensus on Drug Treatment of Chronic Subdural Hematoma. A chronic subdural hematoma is a pool of blood slowly collecting between the brain's membranes - technically a chronic space-occupying collection between the arachnoid and the dura. It "usually forms in the third week after head trauma" and is commonest in the elderly. Post-surgery recurrence reaches 33%, mortality 32%. Of patients over 90, only 24% recover well overall. Systematic review: worldwide, 3.39 to 39.1 new cases per 100,000 people per year, rising with age - up to threefold over 80. The commonest causes are trauma and falls, then long-term heavy drinking, anti-clotting drugs (anticoagulants) and violence. A geriatrics review: the clinical picture "typically includes confusion, cognitive decline, gait disorder or drowsiness, and can resemble other geriatric syndromes"
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Zhang J, et al. (2021). Expert consensus on drug treatment of chronic subdural hematoma. Chinese Neurosurgical Journal, 7(1), 47. <https://doi.org/10.1186/s41016-021-00263-z> ; Dziho A, et al. (2025). Global prevalence and incidence of chronic subdural hematoma: A systematic review. Brain and Spine, 5, 105893. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bas.2025.105893> ; Mathon B, Shotar E (2026). Diagnostic and therapeutic management of chronic subdural hematoma in elderly patients. Gériatrie et Psychologie Neuropsychiatrie du Vieillissement, 24(1), 30-41. <https://doi.org/10.1684/pnv.2026.1274>
- Notes: The impact itself can be completely uneventful - the elder may not even remember bumping. So when the household notices "suddenly confused lately, floating when walking, always sleepy, one hand weak", first ask backward about falls or head bumps in these two or three months, then get the CT. Don't file it straight under "dementia" or "after-effects of the last stroke" - that's the one fork that delays. Elders on aspirin, clopidogrel, warfarin or newer oral anticoagulants are at higher risk, as is long-term heavy drinking. Fall prevention: entry 13 of chapter 1 (train balance and leg strength).

### 11. One leg suddenly swollen, tight and tender to pressure - see a doctor soon; plus sudden breathlessness or chest pain - call 120 at once
<!-- tags: money=low time=mid will=no benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: A leg-vessel ultrasound (lower-limb venous ultrasound) runs one to two hundred yuan.
- TL;DR: One leg suddenly swollen, a size thicker than the other, tender to pressure - get a leg vein ultrasound soon. Add sudden breathlessness, chest pain on deep breaths, fast heartbeat or faintness, and the clot may have reached the lungs - call 120 directly. Of confirmed pulmonary embolism, 17.4% die within 3 months. Sitting past 4 hours roughly doubles the risk, but per person it stays very low.
- Benefit: The International Cooperative Pulmonary Embolism Registry (ICOPER) collected 2,454 confirmed acute pulmonary embolism patients from 52 hospitals in 7 countries. Pulmonary embolism is a clot blocking the pulmonary artery. Their 3-month all-cause mortality was 17.4% (426/2454). WHO's WRIGHT project: travel of 4+ continuous hours "roughly doubles" the risk of venous clots and clots reaching the lungs (venous thromboembolism). But per person the probability stays very low - over 4 hours sitting still, "about one in six thousand"
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Goldhaber SZ, Visani L, De Rosa M (1999). Acute pulmonary embolism: clinical outcomes in the International Cooperative Pulmonary Embolism Registry (ICOPER). Lancet, 353(9162), 1386-1389. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(98)07534-5> ; WHO (2007). Study results released on travel and blood clots. <https://www.who.int/news/item/29-06-2007-study-results-released-on-travel-and-blood-clots>
- Notes: Both legs swollen together usually points to heart, liver, kidney or venous-function problems. One leg suddenly swollen and thicker than the other is the typical deep-vein thrombosis picture. Risky situations: long car or plane rides, bed rest after surgery or fracture, casts, around pregnancy and delivery, cancer, oral contraceptives. A clot breaking loose and blocking the pulmonary artery is pulmonary embolism: sudden breathlessness, chest pain worse on deep breaths, fast heartbeat, faintness, coughing blood (hemoptysis). At that point call 120 directly. The 17.4% is mortality among confirmed pulmonary embolism - not "a swollen leg carries this much risk"; don't scare yourself with the number.

### 12. Major bleeding: press the wound hard with a hand first; if a limb won't stop, apply a tourniquet - and call 120
<!-- tags: money=low time=mid will=no benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: A windlass tourniquet runs tens to a hundred-plus yuan - keep one in the car and the first-aid kit. Learning "place above the wound, twist until bleeding stops, note the time" takes minutes.

### 30. In an earthquake, first see what building you're in: shelter in place in a code-built structure; in a collapse-prone old building with the door steps away, get out

### 37. Walking into a group fight: back away and leave - don't step in to break it up, don't watch, don't pick up any weapon off the ground; to report, call 110 from a safe distance

For the dead: "those meeting martyr criteria are assessed as martyrs by law"; "where deemed a work-related death, the family receives the one-time work-death subsidy plus a special survivors' subsidy equal to 40 months of the person's wages". For the disabled: "those meeting work-injury-insurance conditions receive the corresponding treatment under the Work Injury Insurance Regulation; others have their disability grade assessed by civil affairs with corresponding treatment". Employment: "priority inclusion in employment assistance with focused support", with public-welfare posts "prioritizing eligible good Samaritans". Housing: "priority inclusion in the housing-security system - priority allocation of subsidized rentals or sales, or rent subsidies"
- Evidence: B
- Sources: National People's Congress (2020). Generally you file with the police or the good-Samaritan body (foundation) where it happened. If it goes to court, good-Samaritan civil matters are already within free legal aid. ### 40. A knife, rebar or glass lodged in the body: press around the object to stop bleeding - don't pull it out
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some benefit=big lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is resisting the "pull it out first" instinct and staying with the person until the ambulance.
- TL;DR: Pulling it out can cause major bleeding and makes the depth harder for the doctor to judge. The moves: call 120, press around the object with a hand or dressing to stop bleeding, and pad it steady so it doesn't wobble. Organs protruding from an abdominal wound: don't push them back - cover with a clean wet dressing. A bubbling chest wound: don't seal it with plastic - sealing can collapse the lung.
- Benefit: The IFRC's 2025 International First Aid Guidelines: "the first aider should press around an object embedded in the body (such as a knife), stabilize it, and not pull it out". "Do not remove any embedded object from an abdominal or chest wound - removal can cause major bleeding and makes it harder for the doctor to judge severity". "An embedded object should be stabilized; if it pulses with the heartbeat, let it pulse and steady it loosely". "The first aider should not push protruding organs back inside". An open chest-wall wound not gushing blood "may be left open without a dressing, because sealing the wound can let air build up in the chest cavity and partially or fully collapse the lung (pneumothorax)". All penetrating chest and abdominal wounds mean calling emergency at once
- Evidence: B
- Sources: International Federation of Red Cross and Red Crescent Societies (2025). International First Aid, Resuscitation and Education Guidelines 2025, pages 256-257, 267-268. <https://www.ifrc.org/sites/default/files/2026-03/IFRC%20International%20First%20Aid,%20Resuscitation%20and%20Education%20Guidelines%202025.pdf>
- Notes: B because this is consensus practice in guidelines, without randomized trials - you can't split casualties into "pull" and "don't pull" groups. The only exception is an object blocking the airway with the person unable to breathe. At hospital, go straight to the triage desk at the emergency entrance and say "penetrating wound" - that ranks for the resuscitation room at once; don't queue at registration (see entry 8 of chapter 24). Actually impaled: call 120 - the ambulance notifies the hospital to prepare in advance. How to press and apply a tourniquet for major bleeding: entry 12.

### 41. Suspected fracture: stop moving that part - support it still, cold-compress under 20 minutes, don't straighten it yourself
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no benefit=mid lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Free. A rigid board, a rolled magazine or clothing serves as support. Cold things go on over a layer of cloth.
- TL;DR: Treat every limb injury as a fracture first - standing and moving don't rule one out; only an X-ray can. Keep the injured part still: hold it with your hand, or prop it in its found position with a triangular bandage or board. Cold-compress early, at most 20 minutes at a stretch. Don't straighten it yourself. Bone through the skin: stop the bleeding and cover with a clean dressing first - don't push the bone back.
- Benefit: The IFRC published the 2025 International First Aid Guidelines. They write "any injury to a hand or foot should first be treated as a possible fracture", and "the only accurate way to confirm a fracture is an X-ray - when in doubt, treat it as one". The point of immobilization is "minimizing movement and pain". Cold compresses: "at most 20 continuous minutes at a time - this eases pain; longer cold can injure skin". "With a long bone like the femur, the bone's own bleeding alone can be fatal". Obvious pain or swelling, signs of shock, a femur (thigh-bone) fracture or a limb visibly out of position: call emergency. For an angulated fracture, whether to straighten it (reduce) on the spot: only when two conditions hold together - "a remote location (or limited resources) with the limb below the angulation cold and pale, or the rescuer unable to stabilize it well enough to move" - and a trained rescuer. For open fractures (bone through the skin), stopping bleeding and preventing infection come first
- Evidence: B
- Sources: International Federation of Red Cross and Red Crescent Societies (2025). International First Aid, Resuscitation and Education Guidelines 2025, pages 301-302. <https://www.ifrc.org/sites/default/files/2026-03/IFRC%20International%20First%20Aid,%20Resuscitation%20and%20Education%20Guidelines%202025.pdf>
- Notes: B because these are consensus practices. Traction-splinting a femur (thigh-bone) fracture before hospital rests on a single records-review study - less blood transfused in 24 hours and shorter stays, with death and complication differences too small to call; its evidence certainty is very low. And on reducing first versus splinting as found, no evidence was found at all. Fix three common misreads: standing and walking don't rule out a fracture; cold only, never heat, and no alternating; a sprain doesn't "walk itself loose" - rest it. Moving bans when an elder's fall suggests fracture: entry 2. One leg swelling after a cast or bed rest - watch for clots: entry 11. Whether to get a disability assessment and a disability certificate afterward: entries 10 and 11 of chapter 24.
