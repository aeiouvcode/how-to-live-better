# 6. The negative list

This chapter collects things that "look cheap and effective". Open the original research and their benefit comes out near zero, at zero, sometimes negative. The ordering weighs two things: how much money people spend on them, and how hard the supporting evidence is. The earlier an entry, the more it's worth stopping first.

### 1. Don't take multivitamins for longevity or to prevent cardiovascular disease
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no benefit=mid lens=money -->
- Cost: About 0.5 to 2 yuan a day, 200 to 700 a year. Taking them costs no time - one pill. Taken long enough they breed dependence: skip a day and it feels like something was left undone.
- TL;DR: 14 thousand male doctors took multivitamins for eleven years. Major heart-and-vessel events and deaths: exactly as many as in non-takers. Pooling 9 trials with over 50 thousand people, no effect on mortality either. Not buying saves two hundred to seven hundred a year. People with a confirmed deficiency, and pregnant women taking folic acid, are not in this entry.
- Benefit: The PHS II trial enrolled 14,641 male doctors, followed 11.2 years (median - half longer, half shorter). Takers and non-takers had the same rate of major cardiovascular events: HR 1.01 (HR is the hazard ratio; 1 means both sides identical). 95% CI 0.91-1.10 is the plausible range. Deaths the same too: all-cause mortality HR 0.94 (0.88-1.02). A US body called the USPSTF grades preventive measures. In 2022 it pooled 9 randomized controlled trials with 51,550 people: multivitamins showed no association with total mortality. Grade I - the evidence is insufficient to tell. It looks like a bargain because "one pill tops up every nutrient" sounds right, and it costs dimes a day
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Sesso HD et al. (2012). Multivitamins in the prevention of cardiovascular disease in men: the Physicians' Health Study II randomized controlled trial. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2012.14805> ; US Preventive Services Task Force (2022). Vitamin, Mineral, and Multivitamin Supplementation to Prevent Cardiovascular Disease and Cancer. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2022.8970> ; Gaziano JM et al. (2012). Multivitamins in the Prevention of Cancer in Men. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2012.14641> (the cancer result in Notes)
- Notes: Disputed: the same male doctors were also checked for cancer. Takers got cancer about 8% less often (0.86-0.998, P=0.04 - barely over the "doesn't look like chance" line). Cancer deaths about 12% lower (0.77-1.01) - a range crossing 1, so possibly chance. The benefit is small and seen only in male doctors. People with a confirmed deficiency, pregnant women (folic acid), and people whose diet is truly lopsided aren't in this entry - supplement as the doctor says

### 2. Don't take ordinary fish oil to prevent cardiovascular disease
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no benefit=mid lens=money -->
- Cost: About 1 to 3 yuan a day, 400 to 1,000 a year. No time cost.
- TL;DR: Two big trials enrolled 26 thousand people and 15 thousand diabetics respectively. People taking 1 g of fish oil a day had exactly as many major cardiovascular events and deaths as non-takers. "Eating fish helps" does not equal "fish-oil capsules help". Not buying saves four hundred to a thousand a year.
- Benefit: The VITAL trial enrolled 25,871 people taking 1 g of fish oil daily, followed 5.3 years (median). No detectable difference in major cardiovascular events or deaths. Major cardiovascular events HR 0.92 (HR is the hazard ratio; 1 means both sides identical). 95% CI 0.80-1.06 is the plausible range - it crosses 1, so no difference is visible. All-cause mortality HR 1.02 (0.90-1.15). The ASCEND trial enrolled 15,480 diabetics, also 1 g daily, followed on average 7.4 years, again no detectable difference. Serious vascular events RR 0.97 (0.87-1.08). All-cause mortality RR 0.95 (0.86-1.05). It looks like a bargain because many people read "eating fish helps" straight through as "fish-oil capsules help"
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Manson JE et al. (2019). Marine n-3 Fatty Acids and Prevention of Cardiovascular Disease and Cancer. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1811403> ; ASCEND Study Collaborative Group (2018). Effects of n-3 Fatty Acid Supplements in Diabetes Mellitus. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1804989> ; Bhatt DL et al. (2019). Cardiovascular Risk Reduction with Icosapent Ethyl for Hypertriglyceridemia. New England Journal of Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1812792> (the high-purity prescription fish-oil trial in Notes)
- Notes: Disputed: one trial did work. REDUCE-IT enrolled 8,179 people who already had cardiovascular disease or diabetes, all on statins, with elevated triglycerides (135-499 mg/dL). Taking 4 g a day of a pure-EPA prescription drug, their event rate was 17.2% versus 22.0% - risk about a quarter lower (HR 0.75, 0.68-0.83). But that's a high-dose prescription drug for high-risk patients, not ordinary people taking supplement fish oil. Eating fish itself isn't in this entry

### 3. Don't supplement vitamin D for longevity in people who aren't deficient
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no benefit=mid lens=money -->
- Cost: About 0.2 to 0.5 yuan a day, 100 to 200 a year. Not much money - the real cost is believing you've already done prevention.
- TL;DR: Two trials: 26 thousand people took it 5 years; 21 thousand elderly took it 5 years. Supplementing people who weren't deficient changed nothing for cancer, cardiovascular disease or death - mortality 5.3% versus 5.1%. People with a confirmed deficiency, osteoporosis, or chronic lack of sunlight aren't in this entry - they supplement as the doctor says.
- Benefit: The VITAL trial enrolled 25,871 people taking 2,000 IU daily, followed 5.3 years (median). No detectable difference in cancer, cardiovascular disease or death. Invasive cancer HR 0.96 (HR is the hazard ratio; 1 means identical; 95% CI 0.88-1.06 is the plausible range). Major cardiovascular events HR 0.97 (0.85-1.12). All-cause mortality HR 0.99 (0.87-1.12). The D-Health trial enrolled 21,315 Australians over 60 taking 60,000 IU monthly for 5 years. No difference either: all-cause mortality HR 1.04 (0.93-1.18), deaths 5.3% versus 5.1%. It looks like a bargain because some studies only observe and record - they found low-vitamin-D people die more - and that got misread as "supplementing fixes it"
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Manson JE et al. (2019). Vitamin D Supplements and Prevention of Cancer and Cardiovascular Disease. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1809944> ; Neale RE et al. (2022). The D-Health Trial: a randomised controlled trial of the effect of vitamin D on mortality. Lancet Diabetes Endocrinol. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S2213-8587(21)00345-4>
- Notes: Disputed: neither trial specifically recruited people truly deficient in vitamin D, so the conclusion governs only "people who aren't deficient". The confirmed-deficient, people with osteoporosis, people chronically without sunlight, and infants should still supplement as the doctor says. This entry looked only at total mortality, cancer and cardiovascular events - nothing else

### 4. Don't take antioxidant supplements (beta-carotene, vitamin E, vitamin A) to prevent cancer
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no benefit=mid lens=mortality -->
- Cost: About 0.5 to 2 yuan a day. For smokers, the real cost is lung cancer risk going up with it.
- TL;DR: This one isn't merely useless. Pooling 78 trials with nearly 300 thousand people, antioxidant takers had slightly higher mortality. In two big trials done specifically in smokers, beta-carotene raised lung cancer incidence 18% to 28% and death 8% to 17%. Smokers and asbestos-exposed people especially must not take it.
- Benefit: Cochrane pooled 78 randomized controlled trials with 296,707 people. Antioxidant takers' total mortality: RR 1.02 (RR is the risk ratio; 1 means identical; 95% CI 0.98-1.05 is the plausible range). Looking only at the more rigorous, less bias-prone trials: RR 1.04 (1.01-1.07) - slightly higher. Beta-carotene alone RR 1.05 (1.01-1.09); vitamin E alone RR 1.03 (1.00-1.05). The ATBC trial enrolled 29,133 male smokers taking 20 mg daily: lung cancer incidence 18% higher (3%-36%), total mortality 8% higher (1%-16%). The CARET trial enrolled 18,314 smokers or asbestos-exposed people: lung cancer risk about 28% higher (RR 1.28, 1.04-1.57), mortality risk about 17% higher (all-cause RR 1.17, 1.03-1.33). It looks like a bargain because "antioxidation is anti-aging, anti-aging prevents cancer" - every link in that chain sounds like common sense
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Bjelakovic G et al. (2012). Antioxidant supplements for prevention of mortality in healthy participants and patients with various diseases. Cochrane Database Syst Rev. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD007176.pub2> ; The Alpha-Tocopherol, Beta Carotene Cancer Prevention Study Group (1994). The effect of vitamin E and beta carotene on the incidence of lung cancer and other cancers in male smokers. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM199404143301501> ; Omenn GS et al. (1996). Effects of a combination of beta carotene and vitamin A on lung cancer and cardiovascular disease. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM199605023341802>
- Notes: The USPSTF, the official American body grading preventive measures, gave beta-carotene and vitamin E a grade D in 2022 - recommending against them. Smokers and asbestos-exposed people have already been harmed by them. Antioxidants naturally present in vegetables and fruit aren't in this entry - eat those as usual

### 5. Don't count on glucosamine/chondroitin to treat knee osteoarthritis
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no benefit=mid lens=money -->
- Cost: About 2 to 5 yuan a day, 700 to 1,800 a year.
- TL;DR: 1,583 people with knee osteoarthritis took them for 24 weeks. In the placebo group, 60.1% already reported less pain. Glucosamine beat that by only 3.9 percentage points, chondroitin by 5.3 - both gaps small enough to be chance. Seven hundred to eighteen hundred a year buys essentially that sixty percent placebo effect.
- Benefit: The GAIT trial enrolled 1,583 knee osteoarthritis patients for 24 weeks. Even on placebo (a fake pill), 60.1% reported pain relief. Glucosamine beat it by only 3.9 percentage points (P=0.30). Chondroitin by 5.3 (P=0.17). Both together by 6.5 (P=0.09). All gaps too small to rule out chance. The real drug used as a comparator in the same trial, celecoxib, beat placebo by 10.0 points (P=0.008). It looks like a bargain because "topping up the raw material of cartilage" sounds exactly on-target, and the fake pill alone already satisfies sixty percent
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Clegg DO et al. (2006). Glucosamine, chondroitin sulfate, and the two in combination for painful knee osteoarthritis. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa052771>
- Notes: Disputed: in the same trial, among those with severe pain at the start, the two together worked for 79.2% versus 54.3% on control (P=0.002). But the authors say that was dug out of the data afterward - a lead, not a conclusion. What genuinely has evidence for knee osteoarthritis is losing weight and training muscle - see chapter 2

### 6. Don't rely on vitamin C to prevent colds
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no benefit=small lens=money -->
- Cost: An effervescent tablet runs about 1 to 2 yuan, 300 to 700 a year.
- TL;DR: Pooling 29 controlled trials with 11,306 people, daily vitamin C basically doesn't reduce the colds you catch. Starting it after a cold begins works only inconsistently. It does shorten a cold by about 8% - about half a day less misery. Whether that half day is worth three to five hundred a year is your math. Marathon runners and soldiers marching in the cold are the exception - among them, takers caught half as many colds.
- Benefit: Cochrane pooled 29 controlled trials with 11,306 people. For ordinary people, daily vitamin C basically doesn't cut colds: incidence RR 0.97 (RR is the risk ratio; 1 means identical). 95% CI 0.94-1.00 is the plausible range. Trials that started vitamin C only after a cold began showed "no consistent effect" in the original - some worked, some didn't. In the same review, long-term daily use shortened cold misery by about 8% (adults, 95% CI 3%-12%) and 14% in children (7%-21%). Marathon runners, skiers and soldiers marching in cold regions are the exception: across 5 trials with 598 people, about half as many caught colds - incidence RR 0.48 (95% CI 0.35-0.64). It looks like a bargain because it's cheap, tastes sweet-sour, and "vitamin C prevents colds" has circulated for half a century
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Hemilä H, Chalker E (2013). Vitamin C for preventing and treating the common cold. Cochrane Database Syst Rev. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD000980.pub4>
- Notes: Disputed: the same review found daily use does shorten cold misery - about 8% in adults (3%-12%) and 14% in children (7%-21%), roughly half a day less sick per cold. Marathon runners, skiers and soldiers marching in the cold are the exception: across 5 trials with 598 people, about half as many caught colds (RR 0.48, 0.35-0.64). The vast majority of people belong to neither category

### 7. Don't get a "full-body PET-CT" or a "tumor-marker panel" when you have no symptoms
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no benefit=mid lens=mortality -->
- Cost: A full-body PET-CT runs about 7,000 to 10,000 yuan, plus a dose of ionizing radiation. A tumor-marker panel costs a few hundred. The real cost comes after: one "abnormality" that turns out to be a false alarm (that's a false positive) - the rechecks, the tissue sampling, the surgery; the money and the misery all live in that stretch.
- TL;DR: Screening people without symptoms mostly finds false alarms. Of 1,192 symptom-free people given whole-body CT, 86% had at least one "abnormality" found and 37% were called back for further workup - yet the overwhelming majority was benign. In the big ovarian-cancer-marker screening trial, the screened group's death rate came out slightly higher. And 0.2% to 3.25% of people went under the knife for a false alarm.
- Benefit: The USPSTF, the American body that grades preventive measures, gave screening symptom-free women for ovarian cancer with CA-125 and ultrasound a grade D - recommending against it. The basis is the PLCO trial: ovarian-cancer deaths were 0.34% in the screened group versus 0.29% in the usual-care group, RR 1.18 (95% CI 0.82-1.71). Screening brought no benefit. The share undergoing surgery for a false positive ran 0.2%-3.25% across trials, and up to 15% of those had serious surgical complications. Another study gave whole-body CT screening to 1,192 symptom-free people: 86% had at least one "abnormality" and 37% were advised further testing, with the original noting "most findings were benign as described". It looks like a bargain because "one scan over the whole body, catch it early, treat it early" fits intuition too well
- Evidence: A
- Sources: US Preventive Services Task Force (2018). Screening for Ovarian Cancer: US Preventive Services Task Force Recommendation Statement. JAMA (the same recommendation also has a USPSTF official page). <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2017.21926>, <https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/ovarian-cancer-screening> ; Furtado CD et al. (2005). Whole-body CT screening: spectrum of findings and recommendations in 1192 patients. Radiology. <https://doi.org/10.1148/radiol.2372041741>
- Notes: The tumor-marker part is backed by randomized controlled trials. The whole-body CT and PET-CT part has only follow-up records of "what happened to people who got scanned", with no randomized groups - one grade weaker, effectively B. The screenings that genuinely have evidence - colorectal, cervical, breast cancer, and low-dose chest CT for high-risk people - are a different matter; see chapter 1. PET-CT for people who already have symptoms or a cancer diagnosis is treatment, not screening, and isn't in this entry

### 8. Don't count on your band/watch's exercise quantification to help you lose weight
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no benefit=mid lens=money -->
- Cost: The device runs 200 to 2,000 yuan. Plus daily attention diverted to the data.
- TL;DR: 471 overweight adults followed the same diet and exercise program. The group wearing devices lost 3.5 kg in two years; the group without lost 5.9 kg. Devices don't help with weight loss - the exercise itself is what works.
- Benefit: The IDEA trial enrolled 471 overweight or obese adults for 24 months. Everyone followed the same diet and exercise program; the only difference was wearing a wearable or not. The wearing group lost 3.5 kg (95% CI 2.6-4.5, the plausible range). The non-wearing group lost 5.9 kg (5.0-6.8) - more. The gap: 2.4 kg (1.0-3.7, P=0.002). It looks like a bargain because "you can't manage what you can't measure" sounds sensible, and a device is a one-time buy
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Jakicic JM et al. (2016). Effect of Wearable Technology Combined With a Lifestyle Intervention on Long-term Weight Loss: The IDEA Randomized Clinical Trial. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2016.12858>
- Notes: This is a single trial, using an early-2010s arm-worn device - it can't support "all wearables are useless". Exercise itself works; see chapter 2. This entry says only one thing: for losing weight, a device is not money you must spend

### 9. Don't pay the organic premium "to be healthier"
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no benefit=mid lens=money -->
- Cost: Usually 1 to 3 times the price of the same conventional food.
- TL;DR: Looking at 17 studies in people and 223 studies measuring composition together, there is no strong evidence organic food is more nutritious. Only 3 of them actually looked at people's health after eating - and found no difference either. Organic produce does show pesticide residues 30 percentage points less often, but detected doesn't mean over the limit. Eating more vegetables and fruit itself works - organic or not doesn't change that.
- Benefit: A systematic review combined 17 studies done in people and 223 studies measuring food composition. Its conclusion: "the published literature lacks strong evidence that organic foods are significantly more nutritious than conventional foods". Only 3 studies looked at health after eating (allergies and symptomatic infections) and found no difference. Organic produce tested positive for pesticide residues 30 percentage points less often - but detection isn't exceeding the limit. It looks like a bargain because "no pesticides means safer, safer means healthier" - each step sounds like common sense
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Smith-Spangler C et al. (2012). Are organic foods safer or healthier than conventional alternatives?: a systematic review. Ann Intern Med. <https://doi.org/10.7326/0003-4819-157-5-201209040-00007>
- Notes: Studies directly measuring health after eating are very few. So this entry means "no evidence it's better", not "proven to be useless". Environmental impact, animal welfare and taste are outside this entry. Eating more vegetables and fruit works - organic or not doesn't change that

### 10. Don't spend big money on health supplements, herbal pastes, and tonics to "regulate your body"
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no benefit=big lens=money -->
- Cost: Anywhere from hundreds to tens of thousands of yuan. Packaged as gift boxes, prices often multiply several-fold.
- TL;DR: The agency that governs health foods itself requires a line on every package: it is not a drug and cannot replace drugs for treating disease. Even its own regulator doesn't hold that it treats disease. Its most common ingredients - multivitamins, fish oil, vitamin D, antioxidants, glucosamine, vitamin C - were checked one by one in this chapter's first six entries, and none made a difference. Most of this money isn't spent on efficacy: it's bought for parents, carried to their door. The sentiment is fine; the problem is only the goods.
- Benefit: The State Administration for Market Regulation requires the front of health-food packaging to carry, on no less than 20% of its area, the sentence: "Health foods are not drugs and cannot replace drugs in treating disease." The same rule fences health foods' purpose into "supplementing dietary nutrients, maintaining or improving bodily health status, or reducing disease risk factors". In other words, the regulator itself does not endorse them as treatments. What their most common ingredients - multivitamins, fish oil, vitamin D, antioxidants, glucosamine, vitamin C - each did in randomized controlled trials was listed entry by entry in this chapter's first 6 entries. It looks like a bargain because "not for curing, just for regulating" can't be wrong no matter how you say it - nobody can prove it false. And money spent on gifts runs on sentiment, not evidence
- Evidence: C
- Sources: State Administration for Market Regulation (2019). SAMR press conference on the Guidelines for Warning Statements on Health Foods and the Administrative Measures for the Health Food Ingredient Directory and Function Directory. <https://www.samr.gov.cn/tssps/sjdt/tpxw/art/2023/art_4b658b824b1b4b0ba57c09a56cc93aad.html>
- Notes: This entry means "no supporting evidence", not "proven ineffective", hence only grade C. For products with clearly stated ingredients, check the corresponding studies under the earlier entries. People with confirmed deficiencies or under treatment should follow their doctors. This entry negates the product, not the sentiment. If an elderly parent bought some themselves and is already taking it, and it neither displaces their actual medication nor causes problems, there's no need to show up and fight that battle - arranging the vaccines and screenings that are due matters more. The same money, moved to the items below, has a source you can look up in this book for every one. Taking them for the annual flu shot: entry 20 of chapter 1. The shingles vaccine after 50: entry 21 of chapter 1. The pneumococcal vaccine at 65-plus: entry 22 of chapter 1. A blood-pressure monitor, plus watching them take their hypertension medication as prescribed until readings hit target: entry 7 of chapter 1. Fixing up the bathroom and stairs at home, plus balance and leg-strength practice: entry 13 of chapter 1. Age-appropriate cancer screening, accompanied once: entries 17 to 19 of chapter 1. For elderly already bedridden long-term, pressure-ulcer prevention and long-term care insurance: entries 7 and 8 of chapter 17. If you want to carry something to their door, fruit, rice and flour, or things they love to eat beat herbal pastes. What genuinely needs blocking is two kinds: pay-first investment-style eldercare, and anything that would displace medication - see entry 5 of chapter 17

### 11. Don't take probiotics long-term when your gut has no problems
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no benefit=small lens=money -->
- Cost: About 2 to 5 yuan a day, 700 to 1,800 a year.
- TL;DR: Pooling 45 trials in healthy adults, probiotics don't durably change gut bacteria - stop and they revert - and blood lipids don't improve either. Specific situations like antibiotic-associated diarrhea have their own evidence and aren't in this entry.
- Benefit: A review pooled 45 trials in healthy adults, concluding it "failed to support that probiotics cause persistent changes in gut microbiota or improve blood lipids in healthy adults". Gut bacteria do shift - but temporarily; stop and they revert. It looks like a bargain because "gut health" is broad enough to fit anything, and a bottle a day costs little
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Khalesi S et al. (2019). A review of probiotic supplementation in healthy adults: helpful or hype? Eur J Clin Nutr. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41430-018-0135-9>
- Notes: The same review also found small improvements in healthy people - better stool form and consistency, more lactobacilli in the vaginal flora of women. Antibiotic-associated diarrhea, and one specific strain for one specific disease, each have their own evidence and aren't in this entry. This entry targets only healthy people taking probiotics daily as a supplement

### 12. Don't force yourself into cold showers "to boost immunity"
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no benefit=small lens=money -->
- Cost: Free. Thirty to ninety daily seconds of misery; sticking with it through winter takes willpower. People with cardiovascular disease should be careful - cold shock carries risk for them.
- TL;DR: In a 3,018-person trial, cold-shower takers called in sick 29% less often, but their total sick days matched everyone else's - so no, not less illness. Pooling 11 trials in 2025, inflammation markers briefly rose right after cold exposure while immune markers didn't change. Shower cold if you enjoy it - just don't book it as a health investment.
- Benefit: A Dutch randomized controlled trial of 3,018 people added a 30-, 60- or 90-second cold blast after a hot shower. They called in sick 29% less often (IRR 0.71, a ratio of counts). But the original states "no significant between-group difference in sick days" - total days ill was the same on both sides. Quality of life, work productivity and anxiety showed no meaningful differences either. A 2025 systematic review pooled 11 randomized trials with 3,177 people: immediately after and 1 hour after cold immersion, inflammation markers rose; immune markers didn't change; stress dropped only at the 12-hour point. The authors themselves say the evidence is "limited by few RCTs and small samples". It looks like a bargain because it costs nothing and "suffering means it works" feels so natural
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Buijze GA et al. (2016). The Effect of Cold Showering on Health and Work: A Randomized Controlled Trial. PLOS ONE. <https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0161749> ; Cain T et al. (2025). Effects of cold-water immersion on health and wellbeing: A systematic review and meta-analysis. PLOS ONE. <https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0317615>
- Notes: Disputed: sick-call frequency really did drop 29%, and that gap doesn't look like chance. But total sick days didn't drop - so it reads more like "coming to work while sick" than genuinely less illness. Athletes using cold water for post-training recovery is a separate question this entry doesn't cover. Cold showers themselves aren't harmful to healthy people; this entry only says don't book them as a health investment

### 13. Don't buy detox, colon-cleanse, enzyme, or alkaline-water products
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no benefit=mid lens=money -->
- Cost: Hundreds to thousands of yuan. The kind that "detoxes" by starving you can also crash your blood sugar and starve you of nutrients.
- TL;DR: One review went looking for evidence behind detox diets, verbatim: the industry has boomed while the clinical evidence supporting these diets is minimal, and no randomized controlled trial of a commercial detox diet has ever been run. As for the "acidic body causes cancer" line, someone went through 8,278 papers, found 1 eligible, and it showed no association.
- Benefit: That review concluded "although the detox industry is booming, there is very little clinical evidence to support the use of these diets", and "no randomized controlled trials of commercial detox diets" exist. Another systematic review checked dietary acid load, alkaline water and cancer: of 8,278 papers screened, only 1 was eligible, and it showed no association. The authors wrote that "promoting alkaline diet and alkaline water to the public for cancer prevention or treatment is not justified". It looks like a bargain because "there are toxins in the body to expel" and "an acidic body causes cancer" - both premises sound like physiology
- Evidence: C
- Sources: Klein AV, Kiat H (2015). Detox diets for toxin elimination and weight management: a critical review of the evidence. J Hum Nutr Diet. <https://doi.org/10.1111/jhn.12286> ; Fenton TR, Huang T (2016). Systematic review of the association between dietary acid load, alkaline water and cancer. BMJ Open. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2015-010438>
- Notes: This entry is of the "no supporting evidence" kind, hence only grade C. Clearing the waste products of metabolism is what the liver and kidneys already do. As long as those two organs aren't diseased, no extra "detox" is needed

### 14. Don't force yourself to drink water to hit "8 glasses a day"
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no benefit=small lens=money -->
- Cost: Free. It costs a bit of attention to remember to drink, and more night-time bathroom trips.
- TL;DR: Someone went specifically to trace the source of "8 glasses a day" and found not one study supporting it. Conversely, diet-and-fluid surveys of thousands suggest nowhere near that much is needed, because food and other drinks already bring in large amounts of water. Drink when thirsty, glance at urine color - that's enough. People working in heat, exercising intensely, with a kidney-stone history, and elderly people who barely feel thirst should drink proactively - separately.
- Benefit: A literature review examined the "8x8" claim - eight 8-ounce glasses of water a day - and concluded it found "no scientific studies supporting 8x8". It also wrote that "surveys of food and fluid intake of thousands of adults strongly suggest that such large amounts are not needed", because food and other beverages already supply large amounts of water. The claim looks like a bargain because water is free, 8 is a tidy number, and "drink more water" sounds harmless in any setting
- Evidence: C
- Sources: Valtin H (2002). "Drink at least eight glasses of water a day." Really? Is there scientific evidence for "8 x 8"? Am J Physiol Regul Integr Comp Physiol. <https://doi.org/10.1152/ajpregu.00365.2002>
- Notes: What this entry negates is the number "you must hit X glasses daily", not drinking water itself. Drink when thirsty and check urine color - enough. People working in heat, exercising intensely, with kidney-stone history, and older people with blunted thirst should drink proactively and aren't in this entry

### 15. Don't pay for fortune-telling, tarot, or horoscopes to make decisions
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no benefit=mid lens=money -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is giving up a shortcut for making up your mind - from now on you think for yourself.
- TL;DR: A classic classroom experiment: the "personality analysis written for you" students received was identical for everyone - yet they broadly found it accurate. That's exactly why fortune-telling always seems right. And the cost isn't just tens of yuan: that fortune-telling gang in Xi County, Henan collected "incense money" and "offering packages" up to 3,888 yuan - the ringleader got eleven years.
- Benefit: The classic classroom experiment: after a personality test, the "individual analysis" students received was identical for everyone, yet broadly accepted as accurate. The author's conclusion: "acceptance by the person or the analyst does not prove the interpretation correct." That explains why fortune-telling always seems right - the phenomenon is called the Barnum effect. Astrology also got a double-blind test: both experiments checked "can a natal chart accurately describe a subject's personality", published in Nature. ### 16. Don't buy blue-light-blocking glasses to "protect your eyes", don't believe "months in front of a screen ruins your eyes" either - but eye pain with redness is an emergency

### 23. Don't count on buying things to improve your mood or sense of status
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=yes benefit=mid lens=money -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is not placing the order when your mood dips.
- TL;DR: The more importance people place on money and material things, the lower their self-reported happiness on average - across 259 combined samples. But the association is weak, and which causes which can't be told. Another set of studies compared buying experiences versus buying things: money spent on an experience satisfies more. So when your mood dips, moving the budget to one outing or one meal with people is more likely to help than moving it to the shopping cart.
- Benefit: One meta-analysis - combining many studies into one calculation - covered 259 independent samples and 753 effect sizes. Result: the more people value material things, the lower their self-reported well-being. On the most commonly used multidimensional scale, correlation r = -0.19 (adjusted ρ = -0.24). On scales measuring only "valuing money", the association is weaker, r = -0.08 to -0.11. Split by which outcome is examined, strength differs: strongest with risky health and consumer behavior and negative self-appraisal, r = -0.28 to -0.44; weakest with life satisfaction and negative emotion, r = -0.13 to -0.15. The authors also ran a mediation analysis - checking what it passes through in the middle - suggesting the negative association may come from unmet psychological needs. The other study: in two surveys of different populations, respondents said money spent acquiring a life experience made them happier than money spent on things. In a follow-up lab experiment, the good mood after recalling an experiential purchase also beat recalling a material one (a nationwide lens doesn't apply - both studies used foreign samples)
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Dittmar H, Bond R, Hurst M, Kasser T (2014). The relationship between materialism and personal well-being: A meta-analysis. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 107(5), 879-924. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037409> ; Van Boven L, Gilovich T (2003). To do or to have? That is the question. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 85(6), 1193-1202. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.85.6.1193>
- Notes: Disputed: most included studies asked one group of people at one point in time whether two things move together - which came first can't be told. The authors themselves say in their conclusion that pinning the direction takes more experiments and long-term follow-up. So this entry can't be read as "buying things makes you unhappy" - only as "counting on buying things to become happy is a long shot". And the association is weak: r = -0.19 explains only a few percentage points of the differences; people vary enormously. Both studies used foreign samples; no comparable Chinese data exists to cite. This entry doesn't oppose spending on things you like - it opposes using shopping as an emotion-regulation tool, because that makes ordering again your first reflex the next time your mood dips. Concrete money-saving moves are in chapter 5. What genuinely has evidence behind it when your mood is bad: the second half of chapter 22 and chapter 29. Budget added to stay "one notch above others": entry 24 of this chapter (moving up a notch).

### 24. Don't spend more on a house, a car, or a new circle just to "move up a notch among the people around you"
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some benefit=mid lens=money -->
- Cost: Free. All you have to do is ask one extra question before adding budget: is this markup buying the thing itself, or my position in this group? The hard part is not following when everyone around you has upgraded.
- TL;DR: Comparing people with the same income: the more the neighbors earn, the lower that person's reported happiness. This happens only among neighbors with the same education who interact often. So money spent to move into a place where everyone is better off than you buys moving yourself down a notch. But when everyone's income rises together, people still feel better off - absolute level still counts.
- Benefit: The US National Survey of Families and Households (NSFH) revisited the same people in two waves; the author matched the data against local average income (PUMA-level, estimated from the 5% sample of the 1990 census). After stripping out the person's own income and a series of personal characteristics, the result: the higher the neighbors' income, the lower the self-reported happiness. The effect is large, stable under alternative calculations, and highly significant. In the author's phrasing, "neighbors' income rising" and "own income falling by the same amount" produce happiness drops of the same order of magnitude. After switching to comparing each person with themselves over time (individual fixed effects), the coefficient keeps its direction and similar size, though significance falls to the 10% level. Restricting comparisons to the same state and same survey wave (state and wave fixed effects) barely changes the result. So it isn't caused by "people who move to poorer places were happier to begin with", nor by unmeasured state-level factors. The effect is stronger among people who interact often with neighbors, and not significant among people whose friends are all outside the community. Renters and homeowners show no difference - which doesn't support the explanation "expensive places have expensive housing, so less money is actually left to spend". Happiness falls only with the income of neighbors of the same education; it barely responds to differently-educated neighbors' income. And it transmits mainly through "satisfaction with one's own material situation", not through satisfaction with other domains like family life. Finally, when own and neighbors' incomes rise together in the same proportion, happiness still rises
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Luttmer EFP (2005). Neighbors as Negatives: Relative Earnings and Well-Being. The Quarterly Journal of Economics, 120(3), 963-1002. <https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/120.3.963> ; Luttmer EFP (2004). Neighbors as Negatives: Relative Earnings and Well-Being. NBER Working Paper No. 10667 (the phrasing and magnitudes cited in this entry were verified verbatim against this version). <https://www.nber.org/papers/w10667>
- Notes: The grade is B for several reasons: the data comes from surveys revisiting the same people, there is only this one study, and the sample is foreign; the outcome measured is self-reported happiness, not money. The author did use statistical methods to rule out several kinds of interference - comparing each person with themselves over time, comparing within the same state and survey wave - but it is ultimately not a randomized trial. No comparable Chinese data exists to cite either. The benefit tier of "mid" follows the lens of entry 23 of this chapter (don't count on buying things for mood). The money saved can genuinely reach the tens of thousands of yuan. But there is no number for "how much happiness that saving buys", so it isn't scored "big" for a ten-thousand-yuan-scale saving. This entry doesn't oppose spending on a bigger home, a quieter neighborhood, or a shorter commute - those buy the thing itself. What it blocks is this one reason: "my colleagues all upgraded, I can't fall behind them". How to rank housing priorities: entry 18 of chapter 4 (put commute length first when choosing where to live). Shopping to regulate mood: entry 23 of this chapter (don't count on buying things for mood). Constantly comparing upward online: entry 21 of chapter 3 (don't make "how others are doing" daily required reading)

### 25. Don't believe "willpower is a muscle that runs out after a while"
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no benefit=mid lens=time -->
- Cost: Free. All that has to change is one story you tell yourself.
- TL;DR: The popular account: do one self-control-draining task and you're more likely to break down afterward, so spend your willpower carefully. 23 laboratories, 2,141 people, redid this experiment under one standardized protocol - the result was essentially zero. Stop explaining yourself with "my willpower is used up today", and stop paying for courses that teach you to stockpile it.
- Benefit: A multi-lab preregistered replication - 23 laboratories, 2,141 participants in total - redid the sequential-task paradigm under Sripada and colleagues' standardized protocol: first a task that drains self-control, then a second task needing self-control. The pooled result showed a very small ego-depletion effect whose 95% confidence interval includes 0 (d = 0.04, 95% CI -0.07 to 0.15). On that basis the authors discussed whether the "self-control is a limited resource" model still holds.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Hagger MS, Chatzisarantis NLD, Alberts H, et al. (2016). A Multilab Preregistered Replication of the Ego-Depletion Effect. Perspectives on Psychological Science, 11(4), 546-573. <https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691616652873>
- Notes: What this entry negates is the model "self-control is a resource that gets used up", not the fact that people get tired. Too little sleep and too long a work stretch lower efficiency - that's a different matter, handled by the sleep and work-hour entries of chapter 3. What genuinely has randomized-trial support is changing the environment and changing the phrasing: entry 10 of chapter 4 (move what you don't want to touch farther away) and entry 1 of chapter 4 (write it as "at what time, where, if what happens, do what"). The other entries in this chapter count money; this one counts time and energy - no conversion between the two.

### 26. Don't count on breakfast or 16:8 intermittent fasting to control your weight - pick the mealtimes you can keep long-term
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no benefit=small lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Free. What you save is the daily mental effort of fighting the clock over meals.
- TL;DR: The popular account: skipping breakfast makes you overeat at lunch and dinner, so the day ends up bigger. In randomized trials that isn't what happens: breakfast-skippers ate over two hundred fewer calories a day and weighed slightly less. 16:8 versus simply eating less: no difference in weight over a year. Long-term breakfast-skippers do show about 17% more cardiovascular disease; total mortality shows no visible difference.
- Benefit: First the claim "skipping breakfast makes you eat more later". A meta-analysis combined 13 randomized trials. People assigned to the breakfast group ate 259.79 kcal a day more than the no-breakfast group (95% CI 78.87-440.71). Weight was 0.44 kg higher (0.07-0.82). So breakfast-skippers did not make it back at lunch and dinner. These trials were short - averaging 7 weeks for weight, 2 weeks for intake. Next, 16:8-style eating confined to fixed hours. One trial ran 116 overweight adults for 12 weeks: the group eating only between noon and 8 PM versus the group eating three regular meals differed by -0.26 kg (-1.30 to 0.78), not significant. Another, run in Guangzhou with 139 patients with obesity over 12 months: time-restricted eating plus calorie control versus calorie control alone lost 1.8 kg more (-4.0 to 0.4), also not significant. Finally, is long-term breakfast-skipping harmful? In a pooled 2.38 million people, skippers had about 17% higher cardiovascular disease risk (OR 1.17, 1.09-1.26) and about 49% higher cardiovascular death (OR 1.49, 1.20-1.84). But those are record-only studies without randomized groups. In the original study that followed 17 to 23 years, the all-cause mortality hazard ratio was 1.19, 95% CI 0.99 to 1.42 - it crosses 1, so it doesn't count as a difference.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Sievert K, Hussain SM, Page MJ, et al. (2019). Effect of breakfast on weight and energy intake: systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials. BMJ. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.l42> ; Lowe DA, Wu N, Rohdin-Bibby L, et al. (2020). Effects of Time-Restricted Eating on Weight Loss and Other Metabolic Parameters in Women and Men With Overweight and Obesity. JAMA Internal Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2020.4153> ; Liu D, Huang Y, Huang C, et al. (2022). Calorie Restriction with or without Time-Restricted Eating in Weight Loss. New England Journal of Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2114833> ; Zhang H, Zhang S, Liu Y, et al. (2025). The association between skipping breakfast and cardiovascular disease: a meta analysis. Frontiers in Cardiovascular Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.3389/fcvm.2025.1565806> ; Rong S, Snetselaar LG, Xu G, et al. (2019). Association of Skipping Breakfast With Cardiovascular and All-Cause Mortality. Journal of the American College of Cardiology. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2019.01.065> ; Fernandes-Alves D, Teixeira GP, Guimarães KC, Crispim CA (2026). Systematic Review and Meta-analysis of Randomized Clinical Trials Comparing Time-Restricted Eating With and Without Caloric Restriction for Weight Loss. Nutrition Reviews. <https://doi.org/10.1093/nutrit/nuaf053> ; Sun T, Zhang L, Lu Y, et al. (2024). Non-linear relationship between the first meal time of the day and gallstone incidence in American adults. Frontiers in Nutrition. <https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2024.1521707> ; Liu T, Wang Y, Wang X, et al. (2023). Habitually Skipping Breakfast Is Associated with the Risk of Gastrointestinal Cancers: Evidence from the Kailuan Cohort Study. Journal of General Internal Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1007/s11606-023-08094-7>
- Notes: Disputed. The 17% cardiovascular figure comes from record-only studies without randomized groups. Breakfast-skippers are already more likely to smoke, drink, sit long, and earn less, and those factors can't be cleanly adjusted away - so how much of the 17% is breakfast itself can't be said. The all-cause mortality estimate already crossed 1. The dispute on the other side concerns 16:8: pooling 30 trials and 1,341 people, with both groups eating the same amount, the time-restricted group lost 1.46 kg more (-2.65 to -0.26), at the cost of losing 0.41 kg more lean mass. But most of those trials were very short, and the longest, strictest 12-month trial found no difference. Long fasting windows also relate to the gallbladder. In a cross-sectional survey of 6,547 people, each hour later in the day's first meal came with about 5% more detected gallstones (OR 1.05, 1.02-1.08); people whose first meal fell between 9 AM and 2 PM had about 49% more than earlier eaters (OR 1.49, 1.24-1.77). The mechanism: the longer bile sits in the gallbladder, the easier stones precipitate. But this is a same-point-in-time survey - people may have changed their eating habits because of a gallbladder problem first. China's Kailuan cohort followed 5.6 years with 369 gastrointestinal cancers: breakfast-skippers had 2.32 times the colorectal cancer risk (1.34-4.01) and 5.43 times the gallbladder and extrahepatic bile-duct cancer risk (1.34-21.93). That last confidence range stretches to 21.93 - too few events, a lead only. Putting the sides together: for weight control, meal timing matters very little - don't spend effort on it. As for whether skipping breakfast long-term is bad, the existing evidence is all association and can't prove cause, but it leans consistently unfavorable - to be safe, eat normally, just don't count on it to lose weight for you. Gastritis and ulcers are mainly caused not by an empty stomach but by H. pylori and long-term painkiller use - what to test is in entry 23 of chapter 1 (test for H. pylori, eradicate if positive). What to do when a checkup finds gallstones that never hurt: entry 20 of this chapter (gallstones that never hurt - no preventive removal). For weight control, what genuinely has evidence is what and how much you eat: entry 28 of chapter 2 (five servings of fruit and vegetables a day), entry 29 of chapter 2 (cut ultra-processed food) and entry 33 of chapter 2 (keep BMI at 20-25). People with diabetes, people on glucose-lowering medication, and pregnant people don't fit this entry - a missed meal can mean low blood sugar; follow medical advice. Extreme fasting and purging are a different matter - entry 1 of chapter 28 (don't use extreme dieting, fasting, or purging to control weight).
