# 3. Don't waste energy

This chapter keeps only the energy and time accounts. It looks at four things: the attention you have available each day, how fast you react, how often you err, and how long it takes to return after an interruption. Every number here was measured directly - from experiments, or from records kept while following groups of people (cohort studies). None of these numbers is converted into lifespan or mortality. Most entries in this chapter are evidence grade B. That's just how this field is; it doesn't mean the advice fails. Entry 20 is about what to expect when dealing with police, doctors and clerks - it cites regulations, not experiments, so it's graded C. Entry 15 (treat hopeless thoughts as a symptom) and entry 23 (treat "others demand perfection from me" as a symptom) cite mortality and suicide-risk figures - a different lens from the rest of the chapter, so they aren't compared with the others.

### 1. Turn off nonessential notifications, and put your phone out of sight while working
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no benefit=mid lens=time -->
- Cost: Free. A few minutes changing settings, almost no willpower.
- TL;DR: A phone buzz - even if you don't look or touch - drops your performance on attention tasks. The drop is about the same as if you had actually taken the call or texted. There's another case too: your phone just sitting on the desk while you resist checking it still shrinks the spare capacity your brain has. The more dependent you are on your phone, the bigger the drop.
- Benefit: Merely receiving a notification - without looking or touching - significantly worsened performance on an attention task, by about as much as actually answering calls or texting. This was done in a lab with university students. Two other experiments showed that even when people successfully resisted checking, having their own phone nearby reduced available cognitive capacity. The most phone-dependent people dropped the most.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Stothart, Mitchum & Yehnert (2015). The attentional cost of receiving a cell phone notification. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance. <https://doi.org/10.1037/xhp0000100> ; Ward, Duke, Gneezy & Bos (2017). Brain Drain: The Mere Presence of One's Own Smartphone Reduces Available Cognitive Capacity. Journal of the Association for Consumer Research. <https://doi.org/10.1086/691462>
- Notes: All of these are single lab tasks. What years of daily accumulation do has never been measured. Jobs that genuinely need you reachable can keep notifications from a handful of contacts.

### 2. Fix your wake-up time - weekends too
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some benefit=mid lens=time -->
- Cost: Free. Sleeping less late on weekends takes a bit of willpower.
- TL;DR: The fifth of people with the most irregular schedules run body clocks about 2.5 hours behind the most regular fifth - effectively living in permanent jetlag. The more regular a student's schedule, the better their grades.
- Benefit: 61 university students were tracked for 30 days. The most irregular fifth had body clocks (circadian rhythms) about 2.5 hours later than the most regular fifth - specifically, melatonin onset at 00:08 versus 21:32. Their sleepiest, most-wanting-to-sleep window was about 1.8 hours later too. The more regular the schedule, the better the academic performance (correlation r = 0.37).
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Phillips et al. (2017). Irregular sleep/wake patterns are associated with poorer academic performance and delayed circadian and sleep/wake timing. Scientific Reports. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-017-03171-4>
- Notes: Only one study, small, no randomized groups. The researchers reasoned that the clock difference comes mainly from different daily light exposure - so a fixed wake time only works together with morning light. For how to recover after an occasional late night, see entry 39 of chapter 2 (catch up on sleep the very next night): the method is an earlier bedtime that night, not sleeping till noon the next day.

### 3. Sleep 7 to 8 hours a night - don't treat 6 as enough
<!-- tags: money=0 time=high will=some benefit=big lens=time -->
- Cost: Free. You have to set aside 1-2 more hours for sleep each day. Getting to bed earlier takes a bit of willpower.
- TL;DR: Two straight weeks of 6-hour nights drags mental performance down to the level of two nights with no sleep at all. The trouble is you can't feel it. Self-rated sleepiness stops rising after the first few days, and the 6-hour group rates themselves exactly as sleepy as the 4-hour group. So "6 hours is enough for me" is itself a symptom of sleep deprivation.
- Benefit: 48 healthy adults aged 21 to 38 slept only 6 hours a night for 14 consecutive days. Their cognitive performance declined cumulatively to the level of 2 straight nights of total sleep deprivation. Meanwhile their self-rated sleepiness rose only in the first few days, then barely moved. The 6-hour and 4-hour groups reported no difference in sleepiness. This was a randomized controlled trial (RCT). Separately, short-term sleeplessness (acute sleep deprivation, under 48 hours) showed an effect size g = -0.776 on simple attention lapses and g = -0.125 on reasoning accuracy. That part pools 70 studies.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Van Dongen, Maislin, Mullington & Dinges (2003). The cumulative cost of additional wakefulness. Sleep. <https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/26.2.117> ; Lim & Dinges (2010). A meta-analysis of the impact of short-term sleep deprivation on cognitive variables. Psychological Bulletin. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0018883>
- Notes: The key point: "I don't feel sleepy" is not evidence you've slept enough. Chronic 6-hour sleepers just get used to the feeling - their performance doesn't follow. The pooled-study part concerns whole nights without sleep. "Chronic short sleep" rests on a single randomized controlled trial.

### 4. No caffeine after 2 PM
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some benefit=big lens=time -->
- Cost: Free. Habitual afternoon coffee drinkers have to resist - a bit of willpower.
- TL;DR: A large coffee (400 mg) six hours before bed cuts over an hour of that night's sleep as measured by instruments. Yet your own sleep diary shows no difference. An ordinary coffee (107 mg) has to be finished at least 8.8 hours before bed to leave sleep untouched. For an 11 PM bedtime, 2 PM is the line.
- Benefit: 400 mg of caffeine 6 hours before bed reduced instrument-measured total sleep time by 1.1 to 1.2 hours versus placebo (a caffeine-free control drink). Yet the subjects' own sleep diaries reported no difference - they couldn't feel it. Pooling multiple studies (meta-analysis): caffeine cuts total sleep time by 45 minutes on average, and sleep efficiency - the share of time in bed actually asleep - by 7%. A cup of coffee (107 mg) must be finished 8.8+ hours before bed to avoid affecting sleep.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Drake, Roehrs, Shambroom & Roth (2013). Caffeine effects on sleep taken 0, 3, or 6 hours before going to bed. Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.3170> ; Gardiner et al. (2023). The effect of caffeine on subsequent sleep: A systematic review and meta-analysis. Sleep Medicine Reviews. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2023.101764>
- Notes: The Drake study was done in a single lab, and 400 mg is on the large side - roughly two or three coffees. Slow caffeine metabolizers need an even earlier cutoff. What this entry buys back is sleep duration, and it stacks with entry 3 (7 to 8 hours a night).

### 5. Batch your email and messages into a few fixed slots a day
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some benefit=mid lens=time -->
- Cost: Free. You'll spend a little time telling colleagues how often you reply. Resisting the urge to check constantly takes a bit of willpower.
- TL;DR: The same people were compared across two weeks. In the week when email was limited to 3 checks a day, daily stress was clearly lower - while the volume of mail sent and received was the same. Unrestricted, people checked 15.5 times a day on average.
- Benefit: 124 adults, compared against themselves across two weeks (within-subject design). In the week limited to 3 email checks a day, daily stress was significantly lower than in the unlimited week (effect size Cohen's d = 0.37). The number of emails received and answered didn't differ between weeks. At the start, unrestricted, people checked email 15.5 times a day on average.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Kushlev & Dunn (2015). Checking email less frequently reduces stress. Computers in Human Behavior. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2014.11.005>
- Notes: Only one study, a medium-small effect, and it measured stress, not output. Instant messaging has never been studied directly - that part is extrapolated by analogy.

### 6. During work that needs sustained thought, block even seconds-long interruptions
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no benefit=big lens=time -->
- Cost: Free. Close the door, wear headphones, or hang a "do not disturb" sign.
- TL;DR: An interruption averaging 2.8 seconds doubles the error rate on what you do next. 4.4 seconds triples it. Field tracking also found that an interrupted task takes 25 minutes on average to get back to, with two other tasks wedged in between. Interrupted people work faster - but their stress, frustration and effort all run higher.
- Benefit: An interruption averaging 2.8 seconds doubled the step error rate on sequential tasks (tasks done step by step in order). 4.4 seconds tripled it. That was lab work. Another study observed 24 office workers (information workers) in the field: they switched work topics every 11 minutes on average; 57% of topics were interrupted; returning to the interrupted task took 25 minutes 26 seconds on average that day; and 2.26 other tasks got wedged in between. A separate 48-person experiment found interrupted people worked faster but reported higher stress, frustration, time pressure and effort.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Altmann, Trafton & Hambrick (2014). Momentary interruptions can derail the train of thought. Journal of Experimental Psychology: General. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0030986> ; Mark, Gonzalez & Harris (2005). No task left behind? Examining the nature of fragmented work. CHI 2005. <https://doi.org/10.1145/1054972.1055017> ; Mark, Gudith & Klocke (2008). The cost of interrupted work: More speed and stress. CHI 2008. <https://doi.org/10.1145/1357054.1357072>
- Notes: That field study splits interruptions into two kinds: others coming to you, and ones you open yourself. A closed door only blocks the first kind. The second kind needs entry 1 (turn off nonessential notifications) and entry 5 (batch messages) working together with this one.
### 7. Do one thing at a time - don't answer messages while in meetings
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some benefit=mid lens=time -->
- Cost: Free. Not answering messages in meetings takes a bit of willpower.
- TL;DR: Every task switch slows reactions and adds errors. People who habitually run several screens at once haven't trained themselves into multitasking ability - they do worse on task-switching tests.
- Benefit: After a task switch, reactions are measurably slower and errors more frequent. That part comes from a review of multiple studies. Another lab study of university students: people who habitually juggle several media at once - heavy media multitaskers - get pulled away more easily by irrelevant stimuli and irrelevant memories, and do worse on task-switching tests.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Monsell (2003). Task switching. Trends in Cognitive Sciences. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S1364-6613(03)00028-7> ; Ophir, Nass & Wagner (2009). Cognitive control in media multitaskers. PNAS. <https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0903620106>
- Notes: How much switching costs varies enormously with the work, so no single number is given here. The Ophir study is a snapshot comparison - it can't tell whether multitasking degrades attention or whether people with poorer attention multitask more.

### 8. No glowing screens in the last hour before bed - read paper or an e-ink screen instead
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=yes benefit=mid lens=time -->
- Cost: Free. Putting the phone down for the last hour of the day means fighting inertia long-term - that takes willpower.
- TL;DR: Reading a glowing screen before bed means falling asleep about 10 minutes later than reading paper. Melatonin is suppressed by 55%. The next day your body clock is pushed back over 1.5 hours, you wake groggier, and full alertness takes longer.
- Benefit: In a lab, the same people cycled through both conditions (a crossover trial). Reading a light-emitting e-reader before bed meant falling asleep about 10 minutes slower than reading a paper book (25.7 versus 15.8 minutes). Evening melatonin was suppressed 55%. The next day's body clock (circadian rhythm) shifted back over 1.5 hours - melatonin onset at 22:31 versus 21:01. The next morning brought more sleepiness and a longer path to full alertness.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Chang, Aeschbach, Duffy & Czeisler (2015). Evening use of light-emitting eReaders negatively affects sleep, circadian timing, and next-morning alertness. PNAS. <https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1418490112>
- Notes: Small sample, done under controlled lab conditions. At everyday phone brightness and usage duration, the effect is probably smaller. How much a phone's night mode helps, this study didn't measure.

### 9. Sleep when it's time - don't stay up for games, short videos, or porn
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=yes benefit=big lens=time -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is fighting the "one more episode" inertia long-term. Keeping the phone out of the bedroom is the most effort-saving fix.
- TL;DR: In 2025 the average Chinese sleeper got 6.97 hours a night, falling asleep at 0:10 on average. People who use phones or tablets before bed have more than double the odds of insufficient sleep and 2.7 times the odds of daytime sleepiness. Devices merely present in the bedroom, unused, show nearly the same numbers. So the lowest-effort fix is: no phone in the bedroom.
- Benefit: In 2025 Chinese residents averaged 6.97 hours of nighttime sleep, with average sleep onset at 0:10 (Chinese Sleep Research Society). People using portable devices before bed had about 2.17 times the odds of insufficient sleep (OR 2.17), 1.46 times the odds of poor sleep quality (OR 1.46), and 2.72 times the odds of daytime sleepiness (OR 2.72). Devices merely present in the bedroom, not used, showed similar numbers - about 1.79, 1.53 and 2.27 times (OR 1.79/1.53/2.27). That part pools 20 snapshot surveys covering 125,198 children and adolescents. Another snapshot survey: among 844 adults aged 18 to 94, those still on phones after lights-out took longer to fall asleep, spent a smaller share of time in bed actually asleep, and were more fatigued by day.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Carter et al. (2016). Association Between Portable Screen-Based Media Device Access or Use and Sleep Outcomes. JAMA Pediatrics. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2016.2341> ; Exelmans & Van den Bulck (2016). Bedtime mobile phone use and sleep in adults. Social Science & Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2015.11.037> ; Chinese Sleep Research Society (2026). 2026 China Sleep Health Research White Paper (Health News report reposted by the Fujian Provincial Health Commission). <https://wjw.fujian.gov.cn/jggk/csxx/xcc/mtbd/202603/t20260323_7114382.htm> ; Grubbs JB et al. (2018). Pornography Problems Due to Moral Incongruence: An Integrative Model with a Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis. Archives of Sexual Behavior. <https://doi.org/10.1007/s10508-018-1248-x> (disputing side one, cited in Notes) ; Prause N, Pfaus J (2015). Viewing Sexual Stimuli Associated with Greater Sexual Responsiveness, Not Erectile Dysfunction. Sexual Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1002/sm2.58> (disputing side two, cited in Notes)
- Notes: Disputed. For the cost of staying up itself, see entry 3 of this chapter (sleep 7 to 8 hours). What porn does to an ordinary person's energy and cognition - no reliable evidence says whether it's the cause of anything. One analysis pooling multiple studies argues that so-called "porn problems" come more from a mismatch between a person's beliefs and their own behavior than from the content itself. Another study pooled data from 280 men and found no relationship between viewing duration and erectile function during partnered sex. This entry targets only its encroachment on sleep time. ### 10. Make no major decisions and send no important messages late at night
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some benefit=small lens=time -->
- Cost: Free. You just push the matter to after you've slept.
- TL;DR: People kept awake 49.5 hours did a risk-decision experiment. Well-rested, they quickly learned to avoid the high-risk options; sleep-deprived, they kept picking the risky ones. The older the person, the bigger the effect. That experiment tested the extreme of two straight nights without sleep; the impact of losing a few hours was never measured directly - but the direction says push important decisions to the next day.
- Benefit: 34 healthy people did the Iowa Gambling Task, an experiment measuring risky decision-making, after 49.5 hours without sleep. Rested, they quickly learned to avoid high-risk options; deprived, they chose high-risk options more often. Older people were more affected. This was an experiment, and a single study.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Killgore, Balkin & Wesensten (2006). Impaired decision making following 49 h of sleep deprivation. Journal of Sleep Research. <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2869.2006.00487.x>
- Notes: The study tested the extreme of two straight nights awake. What an ordinary 2 AM night does was never measured directly. The "don't send messages" half is the author's experience (grade C) - sending it the next day is never too late.

### 11. When afternoon drowsiness hits, nap 10 minutes - not half an hour
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no benefit=mid lens=time -->
- Cost: Free. Ten minutes, plus somewhere to lie or lean.
- TL;DR: A 10-minute nap refreshes immediately and the effect holds two and a half hours. Five minutes is basically useless. Twenty minutes takes half an hour to kick in. Thirty minutes leaves you groggy on waking, performing worse than no nap at all for a while.
- Benefit: After a short night, a 10-minute nap immediately improved sleepiness, fatigue, vigor and cognitive performance, with part of the benefit lasting beyond 155 minutes. Five minutes brought almost nothing. Twenty minutes' benefits appeared only after 35 minutes. Thirty minutes first produced sleep inertia - a stretch of worse performance right after waking - and improvement came only later.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Brooks & Lack (2006). A brief afternoon nap following nocturnal sleep restriction: Which nap duration is most recuperative? Sleep. <https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/29.6.831>
- Notes: A single lab study, all healthy young subjects. If you can't fall asleep, don't force it. Whether lying with eyes closed for 10 minutes helps has no research support. Naps over an hour are linked to higher mortality and coronary risk instead - see entry 38 of chapter 2 (keep naps under half an hour).

### 12. In an open-plan office, use earplugs or find a quiet room for memory work
<!-- tags: money=low time=low will=no benefit=small lens=time -->
- Cost: Earplugs run a few dozen yuan. Booking a quiet meeting room takes a little time.
- TL;DR: Background noise rising from 39 to 51 decibels means fewer words remembered, more fatigue, and less willingness to work. During breaks, watching a nature film with sound restores energy better than continuing to hear office noise.
- Benefit: Students worked in a simulated open-plan office. When background noise rose from 39 to 51 decibels (dB, LAeq), they remembered fewer words, rated themselves more fatigued, and were less willing to work. During breaks, watching a nature film with sound restored energy ratings better than just hearing river sounds or office noise.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Jahncke, Hygge, Halin, Green & Dimberg (2011). Open-plan office noise: Cognitive performance and restoration. Journal of Environmental Psychology. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jenvp.2011.07.002>
- Notes: A single lab study. The most disruptive part of noise is speech you can make out. White-noise earphones help with that, though this study didn't test earphones.

### 13. Keep your work week under 49 hours
<!-- tags: money=low time=low will=some benefit=big lens=time -->
- Cost: Possibly less overtime pay. Possibly some effort negotiating with your manager.
- TL;DR: Output records from a British munitions factory in WWI show: under 49 hours a week, output matches hours put in. Past 49 hours, the growth rate starts falling. A 70-hour week produces almost the same as a 56-hour week - the extra 14 hours are pure waste.
- Benefit: Output records of British munitions workers during WWI: under 49 hours a week, output is proportional to hours. Past 49, the growth rate slows. Maximum output arrives at about 63 hours. A 70-hour week produces almost exactly what a 56-hour week does - the extra 14 hours are wasted.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Pencavel (2015). The Productivity of Working Hours. The Economic Journal. <https://doi.org/10.1111/ecoj.12166>
- Notes: The data comes from a factory a century ago, doing physical work, staffed mostly by women. Whether the same threshold holds for knowledge work is unknown - the author himself says other kinds of work may have higher or lower thresholds. What's reliable is the direction: past some point, more hours produce almost nothing more. The specific number 49 is not reliable.

### 14. When you catch yourself replaying the same bad thing, switch to a hands-on task
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some benefit=mid lens=time -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is noticing you're replaying (that's called rumination) - noticing itself takes practice.
- TL;DR: Replaying the same bad thing never produces an answer. It deepens depression, nails negative thoughts in tighter, makes problems harder to solve and action harder to take, and wears out the people around you who were willing to listen. When you catch yourself going in circles, go do something with your hands.
- Benefit: A review of multiple studies says replaying the same bad thing deepens depression. This kind of replaying is also called rumination. It reinforces negative thinking, weakens problem-solving, obstructs real action, and wears out the people around you willing to listen. It's linked to anxiety, binge eating, heavy drinking and self-harm.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Nolen-Hoeksema, Wisco & Lyubomirsky (2008). Rethinking Rumination. Perspectives on Psychological Science. <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-6924.2008.00088.x>
- Notes: This is the chapter's hardest entry to put a number on - the review gives direction only. "Switch tasks" is the author's own practice. All the literature supports is one sentence: diverting attention beats continuing to circle. What to switch to, nobody has compared.

### 15. Treat thoughts like "things will definitely get worse" as a symptom, not a fact
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some benefit=mid lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is treating the thought as a symptom rather than a fact.
- TL;DR: In a 20-year twin study, each step up in pessimism meant about 13% higher same-period mortality and about 20% higher cardiovascular death. Conversely, a high optimism score brought no matching benefit. So what needs handling is pessimism, not "insufficient positivity".
- Benefit: 2,978 Australian twins over 50, followed on average 20 years, with 1,068 deaths. Each 1 standard deviation higher on the pessimism scale meant about 13% higher same-period mortality risk (hazard ratio 1.134), plausible range 95% CI 1.065-1.207, P=8.85×10⁻⁵. Cardiovascular death about 20% higher (1.196, 1.045-1.368, P=0.0093). No link with cancer death. Optimism scores showed no clear link with mortality either.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Whitfield JB, Zhu G, Landers JG, Martin NG (2020). Pessimism is associated with greater all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, but optimism is not protective. Scientific Reports, 10, 12609. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-69388-y>
- Notes: Disputed. This is observation only, no randomization. Some argue "illness came first, pessimism second" - the authors counter that if so, cancer death should correlate too. Unmeasured factors remain possible. Note that optimism showed no protection - so don't force yourself to "think positive". The task is to stop reading pessimistic expectations as accurate forecasts of the future.

### 16. Cut back relationships that drain you; learn to decline requests you don't want
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=yes benefit=mid lens=time -->
- Cost: Free. Short-term social friction, possibly losing some relationships. Saying no out loud takes real willpower.
- TL;DR: The damage from one person who drains you is bigger and more consistent than the benefit from one person who supports you. So subtract first, then add.
- Benefit: A survey of 120 widowed women aged 60 to 89: unpleasant interactions correlated with psychological well-being more consistently and more strongly than pleasant ones. In other words, one draining person costs more than one supportive person gives.
- Evidence: C
- Sources: Rook (1984). The negative side of social interaction: Impact on psychological well-being. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.46.5.1097>
- Notes: The respondents were a special population, and it was a one-time questionnaire - no before-and-after. It measured well-being, not energy. Placing it in this chapter is the author's inference. There's no objective standard for which relationships count as "draining" - you can only judge yourself.

### 17. Fix the low-value decisions like what to wear and what to eat
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no benefit=small lens=time -->
- Cost: Free. It takes a little time up front to set the defaults.
- TL;DR: This entry has no hard evidence. The original "making choices drains willpower" experiment failed to reproduce in two large-scale replications - one with two thousand people, one with three thousand, across many labs, with effects of essentially zero. Fixing what you wear and eat is still worth doing - the reason is saving those minutes and moments of dithering, not "saving willpower".
- Benefit: Not quantifiable. The original experiments claimed that choosing and self-control consume a shared finite resource (ego depletion). But two large replications - both pre-registered, many labs repeating the original procedure - failed to reproduce the result. One was 23 labs, 2,141 people, effect size d = 0.04, 95% CI [-0.07, 0.15] (the plausible range). The other, 36 labs, 3,531 people, d = 0.06. Differences too small to count.
- Evidence: C
- Sources: Baumeister, Bratslavsky, Muraven & Tice (1998). Ego depletion: Is the active self a limited resource? Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.74.5.1252> ; Hagger et al. (2016). A Multilab Preregistered Replication of the Ego-Depletion Effect. Perspectives on Psychological Science. <https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691616652873> ; Vohs et al. (2021). A Multisite Preregistered Paradigmatic Test of the Ego-Depletion Effect. Psychological Science. <https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797621989733>
- Notes: Disputed. "Decision fatigue" is treated as settled in popular books, but most replications of the original experiment fail to reproduce it. This entry survives only because it costs almost nothing and the time saved is real. Don't expect it to improve your afternoon judgment.

### 18. When angry, leave the scene first - treat the other person as weather, not an enemy
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some benefit=mid lens=time -->
- Cost: Free. In the moment you have to hold back from retorting, turn and walk away, and decide later whether to deal with it. That takes some practice.
- TL;DR: Reinterpreting what just happened, or taking the other person's perspective for a moment, both genuinely lower the emotion - the latter works better. Suppressing it outwardly does nothing. Also, people who punished their offender ended up ruminating longer; those who didn't retaliate moved on faster. The satisfaction of revenge is overestimated in advance.
- Benefit: Pooling 306 experimental comparisons (meta-analysis): reinterpreting what happened (cognitive reappraisal) had an effect on emotion of d = 0.36. Taking the other's perspective, d = 0.45. Distraction, d = 0.27. Suppressing the feeling was ineffective (d = -0.04). Three separate experiments showed people who punished an offender ruminated about them longer afterward; people who didn't punish "turned the page" faster. People overestimate beforehand how good revenge will feel.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Webb TL, Miles E, Sheeran P (2012). Dealing with feeling: A meta-analysis of the effectiveness of strategies derived from the process model of emotion regulation. Psychological Bulletin. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0027600> ; Carlsmith KM, Wilson TD, Gilbert DT (2008). The paradoxical consequences of revenge. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0012165>
- Notes: The pooled part measured emotional intensity, not energy - effects small to medium. The revenge part rests on one set of experiments, hence grade B. The "empty boat" is a fable from Zhuangzi's "The Mountain Tree": an empty boat ramming you doesn't anger you, a boat with someone aboard does - yet the impact is the same. It's an analogy, not evidence. If you've suffered a real loss, take the legal paths in chapters 8 and 9 - far cheaper than exploding on the spot.

### 19. When your mood sinks, do the highest-value things first: move, get sunlight, sleep on time, talk to someone, call 12356
<!-- tags: money=0 time=mid will=some benefit=big lens=time -->
- Cost: Free, or nearly so. A few brisk walks, jogs or strength sessions a week. Daylight outside during the day. A fixed sleep schedule. One phone call when thoughts turn dark.
- TL;DR: For depression, an analysis pooling 218 randomized trials found brisk walking and jogging work best, yoga and strength training close behind, with greater intensity more effective. Light therapy is strongly effective for seasonal depression and also works for the non-seasonal kind. Online self-guided cognitive behavioral courses work, and guided ones work better when symptoms run heavier. When thoughts turn dark, call 12356 first.
- Benefit: Pooling 218 randomized controlled trials with 14,170 people (network meta-analysis), against control groups doing other activities. Walking or jogging's effect size on depression was g = -0.62 (95% credible interval -0.80 to -0.45). Yoga -0.55, strength training -0.49. Greater intensity, greater effect. Pooling multiple light-therapy studies: effect size 0.84 (0.60 to 1.08) for seasonal depression, 0.53 (0.18 to 0.89) for non-seasonal. Another analysis combined patient-by-patient data across studies of internet-based cognitive behavioral therapy - online self-help courses. Both guided and unguided versions beat control groups, and guided beat unguided in moderate-to-severe cases (PHQ-9 above 9). 12356 is the national psychological assistance hotline, open at least 18 hours a day.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Noetel M et al. (2024). Effect of exercise for depression: systematic review and network meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials. BMJ. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2023-075847> ; Golden RN et al. (2005). The efficacy of light therapy in the treatment of mood disorders: a review and meta-analysis of the evidence. American Journal of Psychiatry. <https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.162.4.656> ; Karyotaki E et al. (2021). Internet-Based Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Depression: A Systematic Review and Individual Patient Data Network Meta-analysis. JAMA Psychiatry. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2020.4364> ; National Health Commission (2024). Notice on applying "12356" as the unified national psychological assistance hotline number (Guoweiyizheng Letter [2024] No. 259). <https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/202412/content_6994470.htm>
- Notes: These are all adjuncts for mild-to-moderate depression. Moderate-to-severe cases and anyone with suicidal thoughts need medical care - call 12356 first (see entry 25 of chapter 1). The exercise trials were mostly small, and you can't blind who's exercising the way you blind a pill. The light-therapy research only goes to 2003, with small samples. "Sleep on time" and "talk to someone" are the author's experience, without their own literature. Don't turn "get moving" into a demand on a depressed person - a ten-minute walk counts.

### 20. Treat police, doctors and clerks as people working by rules, not as roles: what moves things is paperwork and deadlines, not emotion
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some benefit=small lens=time -->
- Cost: Free. What changes is your expectations and your order of actions. The first time, you have to resist the urge to "argue until they agree".
- TL;DR: A child's view is "police catch bad guys, doctors save people". The adult view should be: they are people working by the rules of their posts, boxed in by paperwork, deadlines and supervision. Whether their attitude is nice and whether your matter gets handled - and in how many days - are basically two separate things. So what to do is get the receipt, note the deadline, appeal through the proper channel - not argue until the other side agrees.
- Benefit: Swap your moves when dealing with institutions: from "plead, get angry, retell your grievance over and over" to "get the paperwork, note the deadline, appeal through the channel". What you save is the time of repeated communication and repeated rumination. This is a one-time mindset adjustment, not a daily time-saver. What can be checked against documents is the deadlines themselves - they're all written down. For public-security cases, the handling deadline is no more than 30 days from acceptance; major and complex cases can be extended 30 days with approval from the next level up. When a case with a complainant is decided not to be filed, the non-filing notice must be served within 3 days. The complainant may apply for reconsideration within 7 days, and the public security organ must decide within 30 days. If still unsatisfied, apply to the next level up for review within another 7 days.
- Evidence: C
- Sources: Ministry of Public Security (2020). Provisions on the Procedures for Handling Criminal Cases by Public Security Organs (as amended by MPS Order No. 159, Articles 171, 178, 179). <http://www.gov.cn/gongbao/content/2020/content_5549884.htm> ; Ministry of Public Security (2013/2019 amended). Provisions on the Procedures for Handling Administrative Cases by Public Security Organs (MPS Orders No. 125, No. 149). <http://www.gov.cn/gongbao/content/2013/content_2321117.htm>
- Notes: Why a C: the judgment that "many people see the person behind the window as a role rather than someone at work" has no citable literature. Only the rules half is citable: deadlines, paperwork and remedy channels are all in writing. Every number in this entry comes from the two MPS regulations cited in entry 39 of chapter 8 (what happens after you call the police). This entry doesn't fix "the other side has a bad attitude", and it doesn't promise that following the process always works. All it swaps out is your expectations and your first move. Bank tellers and government service windows are the same: their operations leave traces, require review, and errors get traced back. So the right moves are keeping receipts, asking for written confirmations, and complaining through channels - not guessing "will they screw me". Legitimate priorities are written out in the open - for instance, emergency care is triaged by condition, not first-come-first-served (entry 8 of chapter 24, emergency pre-screening and triage). To thank a doctor who saved you, use thank-you letters and satisfaction surveys - see entry 12 of chapter 24. "Sending something so they'll care more", aimed at someone handling or enforcing a case, is the crime of offering bribes, and explicitly punished more severely - see entry 40 of chapter 8. Blatant differential treatment is a target for complaints and supervision, not an unwritten rule to imitate.

### 21. Don't make "how everyone else is doing" daily required reading: limit or quit the apps where you scroll peers' updates
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=yes benefit=big lens=time -->
- Cost: Free. Setting an app time limit or deactivating an account takes minutes. The hard part is not opening them again - fighting inertia long-term.
- TL;DR: People who habitually compare upward online have worse mood and lower self-esteem - a medium-strength correlation across 36 thousand-plus people. Correlation isn't causation, but there are randomized trials too: four weeks off Facebook raised well-being slightly. Another limited three platforms to 10 minutes a day each, and after three weeks loneliness and depression both dropped. Count the cost together: during those four weeks off, knowledge of current events also fell noticeably.
- Benefit: Pooling multiple studies (meta-analysis): 54 independent samples, 94 effect sizes, N = 36,583, using a three-level random-effects model. The average correlation between online upward social comparison and psychological maladjustment was r = 0.330 (95% CI 0.289-0.370, the plausible range). By specific outcome: fear of negative evaluation-type social-evaluative negative affect 0.438, anxiety 0.382, depression 0.306, low well-being 0.268, low self-esteem 0.263. Age, cultural background and year of data collection didn't change the strength. Longitudinal studies came out no stronger than snapshot surveys. Separately, a randomized controlled trial of 2,743 American Facebook users: 61% were willing to deactivate for under $102. Participants were randomized into a four-week deactivation group and a control group. Deactivation freed 60 minutes a day for the average participant. The subjective well-being index rose 0.09 standard deviations - life satisfaction 0.12, anxiety 0.10, depression 0.09, happiness 0.08. These held up after correcting for chance hits across multiple comparisons. The authors give a benchmark: positive-psychology interventions lift subjective well-being about +0.34 standard deviations and cut depression about -0.23 - so this effect is about 25% to 40% of a standard psychological intervention. The cost over the same period: the current-events knowledge index fell 0.19 standard deviations, and political polarization fell 0.16. After the experiment, the deactivation group still used Facebook 12 minutes a day less than controls (0.31 standard deviations, a 23% drop). Another randomized controlled trial of 143 undergraduates split them into two groups: one limited Facebook, Instagram and Snapchat to 10 minutes a day each; the other used as usual. After three weeks, the limited group's loneliness and depression fell significantly. Both groups' anxiety and fear of missing out were lower than at the start.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Lei Y, Hu S, Sun Y, Zheng L (2026). "Looking up" linked to feeling down: a meta-analysis of online upward social comparison and psychological maladjustment. Frontiers in Psychology. <https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1825169> ; Allcott H, Braghieri L, Eichmeyer S, Gentzkow M (2020). The Welfare Effects of Social Media. American Economic Review, 110(3), 629-676. <https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20190658> ; Allcott H, Braghieri L, Eichmeyer S, Gentzkow M (2019). The Welfare Effects of Social Media. NBER Working Paper No. 25514 (the specific figures cited in this entry were checked verbatim against this version; the published abstract contains no figures). <https://www.nber.org/papers/w25514> ; Hunt MG, Marx R, Lipson C, Young J (2018). No More FOMO: Limiting Social Media Decreases Loneliness and Depression. Journal of Social and Clinical Psychology, 37(10), 751-768. <https://doi.org/10.1521/jscp.2018.37.10.751>
- Notes: The "correlation" half only says two things appear together - don't read it as cause, and the authors themselves say more longitudinal research is needed. The randomized half can speak to causation, but its subjects were American Facebook users before the 2018 midterms - transposing it onto Chinese platforms is a stretch. The Hunt study has only 143 undergraduates, and it's a single study. The effects are all small - don't expect quitting an app to fix your mood. What this entry blocks is the "watching how others are doing" kind of use. Keeping in touch with family and friends, looking things up, job hunting - those aren't it. If you truly can't hold back, start by killing push notifications, "people you may know", and the recommendation feed. For the total screen-time account, see entries 15 and 16 of chapter 4 (hard caps on short video, no scrolling news). For what to do first when your mood sinks, see entry 19 of this chapter (move, sunlight, sleep on time). For buying things to recover a sense of identity, see entry 23 of chapter 6. For spending more to "move up a tier", entry 24 of chapter 6.

### 22. When you feel "everyone saw me make a fool of myself", divide that estimate by two
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some benefit=small lens=time -->
- Cost: Free. Just one extra question when you're tense: did they really see it?
- TL;DR: People were made to wear an embarrassing T-shirt into a room with others. Wearers estimated 46% of onlookers could identify who was printed on it; the real figure was 23% - exactly double. With a self-chosen T-shirt they felt good about, the estimate was 48% against a real 8% - six times. Another line of studies found people systematically underestimate how much their conversation partners liked them. So the judgment "I blew that line and everyone remembers" is inflated to begin with - halving it still leaves it inflated.
- Benefit: Experiment one: participants wore a T-shirt printed with Barry Manilow's face into a room with other participants. Wearers estimated on average that 46% of onlookers could correctly identify who was on the shirt; the onlookers' actual rate was 23% - the estimate exactly double the reality. Subtracting actual from estimated session by session, the average overestimate was 23 percentage points (95% CI 9-38, the plausible range); excluding the two sessions with only two and three onlookers, 27 points (11-43). Experiment two: wearing self-chosen T-shirts participants considered positive. Average estimate 48%, actual onlooker rate 8% - six times. Another series of studies had three settings: strangers paired to chat, new college roommates, adult workshops. The result: people systematically underestimate how much their conversation partners like them and how much the partners enjoyed the conversation - the authors call it the "liking gap". It appears in conversations of different lengths and persisted for months among new roommates.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Gilovich T, Medvec VH, Savitsky K (2000). The spotlight effect in social judgment: An egocentric bias in estimates of the salience of one's own actions and appearance. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 78(2), 211-222. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.78.2.211> ; Boothby EJ, Cooney G, Sandstrom GM, Clark MS (2018). The Liking Gap in Conversations: Do People Like Us More Than We Think? Psychological Science, 29(11), 1742-1756. <https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797618783714>
- Notes: Why a B: all are small lab and campus studies, with scenarios of wearing clothes and chatting with strangers. There is no direct evidence for "colleagues gossiping about me" or "saying the wrong thing in a meeting". The numbers show the direction and rough multiple of the overestimate - they aren't a coefficient you can apply. This entry doesn't say others' opinions never matter. In settings that genuinely evaluate you - interviews, thesis defenses, assessments, medical reports - the evaluation is real and you should prepare. What it targets is the everyday "everyone is watching me" feeling; there, your opponent is your own estimation bias.

### 23. Treat "others demand perfection from me" as a symptom, not a fact
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some benefit=small lens=mortality -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is treating the thought as something to handle, rather than as an accurate measurement of what others really demand.
- TL;DR: The feeling "people around me demand perfection, and falling short means rejection" correlates with suicidal thoughts at 0.28. Switch it to "I demand perfection from myself" and the correlation is only 0.10. The dangerous half isn't high standards themselves - it's feeling the standards are imposed by others. Even after stripping out pre-existing suicidal thoughts, the first kind of feeling still predicts more thoughts at follow-up.
- Benefit: Pooling multiple studies (meta-analysis): 45 studies, N = 11,747, covering undergraduates, medical students, community adults and psychiatric patients, using a random-effects model. Weighted average correlations with suicidal thoughts follow. Perfectionistic concerns r = .28 (95% CI .24-.32, the plausible range; 42 studies, 7,936 people). Perfectionistic concerns is a bundle including socially prescribed perfectionism, concern over mistakes, doubts about actions, and the gap between reality and standards. Socially prescribed perfectionism alone also came to r = .28 (.25-.32, 30 studies, 3,640 people). Perfectionistic strivings r = .10 (.07-.13, 31 studies, 4,588 people) - a bundle of self-oriented perfectionism and personal standards. Parental criticism r = .20 (.11-.29). Now the link with suicide attempts: perfectionistic concerns r = .12 (.07-.17, 15 studies, 5,275 people). Socially prescribed perfectionism r = .19 (.08-.29, 8 studies, 689 people). The longitudinal part: after stripping out suicidal thoughts present at the start, socially prescribed perfectionism still moved in the same direction as suicidal thoughts at later follow-ups, by a small margin. Self-oriented and other-oriented perfectionism showed no association.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Smith MM, Sherry SB, Chen S, Saklofske DH, Mushquash C, Flett GL, Hewitt PL (2018). The perniciousness of perfectionism: A meta-analytic review of the perfectionism-suicide relationship. Journal of Personality, 86(3), 522-542. <https://doi.org/10.1111/jopy.12333>
- Notes: Why a B: the included studies are mostly one-time questionnaires with no before-and-after. The correlation is moderate (r about .28), explaining only a few percent to a tenth of the variance. What was measured is suicidal thoughts and attempts, not death itself - so the benefit tier is "small" under the surrogate-endpoint rule. The authors themselves say the perfectionistic-concerns/suicide-attempt group looks affected by publication bias - studies with unflattering results never got published; after imputing the estimated missing studies, the direction held. This entry doesn't argue against high standards, and it doesn't tell you to lower your demands. The difference lies in where the standard comes from, and whether falling short makes you feel "I'm finished as a person". Like the pessimistic expectations in entry 15 (treat "things will definitely get worse" as a symptom), it can be handled as a symptom. If suicidal thoughts appear, call 12356 first and move lethal means out of reach - see entry 25 of chapter 1. For what you can do when someone near you shows such thoughts, see entry 15 of chapter 8. For depression screening on the child side, see entry 8 of chapter 30.
