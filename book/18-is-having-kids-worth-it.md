# 18. Is raising a child worth it?

This chapter works two ledgers: money and time. As with the marriage ledger in chapter 10, it only lays the accounts out for you - it does not decide for you. If you are already pregnant and need to handle the process step by step, see chapter 27.

### 1. Count what you can claim first: the national childcare subsidy is 3,600 yuan per child per year, paid until age 3
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no benefit=big lens=money -->
- Cost: Free. Apply where the child's household registration is; one trip gets it done
- TL;DR: If the child was born after January 1, 2025 and is not yet 3 years old, you can claim this money. One of the parents applies where the child's household registration is. Each child gets 3,600 yuan per year until age 3. The money is exempt from personal income tax. It also doesn't count as household income when you apply for assistance such as the subsistence allowance.
- Benefit: The plan issued by the General Office of the CPC Central Committee and the General Office of the State Council provides: infants and toddlers born on or after January 1, 2025 and under 3 years old receive "3,600 yuan per child per year." One of the child's parents or another guardian files the claim. The subsidy is "exempt from personal income tax." When assistance such as the subsistence allowance is assessed, it is "not counted as household or individual income"
- Evidence: A
- Sources: General Office of the CPC Central Committee, General Office of the State Council (2025). Implementation plan for the childcare subsidy system. <https://www.gov.cn/gongbao/2025/issue_12206/202508/content_7035435.html>
- Notes: This is the national floor. Some provinces and cities have their own local subsidies that stack on top of this one. The three years add up to 10,800 yuan - a very small slice of what raising a child costs, so don't treat it as a reason to have one or not

### 2. Maternity leave is 98 days; the maternity allowance is paid by the maternity insurance fund at the employer's average monthly wage from the previous year
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no benefit=big lens=money -->
- Cost: Free. The employer files the claim; you don't have to run around yourself
- TL;DR: Giving birth gets you at least 98 days of maternity leave, of which 15 can be taken before the birth. A difficult delivery adds 15 days. Each additional baby in a multiple birth also adds 15 days. The money paid during this time is called the maternity allowance, and it comes from the maternity insurance fund. If your employer didn't enroll you in maternity insurance, the employer pays it itself.
- Benefit: The length of maternity leave is set by the State Council. "A female employee who gives birth is entitled to 98 days of maternity leave, of which 15 days may be taken before the birth; for a difficult delivery, maternity leave is increased by 15 days; for a multiple birth, maternity leave is increased by 15 days for each additional baby." The money received during maternity leave is called the maternity allowance. For those enrolled in maternity insurance, it is "paid by the maternity insurance fund at the standard of the employer's average monthly wage of employees in the previous year." For those not enrolled, the employer pays you at your pre-leave wage standard
- Evidence: A
- Sources: State Council (2012). Special provisions on labor protection for female employees (State Council Order No. 619, Articles 7 and 8). <https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/2012-05/07/content_6584.htm>
- Notes: 98 days is the national minimum. Provincial population and family-planning regulations generally add another 60 days or more of bonus leave, bringing the usual total to at least 158 days. Which side pays wages during the bonus leave varies by province

### 3. Know this rule: your pay cannot be cut, and you cannot be fired, because of pregnancy, childbirth or breastfeeding
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no benefit=big lens=money -->
- Cost: Free
- TL;DR: During pregnancy, childbirth and breastfeeding, cutting your pay or firing you is illegal, carrying a fine of 1,000 to 5,000 yuan per person. Until the child turns one, you get one hour of breastfeeding time each day, and your employer cannot schedule you for overtime or night shifts. For how to claim, see chapter 19.
- Benefit: The State Council provides: "An employer shall not reduce the wages of a female employee, dismiss her, or terminate her labor or employment contract because of pregnancy, childbirth or breastfeeding." In other words, during these three periods your employer cannot cut your money, fire you or end your contract. While breastfeeding a baby under 1 year old, you get one hour of breastfeeding time each day. The employer also cannot extend your working hours or schedule you for night shifts. An employer that violates the above is fined 1,000 to 5,000 yuan per person
- Evidence: A
- Sources: State Council (2012). Special provisions on labor protection for female employees (State Council Order No. 619, Articles 5, 9 and 13). <https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/2012-05/07/content_6584.htm>
- Notes: If you really are illegally dismissed, the claim path is in chapter 19. The evidence that matters is the written notice of the transfer or pay cut and the chat records - keep both

### 4. Run the time ledger by "whose time gets taken," not by "how hard it feels"
<!-- tags: money=0 time=high will=yes benefit=big lens=time -->
- Cost: No money - what it spends is time. Before the child turns 3, your days are essentially fully occupied. The hard part is that this is every single day, not something you grit through for a few days
- TL;DR: Convert the daily hours of childcare and the nightly wake-ups into time. Then multiply by the hourly wage of the person doing that work. The number you get is usually far bigger than the cost of formula and diapers. So it's best to settle the division of labor before the birth.
- Benefit: Convert the daily hours spent caring for the child and the nightly wake-ups into hours. Then multiply by the hourly wage of the person doing that work. The result is usually far bigger than the cost of formula and diapers. Caregiving, cooking and getting up at night are unpaid work, but convert them into hours and multiply by the hourly wage all the same, and book them into the cost. The method is the same one chapter 10 uses for housework. Agree on the division of labor first, then decide whether to have a child
- Evidence: C
- Sources: Author's experience, no direct literature; the accounting approach is in chapter 10
- Notes: The point is not to land on an exact number. The point is for two people to sit down before the birth and talk through the division of labor over the same table of numbers, instead of fighting about it afterward

### 5. Run the money ledger in three stretches: ages 0 to 3, compulsory education, and after compulsory education
<!-- tags: money=high time=mid will=some benefit=big lens=money -->
- Cost: There is no fixed figure for what it costs - it varies hugely by city and by how you raise the child. The hard part is sitting down and running the three stretches separately instead of slapping down a number by feel
- TL;DR: The money for ages 0 to 3 goes mostly to how the child is cared for: by you, by the grandparents, or by hired help. From primary school through middle school, it goes mostly to housing and extracurriculars. After that, it goes mostly to the school-advancement track. The three stretches are spent in completely different ways; lump them together and you only get one scary total.
- Benefit: For ages 0 to 3, the big item is the care arrangement - raising the child yourself, having the grandparents do it, or hiring help. During compulsory education, that is primary through middle school, the big items are housing and extracurricular spending. After that, the big item is the advancement track. These three stretches buy completely different things; mixed together, they only produce one scary total
- Evidence: C
- Sources: Author's experience, no direct literature
- Notes: First count the money you can be sure of getting - the childcare subsidy, the maternity allowance, medical-insurance reimbursements - then look at the gap that's left. Don't decide based on the "it costs X million to raise a child" figures circulating online: their accounting basis is unclear, and you don't know what they included or left out

### 6. Having a child for your elders, for the marriage, or for old-age security: book each as its own line
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some benefit=mid lens=money -->
- Cost: No money - what it costs is one serious conversation. The hard part is saying it plainly, not skirting around it
- TL;DR: Other people wanting you to have a child can count as one of your considerations. But the time and the money go on your own ledger. "Raise a child to provide for old age" needs its own separate line, because it bets on another person's financial ability and willingness twenty years from now. Write down the consequences of not having a child too, and hold the two side by side.
- Benefit: The thinking is the same as in chapter 10. Other people's expectations can count as one of your considerations. But the costs go on your own ledger, not theirs. "Raise a child to provide for old age" in particular needs its own separate line, because what it assumes is another person's financial ability and willingness twenty years from now
- Evidence: C
- Sources: Author's experience, no direct literature; a similar analysis is in chapter 10
- Notes: Write down "the consequences of not having a child" too, and set them next to "the consequences of having one" for comparison. A ledger with only one side written down isn't accounting
