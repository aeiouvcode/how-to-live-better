# 4. Don't waste time

This chapter keeps only the time account. What it looks at: how many hours a day, week or year you can win back, or how much the on-time completion rate of a task can improve. Time is not converted into lifespan here, and not into money either. Where money is involved, only the method of calculation is given, not a conclusion. Most entries in this chapter rest on a single study or on aggregate statistics from time-use surveys, so grades run mostly B and C.

### 1. Rewrite "I plan to do it" as "at this time, in this place, if this happens, I do that"
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no benefit=big lens=time -->
- Cost: Free. One to two minutes per task, writing one "if... then..." sentence. For example: "Tuesday 8 AM, sitting down at my desk, I start with the introduction."
- TL;DR: Rewrite "I will do this" as "Tuesday 8 AM, sitting down at my desk, I start with the introduction". Pooling 94 tests, people who write it this way hit their goals at a clearly higher rate - a medium-to-large effect. All that changed is how one sentence is written; it costs no money and no effort.
- Benefit: Pooling 94 independent tests, people who first write their intention as "if... then..." achieve their goals at a higher rate. This form is called an implementation intention in psychology. Effect size d = 0.65, medium-to-large. It works at three points: getting started, holding on when disturbed, and pulling out of things that are bound to fail.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Gollwitzer, P. M., & Sheeran, P. (2006). Implementation intentions and goal achievement: A meta-analysis of effects and processes. Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 38, 69-119. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1>
- Notes: This trick works only for things you genuinely want to do. If you don't want the goal itself, no number of sentences helps. Also, these studies mostly recruited students and mostly looked at health habits - transposed to a work setting it may not work the same.

### 2. Write down your exit conditions before starting
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some benefit=big lens=time -->
- Cost: Free. Five minutes at kickoff to write "if indicator X isn't reached by date Y, stop". The hard part is honoring it when the day comes - stopping in front of people stings a little.
- TL;DR: Before starting, write one sentence: if indicator X isn't reached by date Y, stop. Pooled studies show this form really does help people pull out of things bound to fail. What you save is all the time you would have kept pouring in.
- Benefit: Pooling multiple implementation-intention studies: a pre-set "if... then..." also helps people disengage from failing courses of action - the original phrasing. The time saved is everything you would otherwise have kept investing.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Gollwitzer, P. M., & Sheeran, P. (2006). Implementation intentions and goal achievement: A meta-analysis of effects and processes. Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 38, 69-119. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1>
- Notes: Set exit conditions at kickoff, while emotions are still cool. Set them later and the costs already sunk will skew you - the right frame is future input versus future return only, see entry 3. Also, "willingness to pull out" is just one sub-conclusion of a pooled analysis; nobody has separately computed how much time it saves.

### 3. When deciding whether to continue, look only at future input and future return - not at what you've already put in
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some benefit=mid lens=time -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is accepting one thing: the time already invested is gone and won't come back.
- TL;DR: Money and time already invested make people want to keep going. Pooling 98 datasets, this tendency holds steadily. So when judging whether to continue, count only what you'll still have to put in and what you can still get back. What's already spent doesn't enter the calculation.
- Benefit: Money, effort and time already invested make people want to continue. In psychology this is called the sunk cost effect. Pooling 98 datasets, the analysis confirms the tendency is stable. Avoiding it saves the stretch of future time that "not wanting to waste" would have tied up.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Arkes, H. R., & Blumer, C. (1985). The psychology of sunk cost. Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 35, 124-140. <https://doi.org/10.1016/0749-5978(85)90049-4> ; Roth, S., Robbert, T., & Straus, L. (2015). On the sunk-cost effect in economic decision-making: A meta-analytic review. Business Research, 8(1), 99-138. <https://doi.org/10.1007/s40685-014-0014-8>
- Notes: For the Arkes and Blumer theater experiment, we verified only the direction: people who paid more for their tickets attended more plays over the half year. The per-group show counts haven't been checked against the original (TODO unverified). Roth et al. found the effect fades with time, and older people are less affected.

### 4. Estimate a deadline from how long similar tasks actually took before - not by reasoning from the plan
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no benefit=big lens=time -->
- Cost: Free. A few minutes before estimating, to check how long the last similar thing actually took.
- TL;DR: Students estimated 33.9 days to finish their theses; they actually took 55.5, and only 29.7% finished within their own estimate. Another group, asked to first recall how long the last similar task took before estimating, lifted on-time completion from 29.3% to 60.0%.
- Benefit: Students predicted 33.9 days to complete their theses and actually used 55.5. Only 29.7% finished on their own estimate. Researchers asked another group to first connect past experience with the task at hand before estimating. That group's on-time rate rose from the control group's 29.3% to 60.0%. The optimism that underestimates durations no longer appeared in that group.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Buehler, R., Griffin, D., & Ross, M. (1994). Exploring the "planning fallacy": Why people underestimate their task completion times. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 67(3), 366-381. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.67.3.366> ; Flyvbjerg, B. (2006). From Nobel Prize to project management: Getting risks right. Project Management Journal, 37(3), 5-15. <https://doi.org/10.1177/875697280603700302> ; Halkjelsvik, T., & Jørgensen, M. (2012). From origami to software development: A review of studies on judgment-based predictions of performance time. Psychological Bulletin, 138(2), 238-271. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0025996>
- Notes: The Buehler study used only Canadian university students - a single, small study, 37 people and about 40 per group. Estimating from the historical data of similar projects is called reference class forecasting; for how it's done in engineering, see Flyvbjerg. Halkjelsvik's review of multiple studies notes underestimation is more common than overestimation, but the size of the miss varies greatly by task and method.

### 5. Send an agenda before the meeting; don't hold agenda-less meetings; stand up if you can
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no benefit=mid lens=time -->
- Cost: Free. The organizer spends 5-10 minutes before the meeting writing an agenda. Standing meetings need the attendees' cooperation.
- TL;DR: Meetings with a pre-sent agenda are more likely to be judged useful by attendees. In an experiment, seated meetings ran 34% longer than standing ones, while the decisions made were no better or worse.
- Benefit: Two surveys of 958 and 292 people respectively: meetings with a pre-sent agenda were more likely to be rated effective by attendees, a statistically solid relationship. Another experiment had 55 groups meet seated and 56 standing; the seated ones ran 34% longer. The quality of decisions didn't differ between the two formats.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Leach, D. J., Rogelberg, S. G., Warr, P. B., & Burnfield, J. L. (2009). Perceived meeting effectiveness: The role of design characteristics. Journal of Business and Psychology, 24(1), 65-76. <https://doi.org/10.1007/s10869-009-9092-6> ; Bluedorn, A. C., Turban, D. B., & Love, M. S. (1999). The effects of stand-up and sit-down meeting formats on meeting outcomes. Journal of Applied Psychology, 84(2). <https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.84.2.277>
- Notes: The standing-meeting experiment used 5-person groups on lab tasks. Long meetings and large meetings can't copy that conclusion. Leach measured how effective attendees felt meetings were, not how much work actually got done.

### 6. Cut the number of meetings - whatever text can settle asynchronously doesn't need one
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some benefit=big lens=time -->
- Cost: Free. It needs cooperation from colleagues and managers. The hard part: writing a clear paragraph costs more energy than saying it out loud.
- TL;DR: The more meetings in a day, the more fatigue, and the more work people feel they have on their hands. Cutting one meeting saves exactly its length - no discount.
- Benefit: Two surveys of employees working 35+ hours a week, 676 and 304 people. How meeting time affects work attitudes and well-being isn't fixed - it depends on two things: how well meetings are run, and how much the job depends on others. Another study had people keep a week-long diary: the more meetings in a day, the higher the fatigue and the perceived workload. The time saved equals the length of the meetings you cut.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Rogelberg, S. G., Leach, D. J., Warr, P. B., & Burnfield, J. L. (2006). "Not another meeting!" Are meeting time demands related to employee well-being? Journal of Applied Psychology, 91(1). <https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.91.1.83> ; Luong, A., & Rogelberg, S. G. (2005). Meetings and more meetings: The relationship between meeting load and the daily well-being of employees. Group Dynamics: Theory, Research, and Practice, 9(1). <https://doi.org/10.1037/1089-2699.9.1.58>
- Notes: Disputed: Rogelberg 2006 found the link between meeting load and well-being isn't one-directional. For jobs highly dependent on others, more meetings are actually not a bad thing. Also, no study has directly compared "switching to asynchronous text" against meeting - that part is the author's own inference.
### 7. Break big tasks into subtasks before estimating and before starting
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no benefit=mid lens=time -->
- Cost: Free. The breakdown itself takes 5 to 15 minutes.
- TL;DR: When estimating, people don't automatically break a task into pieces and cost each one. Listing the subtasks first trims the underestimate. Broken down, each small step can be started right away - which also makes procrastinating harder.
- Benefit: When estimating durations, people don't spontaneously decompose a multi-part task - writing a literature review, writing the discussion, sorting references are really several things. Listing subtasks before estimating reduces the tendency to underestimate durations, a bias called the planning fallacy. Broken down, each small step becomes an immediately startable action, and procrastination drops with it. The factors that best predict procrastination in the research include aversion to the task itself, and the task being pushed back.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Kruger, J., & Evans, M. (2004). If you don't want to be late, enumerate: Unpacking reduces the planning fallacy. Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 40(5), 586-598. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2003.11.001> ; Steel, P. (2007). The nature of procrastination: A meta-analytic and theoretical review of quintessential self-regulatory failure. Psychological Bulletin, 133(1). <https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.133.1.65>
- Notes: How fine to split: each subtask should be finishable in one sitting - splitting too finely costs time itself. Also, for the Kruger and Evans study we verified only the direction from the abstract; the specific percentages haven't been checked against the original.

### 8. Give self-driven things a date of your own when no external deadline exists
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some benefit=big lens=time -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is honoring what you set. Treating the day as nothing when it arrives equals never setting it.
- TL;DR: With an externally set deadline, 80.6% of students finished before it. Only 38.7% finished within their own estimated time. When things get done is driven almost entirely by the deadline, barely by one's own estimate. So for things without a deadline, set one yourself - and honor it.
- Benefit: Students with external deadlines finished before them 80.6% of the time; only 38.7% finished within their own predicted time. Completion time tracked the deadline almost exactly - correlation r = 0.82, where closer to 1 means tighter tracking - and related only weakly to their own predictions.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Buehler, R., Griffin, D., & Ross, M. (1994). Exploring the "planning fallacy": Why people underestimate their task completion times. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 67(3), 366-381. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.67.3.366>
- Notes: Tell someone your self-set date and it moves closer to the externally set kind. To be clear: what the research verified is that externally set deadlines work. Self-imposed devices like time-boxing and pomodoros have never been tested directly - that part is extrapolated.

### 9. Treat procrastination as dodging discomfort, not as insufficient effort
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some benefit=mid lens=time -->
- Cost: Free. What changes is where you look: first ask what about the task makes you uncomfortable, then find a way.
- TL;DR: Procrastination has little to do with laziness or personality. What best predicts whether someone procrastinates is how aversive the task feels, whether they believe they can do it, and how easily the thing in front of them carries them off. So what works is making the task less aversive and the first step smaller - not scolding yourself and charging harder.
- Benefit: A meta-analysis pooling 691 correlations found a few things strongly and stably linked to procrastination: how aversive the task is, how far away the deadline is, self-efficacy (believing you can do it), impulsiveness, and conscientiousness with its facets of self-control, distractibility, organization and achievement motivation. Neuroticism, rebelliousness and sensation-seeking linked only weakly. The author explains the pattern with temporal motivation theory: a task's present appeal shrinks fast as its reward moves further away.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Steel P (2007). The nature of procrastination: a meta-analytic and theoretical review of quintessential self-regulatory failure. Psychological Bulletin, 133(1), 65-94. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.133.1.65>
- Notes: This entry gives direction, not moves - so its benefit tier is "mid": it never measured hours saved. The concrete moves are entry 1 of this chapter (write "when, where, if what, do what"), entry 7 (break big tasks into subtasks), and entry 8 (set a date for things without an external deadline). Bear in mind this pools correlational studies - don't read it as cause and effect.

### 10. Put what you need within reach and move what you shouldn't touch farther away - don't count on resisting in the moment
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=no benefit=mid lens=time -->
- Cost: Free, a few minutes once. Put the phone in another room; lay the book and laptop out on the desk.
- TL;DR: The farther away something is, the less of it people take. Moving the same snacks farther away cut how much was eaten by a clear, medium-sized margin. Offering fewer options cut takers even more clearly. These moves need no in-the-moment resistance - minutes to set up.
- Benefit: A Cochrane review of 24 randomized trials. Moving food farther away reduced the amount eaten - standardized mean difference -0.60 (95% CI -0.84 to -0.36, the plausible range; 12 studies, 1,098 people, low-certainty evidence), a medium effect. Reducing the number of options cut selection of the food even more - standardized mean difference -1.13 (95% CI -1.90 to -0.37, 3 studies, 154 people, low-certainty evidence). Regression analysis showed: the farther the placement and the fewer the options, the stronger the effect.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Hollands GJ, Carter P, Anwer S, et al. (2019). Altering the availability or proximity of food, alcohol, and tobacco products to change their selection and consumption. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 9, CD012573. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD012573.pub3>
- Notes: Why a B: all 24 studies in this review involved food - not one involved alcohol or tobacco; 14 were lab studies, all from high-income countries. So extending "farther away means less touching" to phones and games is an extrapolation with no direct trials. Putting the phone out of sight specifically has been measured directly - see entry 1 of chapter 3 (turn off nonessential notifications, keep the phone out of sight while working).

### 11. To make yourself follow through, put money on the line - but first ask whether you'd actually stake it
<!-- tags: money=low time=low will=some benefit=mid lens=time -->
- Cost: A deposit scheme means putting up your own money first; fail and it's forfeited. The trial staked $150.
- TL;DR: A 2,538-person randomized trial compared two approaches: a bonus for succeeding, versus a deposit you pay up front and get back - plus a bonus - only if you succeed. Among people willing to take either, the deposit kind beat the bonus kind by 13 percentage points. But only 13.7% would put up a deposit, versus 90% willing to take a bonus. The deposit works best on people who'll stake it - and doesn't exist for those who won't.
- Benefit: An American randomized trial of 2,538 people assigned employees and their friends and family to four incentive schemes or usual care. Acceptance rates: reward-type (about $800 for succeeding) 90.0%; deposit-type ($150 up front, plus a $650 reward) 13.7%. Six-month sustained quit rates: 9.4% to 16.0% across the four incentive schemes, versus 6.0% for usual care. Reward-type 15.7%, deposit-type 10.2%. But among only the 13.7% willing to take either, the deposit type's six-month quit rate beat the reward type by 13.2 percentage points (95% CI 3.1 to 22.8, the plausible range).
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Halpern SD, French B, Small DS, et al. (2015). Randomized trial of four financial-incentive programs for smoking cessation. New England Journal of Medicine, 372(22), 2108-2117. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1414293>
- Notes: This trial measured smoking cessation - extending it to writing a thesis or exercising is an extrapolation, hence the "mid" benefit tier. The point isn't "which works better" but "would you actually use it": a strong method most people refuse equals zero for most people. So pick the kind you'll accept - a small stake beats no stake. For how to quit smoking itself, see entry 3 of chapter 2 (quit-smoking medication).

### 12. Count new habits in months, not in "21 days"
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=yes benefit=mid lens=time -->
- Cost: Free. What it takes is repeating the same action in the same setting daily for a few months.
- TL;DR: "21 days to form a habit" has no basis. A 12-week tracking study found that for an action to become automatic, the fast cases took 18 days and the slow ones 254. Still feeling effortful at two or three weeks is normal. Missing one day in between doesn't zero out your progress.
- Benefit: 96 volunteers each picked an eating, drinking or activity behavior to perform daily in a fixed setting (like "after breakfast") for 12 weeks, scoring their own automaticity each day. 82 people's data was analyzable and the model fit 62 of them. The days needed to reach 95% of each person's automaticity ceiling ranged from 18 to 254. The study also found that missing one opportunity to perform had no material effect on habit formation.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Lally P, van Jaarsveld CHM, Potts HWW, Wardle J (2010). How are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world. European Journal of Social Psychology, 40(6), 998-1009. <https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674>
- Notes: Only one study, 96 people, all simple actions (like eating a fruit after a meal). How long complex things take - running three times a week, writing daily - nobody has measured. This entry's use is calibrating your expectations: don't read "still hard at two weeks" as evidence of failure. To make the repetition actually happen, bind the action to a fixed setting - see entry 1 of this chapter (write "when, where, if what, do what").

### 13. If procrastination is already hurting your life, use self-help material backed by randomized trials - no need to insist on a therapist
<!-- tags: money=0 time=mid will=some benefit=mid lens=time -->
- Cost: A self-help book runs a few dozen yuan; some are free. Budget about ten weeks, reading one section and doing one exercise a week.
- TL;DR: Procrastination severe enough to cause real distress is treatable. A 150-person randomized trial used ten weeks of cognitive behavioral self-help material; both the guided and fully self-directed groups clearly beat a do-nothing control. No difference showed between those two groups - meaning working through it yourself works too.
- Benefit: 150 people randomized into three groups: therapist-guided self-help, unguided self-help, and a waitlist control. After ten weeks, between-group effect sizes on the Pure Procrastination Scale were d = 0.70 for the guided group (95% CI 0.29-1.10, the plausible range) and d = 0.50 for the unguided (0.10-0.90); on the Irrational Procrastination Scale, 0.81 (0.40-1.22) and 0.69 (0.29-1.09) respectively. Rates of clinically meaningful improvement: 31.3% to 40.0% guided, 24.0% to 36.0% unguided. The two groups showed no significant difference on any outcome.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Rozental A, Forsell E, Svensson A, Andersson G, Carlbring P (2015). Internet-based cognitive-behavior therapy for procrastination: A randomized controlled trial. Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology, 83(4), 808-824. <https://doi.org/10.1037/ccp0000023>
- Notes: The subjects were Swedish adults using Swedish-language material - no ready-made Chinese equivalent of the same program exists, hence the "mid" benefit tier. The material's components are the usual ones: behavioral activation, behavioral experiments, stimulus control, and teaching about motivation and work methods. The stimulus-control piece is entry 10 of this chapter (move what you shouldn't touch farther away). If procrastination comes with clearly low mood or anxiety, follow entry 19 of chapter 3 first (move, sunlight, sleep on time, talk to someone, call 12356).

### 14. Use your own hourly wage to decide which chores to outsource
<!-- tags: money=high time=low will=no benefit=big lens=time -->
- Cost: The money for the outsourcing itself, varying by service. Plus one up-front session to compute your own hourly wage.
- TL;DR: Chinese people spend an average of 1 hour 17 minutes a day on housework; counting only those who did housework that day, 1 hour 59 minutes. People who buy time report higher life satisfaction. The same money spent buying time beats buying things for in-the-moment happiness.
- Benefit: Chinese residents average 1 hour 17 minutes of housework a day; counting only those who did housework, 1 hour 59 minutes. That's from a 2024 survey covering 38.5 thousand households and 107 thousand people. Another study sampled 6,271 people across four countries: people who buy time report higher life satisfaction. The researchers also ran real-life experiments: the same amount of money spent buying time brought more in-the-moment happiness than spent on material goods.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Whillans, A. V., Dunn, E. W., Smeets, P., Bekkers, R., & Norton, M. I. (2017). Buying time promotes happiness. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. <https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1706541114> ; National Bureau of Statistics (2024). Third National Time Use Survey Bulletin (No. 2). <https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/zxfb/202410/t20241031_1957216.html>
- Notes: First compute your hourly wage: monthly after-tax income divided by actual monthly work hours. Then check what outsourcing this chore costs. If the outsourcing rate is below your hourly wage, and the freed time will genuinely go to earning or resting, outsource. Only the method is given here, no conclusion - everyone's wage and outsourcing price differs. Also, Whillans measured whether buying time makes people happier, not how many hours it saves.

### 15. Set hard caps on short video and aimless scrolling
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=yes benefit=big lens=time -->
- Cost: Free. The moves take minutes: kill push notifications, delete the app, or set a daily time limit. The hard part is holding your hand back from tapping.
- TL;DR: Chinese people average 5 hours 37 minutes online a day; counting only those who went online that day, 6 hours 3 minutes. In 2018, computed the same way, it was only 2 hours 42 minutes. This is the biggest number in the chapter. Cutting one hour a day adds 365 hours a year.
- Benefit: Chinese residents average 5 hours 37 minutes online a day; counting only those who went online, 6 hours 3 minutes. 92.9% of people used the internet that day. These are 2024 figures. In 2018, computed the same way, it was only 2 hours 42 minutes. This is the biggest number in the chapter. Cutting one of those hours a day means 365 extra hours a year.
- Evidence: C
- Sources: National Bureau of Statistics (2024). Third National Time Use Survey Bulletin (No. 2). <https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/zxfb/202410/t20241031_1957216.html> ; National Bureau of Statistics (2019). 2018 National Time Use Survey Bulletin. <https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/zxfb/202302/t20230203_1900224.html> ; TODO (unverified: per-user weekly online hours and short-video user counts in CNNIC's 55th/56th Statistical Reports on China's Internet Development - the official PDFs resist text extraction, figures unverified)
- Notes: Online time includes work, study and socializing - not all of it is waste. Graded C because no study has computed "how much a limit wins back and where that time went" - all we have is aggregate statistics. For the "watching how others are doing" slice specifically, randomized trials have measured how much time comes back and how mood shifts - see entry 21 of chapter 3.

### 16. No TV or rolling news - check the information you need at set times, in batches
<!-- tags: money=0 time=low will=some benefit=big lens=time -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is breaking the reflex of opening it idly - and you may miss some breaking news.
- TL;DR: Americans 15 and up watch 2.6 hours of TV a day, half of all their leisure time. Chinese people average 1 hour 40 minutes, and 75-to-84-year-olds 3 hours 16 minutes. Switching what you need to scheduled, batched checking wins back that whole block.
- Benefit: Americans 15 and up watch 2.6 hours of TV a day. Their total leisure time is 5.2 hours, so TV takes half. That's 2025 data. Chinese residents average 1 hour 40 minutes of TV a day; 75-to-84-year-olds 3 hours 16 minutes. That's 2018 data.
- Evidence: C
- Sources: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (2026). American Time Use Survey - 2025 Results. <https://www.bls.gov/news.release/atus.nr0.htm> ; National Bureau of Statistics (2019). 2018 National Time Use Survey Bulletin. <https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/zxfb/202302/t20230203_1900224.html>
- Notes: All we have is aggregate statistics. No study has computed where the saved time goes after watching less TV and news. The "batched checking" practice is the author's experience.

### 17. Spend one stretch of time learning keyboard shortcuts and automation for the software you use most
<!-- tags: money=0 time=mid will=some benefit=mid lens=time -->
- Cost: Free. It takes a one-time investment of hours to tens of hours. The hard part: at first you're slower than with the mouse.
- TL;DR: Most people who have used Word for years still click toolbar icons. In group-comparison experiments, shortcuts beat menus and icons for speed. Learning once is a one-time cost; afterward every operation every day pays it back. The longer you do the same kind of work, the more it earns back.
- Benefit: A study looked at 251 experienced Word users: most rarely used the faster keyboard shortcuts, sticking mainly to the icon toolbar. Group-comparison experiments confirmed that among menus, icons and shortcuts, shortcuts are fastest. The time won back accumulates with daily operation counts. People doing the same kind of work long-term gain the most.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Lane, D. M., Napier, H. A., Peres, S. C., & Sándor, A. (2005). Hidden costs of graphical user interfaces: Failure to make the transition from menus and icon toolbars to keyboard shortcuts. International Journal of Human-Computer Interaction. <https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327590ijhc1802_1>
- Notes: Learn only the operations you use ten-plus times a day - learning everything is another kind of waste. To be clear: that efficiency experiment had only 6 participants, and the abstract gives no per-operation seconds saved.

### 18. When choosing where to live, put commute length near the top - shorten the one-way commute
<!-- tags: money=high time=mid will=no benefit=big lens=time -->
- Cost: High. Possibly higher rent, or a smaller place. Moving itself takes time too.
- TL;DR: The longer the commute, the worse people feel their lives are going - and that loss is not made back in pay or housing. 30 minutes less each way saves 5 hours a week.
- Benefit: Chinese residents average 50 minutes a day on transportation; counting only those who traveled that day, 1 hour 2 minutes (2024 data). German data tracking the same people long-term shows that longer commuters have steadily lower subjective well-being - they feel their lives go worse - and the loss is not compensated in income or housing. A review pulling multiple studies together also confirms that longer commutes mean less commute satisfaction, regardless of transport mode. 30 minutes less each way saves about 5 hours a week.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Stutzer, A., & Frey, B. S. (2008). Stress that doesn't pay: The commuting paradox. Scandinavian Journal of Economics, 110(2), 339-366. <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9442.2008.00542.x> ; Chatterjee, K., et al. (2020). Commuting and wellbeing: A critical overview of the literature with implications for policy and future research. Transport Reviews, 40(1), 5-34. <https://doi.org/10.1080/01441647.2019.1649317> ; National Bureau of Statistics (2024). Third National Time Use Survey Bulletin (No. 2). <https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/zxfb/202410/t20241031_1957216.html>
- Notes: Disputed: Chatterjee et al., pooling multiple studies, found no stable relationship between commute length and overall life satisfaction. They argue people can largely trade a long commute back for a better job or better housing. Note also that the transportation statistic counts all trips, not just commutes. And if you can shift your work hours off-peak, the same road runs shorter too.
