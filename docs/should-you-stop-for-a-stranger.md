# A stranger is in trouble on the road: walk on or stop

This is the long-form version of entry 2 in chapter 13 (an elderly person fallen, someone on the ground - don't rush to help them up). The book sorts beneficiaries into four tiers, ranked by how likely the benefit is to come back to you. Strangers are the lowest tier: the chance they repay you is small, and you don't know who they are. So this piece doesn't urge you to do anything. What each of the three routes costs is laid out below; which to take is your call.

**This piece gives no probabilities.** Every cost layer below is real and has checkable provisions. But "out of a hundred people who helped, how many actually got burned" - no public statistics exist. The book's rule is that uncertain numbers are better left unwritten. So don't read "seven layers are listed here" as "all seven will land on you."

## The three routes

| | Walk on | Stop but don't touch | Stop and see it through |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Legality | Legal. An ordinary person owes strangers no duty to rescue, and criminal law has no "saw it and didn't help" offense | Legal | Legal |
| Time | 0 | A few minutes. Calling 120 leaves your number | Half a day and up, possibly following to the hospital |
| Chance of being sought out afterward | Lowest | You're the one who left a number | Highest |
| Benefit to the other person | 0 | The ambulance comes sooner | Largest |

## The possible costs after stopping, lightest to heaviest

**One, return calls and being questioned.** Call 120 or 110 and your number stays on record. They may call you back to pin down the location. You may also be called in for questioning afterward. In a criminal case, if the reporter doesn't want their name made public, the handling authority must keep it confidential (Criminal Procedure Law, Article 111). A 120 call has no such clause protecting you.

**Two, being sought out by the injured person's family.** If the cameras can't find the vehicle responsible, the easiest person for the family to find is you - you left a number, and you were on the scene. They may call and press, or show up at your door.

**Three, being sued in court.** Whoever claims you hit the person must produce the evidence; if they can't, they bear the consequence (Interpretation of the Civil Procedure Law, Article 90). **But this clause governs only the final judgment, not whether you get dragged in.** Under ordinary procedure, a first-instance case must close within 6 months, extendable another 6 in special circumstances; under simplified procedure, 3 months (Civil Procedure Law, Articles 152, 164). Even if you win outright, what you can make the other side pay is only three items: the case-acceptance fee, application fees, and the travel and lost-work costs of witnesses and appraisers appearing in court (Measures for the payment of litigation fees, Articles 6, 29). **Your own attorney's fees are not among the three - you pay them yourself.**

**Four, a scene at your workplace.** If someone comes to make a scene at your workplace, you can call the police. Disrupting the order of an enterprise or institution so work can't proceed normally: a warning or a fine up to 500 yuan; heavier circumstances, 5 to 10 days' detention plus a fine up to 1,000 yuan; a group coming together, the ringleader gets 10 to 15 days (Public Security Administration Punishments Law, Article 26, item 1). Repeated harassment, pestering and following that keeps you from living normally is punished under Article 50, item 5 - and with approval from the police authority's head, the person can be ordered not to contact you for a set period. If the company fires you over this, it's generally an unlawful termination, with compensation at 2N (entry 6 of chapter 19). But the police report and the labor arbitration both require your own repeated trips - and the stretch with the job already gone doesn't come back when the 2N eventually arrives.

**Five, being filmed and posted online.** What this layer most needs you to know: **you may not be able to stop the filming.** Without the person's consent, another's likeness may not be made, used or published (Civil Code, Article 1019). But there's an exception: "inevitably making, using or publishing a likeness for news reporting" is reasonable use, not infringement (Article 1020, item 2). In other words, a journalist filming the news may be beyond your power to stop. What you can do: decline the interview, stay off camera, say nothing in front of the lens. Whether to agree is yours to decide - nobody can agree for you.

**Six, the video circulating, and the abuse that follows.** Once the video spreads, the comment section is no longer yours to control. What you can do yourself is written in the Provisions on the governance of online violence information (effective August 1, 2024). The platform must give you these protection settings. You can block strangers, or block a specific person. You can limit who sees your information. You can forbid reposts or comments. You can take private messages from friends only, or from no one (Article 23). The platform must provide one-tap evidence capture to fix the abusive content (Article 25). It must put a dedicated quick complaint channel in a prominent place (Article 26). On finding suspected illegality or crime, it must promptly report to the police with leads (Article 15). How to keep evidence and report to the police yourself: entry 15 of chapter 8.

**Seven, when you can't hold up.** The six layers above all have provisions to answer them; the seventh doesn't. How much being besieged continuously affects a person's emotions - there is no reliable domestic data to cite. This book gives no number, only an exit: the psychological-assistance hotline 12356, in entry 25 of chapter 1.

## The two situations where "walking on" stops being free

- **You are the operator or manager of the premises, or the organizer of a mass event.** Premises means places like hotels, malls, banks, stations, airports, stadiums and entertainment venues. You owe the people inside a safety-protection duty. Fail it, and you bear tort liability - meaning you pay. If the harm was caused by someone else but you failed the duty, you pay within the share you should answer for - supplementary liability (Civil Code, Article 1198). An employee on duty walking away, a venue operator walking away, and a passerby walking away are entirely different things.
- **The matter involves you to begin with.** You hit the person, the car is yours, or the danger came from your own earlier act. Then it's no longer a question of "whether to bother with someone else." What to read then is traffic-accident handling and hit-and-run - entry 1 of chapter 8.

## If you decide to stop, the lowest-trouble way

1. **Don't move or lift the person with your hands.** This one has no second answer. The reason is medical, not legal: a forceful lift can worsen a brain bleed or a spinal injury. On the legal side there's nothing to worry about - harm caused in the course of rescuing is already exempted by Civil Code Article 184.
2. Call out to them from a short distance and check for breathing. No breathing: press hard on the chest - entry 1 of chapter 13. Breathing: stay where you are and call 120, stating the location clearly.
3. Pull a passerby in with you, and start video recording on your phone at the same time. A witness plus a recording beats arguing with people afterward.
4. Don't lift the person into a car by yourself, and don't pay out of pocket to rush them to the hospital. This step costs the most on the whole route and is the hardest to close out afterward.
5. If you're injured yourself in the rescue, or out money: how to claim it back is in entry 39 of chapter 13 (injured or out of pocket from helping someone).
