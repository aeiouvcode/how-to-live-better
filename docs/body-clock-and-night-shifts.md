# How the body tells time, and why night shifts hurt

This is the long-form version of entry 40 in chapter 2 (the longer you work night shifts, the higher the cardiovascular risk). That entry in the main text only counts the ledger and doesn't explain. This piece explains.

The question to answer: can the body really know what time it is? If so, why isn't night-shift work something you "get used to after a few rough stretches"?

## 1. There really is a timekeeping organ in the body

A small cluster of neurons in the hypothalamus called the suprachiasmatic nucleus - about twenty thousand cells - is the body's master clock.

It keeps time through a set of "clock genes" inhibiting each other. Two proteins called CLOCK and BMAL1 switch on the transcription of two other genes, PER and CRY. As PER and CRY proteins accumulate, they turn around and switch off the pair that activated them. When those proteins finish degrading, the cycle restarts. One lap takes about 24 hours.

So this clock isn't "inferred by observing the outside world." Lock a person in a room with no light, no clocks and no social cues, and this rhythm keeps running. The period just isn't exactly 24 hours - it drifts a little day by day. The 2017 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine went to the three researchers who discovered this molecular machinery.

## 2. The clock sets itself by light, over a pathway that isn't "seeing things"

Besides the image-forming cells, the retina holds a dedicated class of light-sensing cells. They contain a pigment called melanopsin, play no part in seeing clearly, and feed light intensity straight into the suprachiasmatic nucleus.

So the light that calibrates the body clock and "being able to see" are two different things. Some completely blind people still have their body clocks calibrated by light.

This is also why entry 2 of chapter 3 (a fixed wake time, weekends included) pairs with morning light.

## 3. The key: there's more than one clock

The liver, gut, heart and fat tissue each carry their own peripheral clock.

The master clock sets itself by light. **The peripheral clocks set themselves largely by "when you eat."**

On a normal schedule the two are synchronized: you see light by day and eat by day, and the master and peripheral clocks point at the same time. Which is why you never notice they're two separate systems.

## 4. The night shift's problem isn't "staying up" - it's the two clocks being pulled apart

Night-shift workers still see daylight and still live a daytime social life. So the master clock stays basically locked to the outside day-night cycle.

But you're eating, working and mobilizing metabolism in the biological middle of the night. The peripheral clocks get dragged the other way.

This state is called circadian misalignment. The problem isn't the few hours of sleep you lost - it's that the body's clocks no longer agree with each other.

## 5. And you can't adapt

One might think a long-term fixed night shift would eventually be adapted to.

A review pooled six studies that measured long-term permanent night workers by their melatonin secretion rhythm - the most reliable marker of the internal body clock's state.

The result: under 3% achieved "complete" adaptation. Under a quarter reached "substantial" adaptation.

No matter how many years in. The reason is the previous section: you still see daylight and still live a daytime social life, and those cues keep pulling the master clock back.

Source: Folkard S (2008). Do permanent night workers show circadian adjustment? A review based on the endogenous melatonin rhythm. Chronobiology International. <https://doi.org/10.1080/07420520802106835>

## 6. How do we know the misalignment itself does the harm, not just lost sleep

This is the layer most worth asking about. Night-shift workers sleep less, eat chaotically, carry more stress and smoke more - any of those could be the real cause.

Fortunately this can be isolated in a lab.

Scheer et al., 2009: 10 adults lived in a lab for 10 days. The researchers artificially set a "day" to 28 hours, forcing eating and sleeping to sweep through every phase of the body clock. **Four meals a day, calories fixed.**

With behavior and the body clock offset by about 12 hours, the results:

- Leptin dropped 17% (leptin is the hormone that makes you feel full)
- Blood glucose rose 6% - and that was with insulin already up 22%
- The daily cortisol rhythm fully inverted
- Mean arterial pressure rose 3%
- Sleep efficiency dropped 20%

The line that matters most: of the 8 people with sufficient data, 3 had post-meal glucose in the prediabetic range. In 10 days.

Because calories were fixed and the experiment ran under lab control, these changes can't be blamed on "less sleep" or "eating more." **The misalignment alone is enough.**

Source: Scheer FA, Hilton MF, Mantzoros CS, Shea SA (2009). Adverse metabolic and cardiovascular consequences of circadian misalignment. PNAS, 106(11), 4453-4458. <https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0808180106>

## 7. The first changeable thing: move eating to daytime

Since the peripheral clocks set themselves by meal timing, there's a direction to try: keep the night shift, but don't eat at night.

A randomized controlled trial did exactly this (registration NCT02291952). 20 healthy people simulated night work, randomized into two groups. One ate both at night and by day, as night workers commonly do. The other ate only by day.

The primary-outcome paper reported: the night-eating group showed misalignment between the central rhythm and the peripheral glucose rhythm, and worse glucose tolerance; the daytime-only group showed neither.

The cardiovascular paper (a secondary analysis of the same trial) reported: in the night-eating group, the cardiac vagal-modulation marker pNN50 fell 25.7%, RMSSD fell 14.3%, the sympathetic-vagal balance LF/HF rose 5.5%, and the pro-thrombotic factor PAI-1 rose 23.9%. The daytime-only group showed none of these changes.

**But to be plain: this was 20 people, and simulated night work, not real night workers.**

A systematic review hunting for exactly this kind of randomized trial searched six databases and found only 4. The results aren't tidy either: some trials saw fasting insulin and insulin resistance improve, some didn't; none of the four saw a change in HDL; on post-meal glucose, fasting 10.75 hours overnight did beat eating a meal plus a snack at night. The review's authors say it themselves: too few trials to generalize.

So all that can be said today is this much: **concentrating eating into daytime on night shifts has randomized-trial support, but the evidence is still thin.** It costs nothing and isn't hard - try it if you like. The second thing to try is in the next section: using light to move the whole clock over.

Sources: Chellappa SL et al. (2021). Daytime eating prevents internal circadian misalignment and glucose intolerance in night work. Science Advances. <https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.abg9910>; Chellappa SL et al. (2025). Daytime eating during simulated night work mitigates changes in cardiovascular risk factors: secondary analyses of a randomized controlled trial. Nature Communications. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-57846-y>; Wulandari F et al. (2026). Cardiometabolic status among shift workers under meal time regulation: A systematic review of randomized controlled trials. Nutrition and Health. <https://doi.org/10.1177/02601060261464874>

## 8. Can the clock be moved over

Everything above is "misalignment is harmful." What about the reverse - moving the whole clock onto the night-shift side?

**In theory, yes - and it's been done. But the light requirements are an order of magnitude higher than most people think.**

Czeisler et al., 1990: 8 young men worked a week of night shifts under two compared conditions.

In the control condition, nights had ordinary indoor lighting, about 150 lux. Lux is the unit of illuminance - an ordinary fluorescent-lit room runs about one or two hundred. Result: after six consecutive nights, the body-temperature minimum still landed around 3:30 AM. The temperature minimum is the standard marker for where the body clock points. In other words, the clock didn't budge.

In the treatment condition, nights had bright light of 7,000 to 12,000 lux, and daytime sleep happened in near-total darkness. After four days, the temperature minimum moved to almost 3 PM. Effectively flipped all the way over.

Source: Czeisler CA, Johnson MP, Duffy JF, Brown EN, Ronda JM, Kronauer RE (1990). Exposure to bright light and darkness to treat physiologic maladaptation to night work. New England Journal of Medicine, 322(18), 1253-1259. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM199005033221801>

### Three things, none dispensable

**First, the night light has to be bright enough.** Working under ordinary fluorescents is the "nothing happened" control group above. Getting an effect takes a dedicated light-therapy box.

**Second, daytime sleep needs strict blackout.** Blackout curtains plus an eye mask. The experiment above did "bright light at night" and "near-total darkness by day" together - not one or the other.

**Third, wear sunglasses on the way home from work.** This is the easiest one to miss, and missing it basically voids the whole effort.

Why the third matters so much comes down to how light acts: the same light, landing at different times, pushes the body clock in opposite directions. Light **before** the temperature minimum pushes the clock later; light **after** it pulls the clock earlier.

Walking home after a night shift, you're right after the temperature minimum. **That stretch of morning light does exactly the opposite of what you spent the whole night doing in front of the light box.**

### Doable in a lab, not in a life

The lab subjects didn't leave for a week. In reality your days off are lived by day - picking up kids, being with family, running errands. One day of it and the clock gets pulled back. That's where the section-five number comes from: under 3% of long-term permanent night workers fully adapt.

So the real world usually doesn't chase a full flip - it moves the clock only partway, parking it at a middle position: "alert enough at work, and days off not completely wrecked."

### What's realistically obtainable

A meta-analysis pooling 11 studies: light therapy gave shift workers about 32.5 more minutes of total sleep and about 2.91 points better sleep efficiency. The effective dose was 900 to 6,000 lux, at least 1 hour per night.

Another lab study used intermittent bright light. In the treatment group, melatonin secretion overlapped the scheduled sleep window by 4.90 hours, versus 2.62 in controls (P=0.002). The cognitive-performance decline the control group showed on the last night shift was suppressed in the treatment group. The authors state in their conclusion that the method still needs validating in real night workers.

Sources: Zhao C, Li N, Miao W, He Y, Lin Y (2025). A systematic review and meta-analysis on light therapy for sleep disorders in shift workers. Scientific Reports. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-83789-3>; Lammers-van der Holst HM et al. (2021). Efficacy of intermittent exposure to bright light for treating maladaptation to night work on a counterclockwise shift work rotation. Scandinavian Journal of Work, Environment & Health. <https://doi.org/10.5271/sjweh.3953>

### One thing nobody has measured

"If the clock is moved correctly, does cardiovascular risk come down with it" - **no trial has measured this.**

What's known is that misalignment is harmful - that's what the section-six experiment established. But that doesn't make the reverse, "aligned means safe," true. And whether flipping the clock back on days off, back and forth, is good or bad - likewise unmeasured.

Neither of the two countermeasures above reached disease or death as endpoints - one stops at metabolic and cardiovascular markers, the other at sleep.
