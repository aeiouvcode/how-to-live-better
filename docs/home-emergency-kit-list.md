# Home emergency kit list: what to buy, where to put it, how often to check

This belongs to entry 26 of chapter 1 in the README (stock a fire extinguisher, fire blanket, escape breathing hoods and a first-aid kit). This list answers one thing only: what to keep at home. How to use it isn't taught here. What to do on the spot in a fire, CPR, heavy bleeding, scalds or carbon-monoxide poisoning is all written in chapter 13. How to choose and install smoke and carbon-monoxide alarms: entry 3 of chapter 1. Gas hoses and stoves: entry 4 of chapter 1.

This chapter counts the ledger of fewer deaths and fewer injuries. It covers harm from accidents, not disease. How much the gear itself actually helps, the evidence is honestly not hard - the reasons are in the last section. Read that part before reading on.

## 1. The official list, verbatim

The Ministry of Emergency Management published a National basic recommended list of household emergency supply reserves - 11 items. The table below carries the original explanations.

| No. | Item | Original explanation |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Drinking water | Covers each person's basic drinking needs for 3 days, at least 3 liters per person |
| 2 | Ready-to-eat food | Covers each person's basic food needs for 3 days. Ready-to-eat food is small and calorie-dense |
| 3 | Fire extinguisher and fire blanket | For fighting incipient fires. The fire blanket can be draped over the body to escape |
| 4 | Breathing hood | Fire-filtering self-rescue respirator, used for escaping fires |
| 5 | Flashlight | Waterproof, explosion-proof flashlight. Recharge or replace batteries regularly |
| 6 | Multitool knife | Combined functions: blade, saw, screwdriver, pliers |
| 7 | Radio | For receiving emergency broadcasts |
| 8 | Rescue whistle | Can sound a high-frequency distress signal |
| 9 | External-use medication | Hemostatic powder, hemostatic bandages, gauze and the like, for treating wounds |
| 10 | Disinfectant wipes | For personal hygiene and cleaning |
| 11 | Surgical masks | Virus protection |

Source: Ministry of Emergency Management (2020). National basic recommended list of household emergency supply reserves. <https://www.mem.gov.cn/kp/shaq/202011/t20201129_372149.shtml>

Local versions list more detail. The Beijing Municipal Emergency Management Bureau's household list comes in two tiers: basic and expanded. The basic tier is split into "emergency items, emergency tools and emergency medicine - 3 categories." The concrete things: a hand-crank rechargeable flashlight that receives broadcasts, a rescue whistle, towels and tissues or wet wipes, a breathing hood, multitool combination scissors, an emergency escape rope, a fire extinguisher or fire blanket. Medicine and medical materials separate: anti-infective and anti-cold medication, surgical masks, medical materials like gauze and bandages, iodophor cotton swabs. The expanded tier splits into "food, personal items, escape and self-rescue tools, medical first-aid supplies, important documents - 5 categories of emergency supplies." The bureau also gives three suggestions. One, "choose emergency supplies from producers and sellers with lawful qualifications and good reputation." Two, "prioritize stocking the basic tier's varieties." Three, "become familiar with the correct use of emergency supplies, check their condition regularly, and promptly replace any past their shelf life."

## 2. The fire trio

**Fire extinguisher.** Portable extinguishers are a state-mandated certified product. A compliant body carries the CCC mark. The standard it follows is GB 4351-2023, Portable fire extinguishers. The pre-pressurized kind has a pressure gauge on the body - CO2 extinguishers excepted. The needle must rest in the green zone. Red or yellow means the unit is done. Retirement age is counted by standard XF 95: water-based 6 years, dry-powder 10 years, CO2 12 years, counted from the manufacturing date - find it in the stamped marking on the body. For home use, buy ABC dry-powder. One by the kitchen door, one by the entrance. Don't stuff it into the cabinet directly above the stove - unreachable in a real fire, same as not buying it.

**Fire blanket.** In the official list it shares a row with the extinguisher. Two uses: smother a burning oil pan, or drape it over yourself and get out. One in a kitchen drawer is enough. It takes no space and doesn't expire. Pan on fire: kill the heat first, then lay the blanket on. Don't lift it right back off afterward.

**Escape breathing hood.** Its official name is the fire-filtering self-rescue respirator. The standard it follows is GB 21976.7-2012, Escape and refuge equipment for building fires, Part 7: filtering fire self-rescue respirators - a currently valid mandatory national standard, implemented June 1, 2013, reaffirmed as still valid in a 2023 review. When buying, look for that standard number and the CCC mark. One per person in the household. Keep them within arm's reach of the bed, not locked in a storage room. It works by the filter canister inside trapping the toxic gases in smoke. One use and it's spent. It only lasts its rated time. So it has exactly one use: put it on and walk out. What actually kills in a fire is smoke, not flame. How to get out (crawl low, feel the door before opening, stairs not the elevator): entry 24 of chapter 13. Smoke alarms: entry 3 of chapter 1. Those two things deserve doing before the trio above.

## 3. What goes in the first-aid kit

On this item the official list writes one line: "external-use medication: hemostatic powder, hemostatic bandages, gauze and the like, for treating wounds." Chapter 13 has already verified which on-the-spot actions to take. Working backward from those actions, this is a reasonable home kit:

- Bleeding: one windlass tourniquet - the kind with a turning rod that tightens. Plus sterile gauze and bandages, triangular bandages, medical tape. How to use a tourniquet, when not to, and why "don't loosen it to let blood out": entry 12 of chapter 13
- Cleaning wounds: iodophor cotton swabs, saline, adhesive bandages, sterile dressings, disposable gloves, scissors and tweezers
- Burns and scalds: no ointment of any kind. Run cool tap water over it for 20 minutes - the one thing to do on the spot, entry 14 of chapter 13 (cool running water for 20 minutes after a scald)
- Records: prepare a card with the family's allergies, chronic diseases and regular medications. When the medics arrive, hand them the card directly

If someone at home already knows they have severe allergies, keep an epinephrine auto-injector on hand separately. It's prescription-only - get it from a doctor; entry 15 of chapter 13 (recognizing anaphylactic shock, giving the shot).

This first-aid list is grade C evidence. C means written from experience and consensus, without direct literature. The official documents give only the words "external-use medication" for this item. What specifically to buy is inferred backward from the first-aid actions this book has already verified.

## 4. Lighting, communication and signaling

Buy the flashlight per the official description: waterproof and explosion-proof. Recharge it or change its batteries every so often. The rescue whistle is for signaling continuously when trapped - blowing a whistle costs less effort than shouting, and the sound carries better through noise. The radio is for emergency broadcasts. The Beijing list also adds an emergency escape rope and multitool combination scissors. The best value in this category is honestly a phone power bank - most households already have one.

## 5. Check once a year, ten minutes

- Fire extinguisher: is the pressure-gauge needle still in the green? Then read the manufacturing date on the body and count against the retirement age
- Smoke and carbon-monoxide alarms: press the test button. Change batteries yearly - entry 3 of chapter 1 (install smoke and carbon-monoxide alarms)
- Breathing hoods: is the packaging intact, is the rated shelf life past?
- First-aid kit: expired medication or dressings? Does the tourniquet's buckle still lock?
- Flashlight and power bank: any charge left?

The Beijing bureau's own words: "regularly check the condition of emergency supplies, and promptly replace any past their shelf life." Pin this to the day you change the alarm batteries each year and check everything in one pass.

## 6. What not to buy

- **A home AED - automated external defibrillator.** One costs over ten thousand yuan, and there's no evidence supporting households buying their own. What to do in a cardiac arrest: start compressions immediately, send someone to call 120, and fetch the AED from the nearest public place - entry 1 of chapter 13
- **"Fire masks" and "gas masks" without the CCC mark.** Article 24 of the Fire Protection Law provides that fire-protection products within the mandatory product-certification scope may be "produced, sold and used only after being certified as meeting the mandatory requirements of national and industry standards by a certification body with statutory qualifications" - uncertified means it can't be sold or used. Also, industrial filter canisters protect against workshop toxins - not the same thing as fire smoke
- **Pre-bundled "emergency gift packs."** These are mostly padded out with cheap flashlights and masks. Buying the extinguisher, blanket, hoods and first-aid kit separately costs less and makes checking each certification easier
- **Stockpiling more than three days of food and water.** The official list counts 3 days per person. The extra stockpiled part mostly ends up expiring in the trash - entry 24 of chapter 5 (no stockpiling for sales)

Source: Standing Committee of the National People's Congress (2021). Fire Protection Law of the People's Republic of China (2021 amendment, Article 24). <https://www.beijing.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengcefagui/qtwj/202307/t20230726_3207767.html>

## 7. How much does this gear actually help

Honestly: this chapter's evidence is weaker than the other entries in chapter 1.

A Cochrane systematic review pooled 98 studies and 2,605,044 people. It assessed whether home-safety education and handing out safety equipment actually help. The result: these practices may lower the injury rate somewhat, IRR 0.89 (95% CI 0.78 to 1.01) - about ten percent lower. But the credible range's upper bound crosses 1 - crossing 1 means there may be no difference at all. Among them, the ones done door-to-door worked better, IRR 0.75 (95% CI 0.62 to 0.91) - about twenty-five percent lower. The teach-only-no-gear ones, IRR 0.78 (95% CI 0.66 to 0.92) - about twenty percent lower. In other words, what works looks more like the home visits and home modifications, not the act of handing things out itself. The review also concluded: no evidence that these practices reduced burns, scalds or poisonings.

In the same review, these practices did improve people's safety habits. More households had working smoke alarms, OR 1.81 (95% CI 1.30 to 2.52) - about eighty percent higher. More had thought out an escape plan, OR 2.01 (1.45 to 2.77) - about double. More stored medicines properly, OR 1.53 (1.27 to 1.84) - about fifty percent higher. More had stair gates installed, OR 1.61 (1.19 to 2.17) - about sixty percent higher.

Source: Kendrick D et al. (2012). Home safety education and provision of safety equipment for injury prevention. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD005014.pub3>

So this chapter's positioning: buying out the official list in one pass costs little and counts as reasonable preparation. But don't think buying it makes you safe. What research repeatedly proves useful is three things: install smoke alarms, think out how you'll get out ahead of time, move the things in your home that hurt people. Those three are either not on the shopping list at all, or a very small part of it.
