# Is marriage worth it: splitting one muddled ledger into five clear ones

What many people want to ask is one general question: does marriage lose or pay. Several different things are stuffed inside that question: whether the other person gives emotional support, whether they share the housework, whether they earn, and whether to marry just to appease the elders. These things can't be converted into each other, so served up whole, the question has no answer. This piece splits it apart and counts each ledger separately. Every ledger uses only two kinds of numbers: official statistics, or numbers from meta-analyses - a meta-analysis pools many studies and counts them together. Where it can't be counted, this piece says so plainly. At the end there's a checklist you can fill in yourself. The piece gives no conclusion on "should you marry."

## 1. Split the question first

"Is marriage worth it" holds at least five ledgers. They have different units and different arithmetic, and can't be added together.

1. Money: first, what you actually pay out - bride price, the wedding, the marital home. Second, who the law says the property belongs to. The first has no official statistics. The second has explicit provisions.
2. Time: housework, caring for family, yielding to the other person's work. This is all labor nobody pays for. The unit is minutes per day.
3. Health: how much the death rate differs between the married and the unmarried. This ledger can only show that two things appear together. It can't show the gap was caused by marriage.
4. Legal risk: how property is split. How long exiting takes. Which contributions can be claimed back as compensation. This ledger reads what the legal provisions write.
5. Relationship quality: whether the other person supports you emotionally, how badly the two of you fight. This ledger can only be measured by the strength of association found in research. It converts into neither money nor time.

The fifth is the hardest to turn into numbers. It's also the closest to what most people actually want to ask.

## 2. Each ledger's data and sources

### Registration data: the marriage rate and divorce rate are two crude rates

The numbers come from the Ministry of Civil Affairs' 2024 Statistical bulletin on civil affairs development. In 2024, 6.106 million couples registered marriages nationwide, down 20.5% from the prior year - a marriage rate of 4.3 per thousand. 3.513 million couples completed divorce procedures - a divorce rate of 2.5 per thousand. Of those 3.513 million, 2.622 million divorced by registration at civil-affairs offices and 891,000 by court judgment or mediation. The bulletin's notes spell out how the two rates are computed: the year's marriages (divorces), divided by the year's average total population, times 1000 per thousand.

Both rates divide by the whole population, so they're called crude rates. "Crude" means no breakdown of any kind. They answer this question: of every thousand people this year, how many couples registered a marriage or divorce. They don't answer the other question: whether a given marriage will end. Answering that needs data following the same cohort over time. This piece found no such data in official originals.

### The health ledger: the married die less, but that's correlation, not causation

Three meta-analyses pooled many studies, and the magnitudes agree. "All-cause mortality" below means all deaths lumped together regardless of cause.

- Manzoli et al. (2007) pooled 53 comparison groups and over 250,000 elderly people. Here "not in a marriage" covers three kinds: widowed, divorced or separated, never married. The married against these three: all-cause mortality RR 0.88, about twelve percent lower. RR is the ratio of the two groups' death risk; under 1 means the married side is lower. The 95% CI of 0.85-0.91 is the credible range for that number. Separately: widowed 1.11, divorced or separated 1.16, never married 1.11 - about ten, sixteen and ten percent higher respectively. Splitting by sex, by study quality, or by Europe vs. America changed nothing. Rerun with the most conservative method: RR 0.94, only about six percent lower.
- Roelfs et al. (2011) pooled 95 papers and over 500 million people. The never-married against the married: all-cause mortality HR 1.24, about twenty-four percent higher. HR is a multiplier like RR; above 1 means higher risk. 1.19-1.30 is the credible range. The older the age, the smaller the gap. The more rigorous the study, the smaller the computed gap.
- Wang et al. (2020) pooled 21 studies and over 7.89 million people. Not being in a marriage is associated with all-cause, cancer and cardiovascular mortality, for both sexes - with stronger associations in men for cardiovascular and all-cause mortality. Never-married men against never-married women: 31% higher risk of dying from stroke, 9% higher all-cause mortality.

All three studies only followed and recorded populations, with no randomized groups. Healthy, high-income, socially active people were always more likely to marry, and always more likely to live long. That's the selection effect. Manzoli's paper also found signs of publication bias - studies with good-looking results get published more easily, skewing the pooled result optimistic. So these numbers read as one sentence only: within married populations, the death rate is lower. They cannot be read as "marry and you'll die 12% less." The samples are mostly European and American populations; applied to China they're only an order-of-magnitude reference.

### The time ledger: the gender gap in unpaid labor is an upper-bound reference

The numbers come from the National Bureau of Statistics' third national time-use survey, conducted May 2024. Three terms first. Unpaid labor is the work nobody pays for. "Participants" means people who actually did the activity that day. "Participation rate" means what share of people did it that day. Results: participants in unpaid labor averaged 2 hours 45 minutes a day - men 1 hour 52 minutes, women 3 hours 29 minutes. Participation rate 75.6% - men 67.5%, women 83.9%. Housework alone: averaged over all residents, 1 hour 17 minutes a day. Counting only those who actually did it: 1 hour 59 minutes, 28 minutes less than 2018, participation rate 64.9%. Companionship and care of family alone: averaged over all residents, 30 minutes. Counting only those who actually did it: 1 hour 46 minutes, participation rate 28.4%.

The second survey in 2018: housework averaged over all residents, 1 hour 26 minutes - men 45 minutes, women 2 hours 6 minutes. Participation: men 40.4%, women 75.6%. Companionship and care of children's daily life averaged over all residents: 36 minutes - men 17, women 53. Participation: men 12.3%, women 25.1%.

Both surveys break down only by sex, urban/rural and age - not by married or not. So "how much more housework per day after marriage" has no official number. What can be used is the gap between men and women. The 2024 gender gap in unpaid labor was 1 hour 37 minutes. If the division of housework isn't talked through, that's the most two people can drift apart.

### The money ledger: default rules, written agreements and housework compensation

There's no official statistics on bride price, weddings and marital homes, so this piece writes no numbers. What can be written clearly is the rules set by the Civil Code's Book on Marriage and Family. The Civil Code took effect January 1, 2021.

- Article 1062: from the wedding day to the divorce day, four kinds of things acquired in that span count as community property. One, wages, bonuses and labor remuneration. Two, income from business and investments. Three, income from intellectual property. Four, inherited or gifted property. The fourth has an exception: what a will or gift contract writes as going to one person only is not community property. Both spouses have equal rights to dispose of community property.
- Article 1063: four kinds count as personal property and don't enter the community pot at divorce. One, property held before the marriage. Two, compensation or indemnity received for bodily harm. Three, property a will or gift contract writes as going to one side only. Four, articles for one side's exclusive personal use.
- Article 1065: who owns property before and during marriage can be agreed between the two. It can be agreed separate, all shared, or partly each. The agreement must be in writing. Written down, it legally binds both. No agreement, or an unclear one, follows the two articles above.
- Article 1088: if one side, for raising children, caring for the elderly or assisting the other's work, contributed more than the other, they may claim a compensation from the other at divorce. How much: first the two negotiate; if they can't agree, the court decides.

These rules serve both sides. The higher earner: focus on Articles 1062 and 1065. The one doing more at home: focus on Article 1088. A property agreement only needs to be written. Whether to notarize it is not legally required either way.

### Exit costs: the cooling-off period and litigation conditions

- Article 1076: both willing to divorce - first sign a written divorce agreement, then apply in person at the marriage-registration authority. The agreement must write who raises the children and how property and debts are handled. All of it must be what both negotiated and agreed.
- Article 1077: in the thirty days from when the registration authority receives the application, either side who reconsiders may withdraw it. Those thirty days are the much-discussed cooling-off period. After those thirty days comes another thirty. Within those later thirty days, both must apply in person for the divorce certificate. Not going counts as withdrawing the application.
- Article 1079: one side wants out, the other disagrees - mediation first, or straight to court. At court, mediation comes first too. If the court confirms the affection has truly broken down and mediation fails, it should grant the divorce. These circumstances, with mediation failed, should mean a granted divorce: bigamy or cohabitation with another; domestic violence or abuse and abandonment; gambling, drugs and other vices uncorrected after repeated admonition; two full years of separation over incompatibility. One more: after a first judgment denying divorce, another full year of separation, then refiling - should be granted.

Of the 3.513 million divorces in 2024, 891,000 went through court judgment or mediation. The cooling-off period governs only the registration route. Court litigation doesn't apply it.

### Relationship quality: the ledger more worth counting than "married or not"

Robles et al. (2014) pooled 126 studies and over 72,000 people. Conclusion: the higher the marital quality, the better the health. How tightly two things track each other is measured here by an effect size called r. The closer r is to 0, the less the two things relate; the larger r's absolute value, the tighter they track. A negative sign means one goes high while the other goes low. These associations ran from r 0.07 to 0.21. With mortality risk, r = 0.11. With cardiovascular response during couple conflict, r = -0.13. None of these are large - though associations between health behaviors like eating well and health are the same magnitude. Part of the results carried publication bias - good-looking results get published more easily. Some studies specifically tested for sex differences and basically found none. Most of these studies only followed and recorded, with no randomized groups, so causation can't be inferred. Dhindsa et al. (2020), a cardiovascular review, also points out that marital satisfaction and marital quality have a clear influence on cardiovascular risk.

This piece found no meta-analysis directly comparing "a bad marriage" against "unmarried" on mortality. So "a bad marriage is worse than not marrying" is currently an inference, not a number-backed conclusion. What stands is this: the "married or not" difference is mixed with selection effects. "Marital quality, good or bad" is a separate, independent thing. Looking only at whether someone married, not at how it's going, misses the second ledger whole.

## 3. How to count for yourself: a fill-in checklist

Fill it one ledger at a time. Don't add numbers from different ledgers together. Where you can't fill a cell, write "don't know" - "don't know" is itself useful information.

What the other person provides (fill one by one)

- Money: their income, their debts, their pre-marriage property. Post-marriage property - follow the law's default rules, or write a written agreement. If an agreement, what it writes.
- Time: how much unpaid housework and care the other person does daily now. National averages for comparison, among those who actually did it that day: men 1 hour 52 minutes, women 3 hours 29 minutes. How this work splits after marriage. How it splits with children, or when elders at home need care. Whose work between the two can be put second.
- Relationship quality: how many conflicts in the past six months, and how each one ended. When you were sick, unemployed or down, what they did. And the reverse.
- Emotional value: being with them - do you come away with more energy, or drained. This takes months of living together to see. A checklist of conditions won't show it.

What you'll pay (fill one by one)

- Money: how much marrying itself costs - bride price, wedding, marital home. Where that money would go if you didn't marry.
- Time: estimate the extra daily housework and care. Then the extra weekly time on social obligations.
- Health: your own sleep schedule, eating, exercise - will marriage make them better or worse. This line is closer to you than any meta-analysis number.
- Exit costs: registration divorce means the 30-day cooling-off first, then collecting the certificate within the next 30. The court route means meeting the circumstances written in Article 1079. Can you hold up through that stretch.

How to protect yourself legally (applies to both sides)

- Is there documentation for pre-marriage property - purchase contracts, bank statements, gift contracts, wills. Can it prove "held before the marriage" or "written as one side's only."
- Whether to sign a written property agreement. Does it cover only pre-marriage property, only post-marriage earnings, or both.
- Who does more of the housework and care after marriage. Does the one doing more know Article 1088 lets them claim a compensation. Are the two willing to agree on the compensation method in advance.
- Debts: money borrowed post-marriage in one person's name - which of it counts as joint debt. Does the other side know about it.

The marrying-for-the-elders column, filled separately

- What the elders get: peace of mind, face saved, no more pressure to marry, someone to support them in old age.
- Of those, which are truly the elders' benefit, and which are actually yours.
- All the costs in the three columns above are borne by you. The benefits the elders get don't automatically become your health, and don't automatically become your relationship quality.
- Split out and written this way, whether the ledger weighs heavy or light is for the person filling it to judge.

## 4. Common mistakes

Mixing up the two divorce rates. One is the crude divorce rate: divorces per thousand people, 2.5 per thousand in 2024. The other is the "divorce-to-marriage ratio": the year's divorces divided by the year's marriages - 351.3 / 610.6, about 57.5%. These are two different things. The ratio's numerator and denominator aren't the same cohort. Most of this year's divorcing people married many years ago. When fewer people marry, the ratio jumps on its own. So it doesn't mean "half of marriages end in divorce." The Ministry's bulletin contains no such indicator at all.

Reading "appearing together" as "because of." The married die about 12% less - a result pooled from many studies. But being healthy, high-income and socially active all influence two things at once: whether a person marries, and whether a person lives long. This ledger can be counted, but read it at a discount. Nor can it substitute for judging the specific relationship in front of you.

Treating marriage as a task to complete. As a task, you watch only the state "married or not," not "how it's going." Yet relationship quality's effect on health is a separate ledger independent of married-or-not. Task-style decisions usually miss that ledger whole.

Taking national averages as your own numbers. The gender gap in the time-use survey is a national average. The property rules are the law's default settings, changeable with one written agreement. National numbers are good for giving you a magnitude, a starting point for negotiation. They don't give you a conclusion.

Where there's no official number, don't fill it with folk numbers. Bride price, weddings and the cost of raising children have no official statistics, so this piece leaves them blank. Filling in your own real numbers beats citing any institution's estimate.
